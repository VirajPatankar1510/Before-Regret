What FEMA Pays Homeowners

When a disaster declaration covers a house, its owner can apply to FEMA for help. We added up every homeowner registration FEMA has published, 14,899,124 across 627 major disasters since 2002. Fewer than half, 47.6%, were approved for any aid, and approved owners got $3,619 on average. FEMA’s aid is built to make a home livable again, behind insurance, and the numbers show it.

Key findings

  • FEMA recorded 14,899,124 homeowner registrations for its Individuals and Households Program in 627 major disasters declared from 2002 to 2026; 47.6% were approved for any aid.
  • Approved homeowner registrations received $3,619 on average, counting repair, rental and other-needs aid together. 1.1% received FEMA's maximum grant.
  • Of the 6,314,477 owner-occupied homes FEMA inspected and found damaged, 3.3% had FEMA-recorded damage above $30,000.
  • FEMA approved 35 cents of home repair and replacement aid for every dollar of damage its inspectors recorded to owners' homes and belongings.
  • In wildfire disasters, FEMA-recorded damage averaged $119,786 per damaged home, and 12.7% of homeowner registrations were approved for aid, the lowest share of any major disaster type.

What the program is for

Livable again, and behind insurance.

FEMA’s Individuals and Households Program pays homeowners in three main ways: repair or replacement money for the home, rental help while it can’t be lived in, and “other needs” such as belongings, medical and funeral costs. Two rules in FEMA’s regulations shape every number on this page.

The first is the purpose of repair money. Under 44 CFR 206.117, FEMA may pay to repair a damaged home “to a safe and sanitary living or functioning condition,” and its help “will be limited to repairs of a quality necessary” for that. The goal is a home that works, not the home as it was.

The second is insurance. Under 44 CFR 206.113, when an applicant has insurance, FEMA may help only when the claim is denied, when insurance money is “significantly delayed” and the owner agrees to pay FEMA back from it, when no housing can be had on the private market, or when insurance and all other help are not enough. Section 206.117 adds that repair aid is for damage “not covered by insurance.” The dataset doesn’t say which applicants were insured, so we can’t split the figures that way.

There is also a cap. FEMA adjusts it every year in the Federal Register. For disasters declared on or after October 1, 2026, it is $46,400 for housing assistance and $46,400 for other needs; the year before, $44,800 each.

How much approved owners got

A few thousand dollars, and the cap for about 1 in 100.

Counting repair, rental and other-needs money together, the average approved homeowner registration got $3,619. Only 80,441 registrations, 1.1% of those approved, received FEMA’s maximum grant. Of the $25.7 billion FEMA approved for homeowners in these disasters, $14.1 billion was for repairing or replacing homes, $3.9 billion for rent and $6.3 billion for other needs.

Part of the reason is what FEMA’s inspectors find. Of 7,997,572 owner-occupied homes inspected, 1,683,095 had no damage recorded. Of the 6,314,477 with damage, most had less than $10,000 of it under FEMA’s guidelines, and 3.3% had more than $30,000. That recorded damage, $40.1 billion in total, covers the home and its belongings. Against it, FEMA approved 35 cents of home repair and replacement money for every dollar.

By disaster type

Wildfire stands apart.

Hurricanes account for 75% of all homeowner registrations. Wildfire is the outlier. In wildfire disasters, FEMA-recorded damage averaged $119,786 per damaged home, against $6,344 across all disasters, because a burned house is often a total loss. Yet only 12.7% of wildfire registrations were approved for any aid, and repair money came to 6 cents per dollar of recorded damage. The January 2025 wildfires in Los Angeles County alone drew 185,871 homeowner registrations, and 10% were approved.

The insurance rule above is the likeliest reason, though the data can’t show it directly: a homeowner whose fire loss is covered by insurance is not eligible for FEMA repair money for the same damage.

The largest disasters

Approval rates swing widely, even between big hurricanes.

Hurricane Irma in Florida drew more homeowner registrations than any other disaster in the data, 1,203,058, and 27% were approved. For Hurricane Katrina in Louisiana, FEMA approved 9 cents of repair money for every dollar of damage it recorded, the lowest among these disasters.

What this means if you’re buying

Count on your own coverage first.

FEMA aid only starts with a federal disaster declaration that includes individual assistance for the county, and even then it is designed to come after insurance and to stop at a livable home. A buyer weighing a house in an area with a disaster history has three practical questions to answer before closing:

  • What does the area face? Look up the flood zone and the county’s disaster history. Here is how to check the flood zone for an address, and the lookup above shows past FEMA aid in the county.
  • What would your own policy pay? Ask for the policy wording on the hazards the area has, not only the price. About a quarter of flood insurance claims were on properties rated outside the high-risk flood zone, as our study Outside the Zone found.
  • What would you cover yourself? Deductibles and limits decide how much of a repair lands on you. The average FEMA grant on this page is a useful reality check against that number.

Method

How the figures were built.

The data is FEMA’s OpenFEMA dataset “Individuals and Households Program – Housing Assistance Program Data – Owners” (version 2), pulled in full through the public API on 2026-11-25: 160,963 rows, one for each disaster, state, county, city and ZIP code with homeowner registrations, from DR-1439 on. Each row counts valid registrations, inspections, homes by FEMA-recorded damage, approvals, and approved dollars for repair or replacement, rent and other needs. We joined each disaster number to FEMA’s Disaster Declarations Summaries for its date, incident type and title.

10 disaster numbers in the owners data have no record in the declarations data, so they can’t be dated or typed. Their 262,703 registrations, 1.7% of the total, are left out of every figure.

“Registrations” are FEMA’s count of valid homeowner registrations; a household that applied in two disasters counts twice. “Approved” means approved for any program aid. “Average per approved” divides all approved program dollars by approved registrations. “Repair per $1 of damage” divides approved repair and replacement dollars by FEMA-recorded damage. FEMA also publishes counts of owners by approved amount ($1 to $10,000, and so on); we don’t use them, because in many rows they don’t agree with the dollar totals in the same row.

Limitations

What this does not show.

Recorded damage is FEMA’s, not a contractor’s. FEMA describes the damage field as FEMA Verified Loss for the home and personal property, recorded at the latest inspection under FEMA’s guidelines. It is tied to what FEMA can pay for, so it is not an estimate of the cost to rebuild, and the true gap between loss and aid is wider than these ratios suggest.

No insurance field. The data doesn’t say who was insured, who was paid by insurance, or who got help from other sources, such as disaster loans. A low approval share can reflect insured owners as much as denied ones.

FEMA’s own caveats. FEMA notes that the county is self-reported, that the financial figures come from its case-management system rather than official financial systems and “may differ slightly from official publication,” and that the data is not intended for official federal financial reporting. Dollars are as approved, not as paid, and not adjusted for inflation. Recent disasters may still be open.

Questions this data answers

How much money does FEMA give homeowners after a disaster?

Across 627 major disasters declared from 2002 to 2026, approved homeowner registrations received $3,619 on average from FEMA's Individuals and Households Program, counting home repair, rental and other-needs aid together. 47.6% of the 14,899,124 homeowner registrations were approved for any aid. Dollars are as approved, not adjusted for inflation.

What is the maximum FEMA assistance for a homeowner?

For disasters declared on or after October 1, 2026, FEMA's notice in the Federal Register sets the maximum at $46,400 for housing assistance and $46,400 for other needs; for disasters declared from October 1, 2025 it was $44,800 each. In FEMA's own data, 1.1% of approved homeowner registrations since 2002 received the maximum grant.

Does FEMA pay for home repairs if I have homeowners insurance?

Generally only for what insurance does not cover. FEMA's rules (44 CFR 206.113) let it help an insured homeowner when the claim is denied, when insurance proceeds are significantly delayed and the homeowner agrees to repay FEMA from them, or when insurance and all other help are not enough. Repair aid is limited to making the home safe and sanitary to live in (44 CFR 206.117), not to restoring it as it was.

What share of FEMA applicants get approved?

47.6% of homeowner registrations in disasters declared from 2002 to 2026 were approved for any Individuals and Households Program aid. The share varies by disaster: 27% for Hurricane Irma in Florida, the largest by registrations, and 10% for the January 2025 California wildfires.
