Built to Which Code?

Every new house is built to a code, but not the same one. We matched each of the 913,081 single-family houses permitted in the US in 2025 to the government that issued the permit and the code FEMA says that government has adopted. Nearly half, 424,067 houses or 46.4%, came from places FEMA rates as having an old or weakened code, or none.

Key findings

  • Of 913,081 new single-family houses permitted in the US in 2025, 424,067 (46.4%) were permitted by local governments that FEMA, as of September 30, 2026, rates as having an old or weakened building code, or none.
  • 66,407 new houses, 7.3% of the total, were permitted where FEMA lists no residential code at all, most of them in the unincorporated parts of Texas counties.
  • Texas and North Carolina together account for 35% of the houses permitted under codes FEMA does not rate as current: 82,993 in Texas and 65,303 in North Carolina, where the statewide code is still based on the 2015 International Residential Code.
  • In Harris, Montgomery and Fort Bend counties, Texas, 30,995 new houses were permitted in places where FEMA lists no residential code.
  • Among houses permitted in places FEMA marks as tornado-prone, 28.9% were permitted under codes FEMA rates current for tornadoes, the lowest of the five hazards it tracks.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Building Permits Survey, 2025 annual place files; FEMA Building Code Adoption Tracking, data as of September 30, 2026. Analysis by Before Regret. Puerto Rico is not included.

A code rating is not an inspection

FEMA rates the code a city or county adopted. It does not look at houses, and a house permitted under an older edition can be well built. What the rating tells you is which questions are worth asking about a newer house: which code edition it was permitted under, and whether the inspections were signed off.

What FEMA counts as current

The two newest editions, with nothing weakened.

FEMA’s Building Code Adoption Tracking, BCAT for short, follows the codes adopted by about 30,000 state, local, tribal and territorial governments, and updates them every quarter. Its test is narrow. In FEMA’s words, it considers a community “hazard-resistant” if it has “adopted the latest or second most recent codes without weakening the hazard-resistant provisions through amendments.” Today that means the 2021 or 2024 International Residential Code, and the International Building Code of the same vintage. The map legend in FEMA’s portal labels everything else “Old or Weakened IBC/IRC, or No IBC/IRC Adopted.”

FEMA reports the result two ways: as of its FY2026 fourth-quarter data, 34% of hazard-prone jurisdictions have current codes, and 53% of the population lives in one. Neither number says where new houses are going up. A small town with a current code and a fast-growing county without one count the same in the first measure. This study weights the ratings by the houses each permit office actually approved.

FEMA is careful about what “not current” means, and so are we. Its page says the measure “does not mean communities built to the 2015 codes – for example – are suddenly underprepared,” and that “adoption is just the first step in natural hazard resistance. Enforcement is key.”

By state

Whole states sit on one side of the line or the other.

Where a state sets one mandatory code for everyone, every permit office lands in the same place. In Florida, California, Georgia, South Carolina, Washington, Utah, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Louisiana, Maryland, Maine, New Hampshire, Montana and Connecticut, every new house in our data was permitted under a code FEMA rates current. In North Carolina, Virginia, Indiana, Ohio, Michigan, Idaho, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Oklahoma, Oregon, Kentucky, Massachusetts, Hawaii and Vermont, none was. The split states are the ones where cities and counties choose for themselves, and Texas, which permitted more new houses than any other state, 141,191, is the largest of those: 41% of its new houses came from offices FEMA rates current.

Same county, different codes

The line usually runs between a city and the land around it.

A county is not one building code. Texas

“No residential code” doesn’t always mean no rules.

The top three counties on that list are all around Houston. In Harris, Montgomery and Fort Bend counties, 30,995 new houses were permitted in places where FEMA lists no residential code, nearly all of them in unincorporated areas. That follows from how Texas is set up. FEMA’s note for Texas says the state “has no mandatory statewide building or residential code.” Cities adopt their own; counties mostly can’t.

There is one state rule that can apply, and buyers should know about it. Under Texas Local Government Code § 233.153, new houses in the unincorporated area of a county that has opted in “shall conform to the version of the International Residential Code published as of May 1, 2008, or the version of the International Residential Code that is applicable in the county seat of that county.” It applies only in a county “that has adopted a resolution or order requiring the application” of those rules (§ 233.152). The builder, not the county, hires the inspectors, and § 233.154 requires at least three inspections: the foundation before the concrete is poured, framing and mechanical systems before the drywall goes up, and completion.

So for a new house outside city limits in Texas, the useful questions are whether the county opted in, which code the builder used, and whether the builder can show you the three inspection reports. If the house sits in a city’s extraterritorial jurisdiction and that city has a building code there, the city’s code controls instead (§ 233.153(c)).

North Carolina

A whole state still on the 2015-based code.

North Carolina permitted 65,303 new houses in 2025, and every one of them came from an office working under the state’s residential code based on the 2015 IRC. Wake County alone, Raleigh and the towns around it, accounted for 8,392. FEMA’s note for the state explains the delay: North Carolina adopted the 2021 codes with an original effective date of July 1, 2025, then “enacted S.L. 2025-2, which delays the effective date until 12 months after the SFM certifies that certain statutory and publication and distribution requirements have been met.” Until then, it says, the previous code “remains in effect.”

New edition, still not current

62,249 houses came from offices on the 2021 or 2024 IRC that FEMA still marks down.

An office can adopt the newest residential code and still miss FEMA’s bar, because the rating looks at the building code too, and at amendments that weaken flood, wind, tornado or seismic provisions. Virginia has the most houses in this group, 20,458: its statewide code is based on the 2021 IRC, and FEMA still rates it not current for at least one hazard. Oklahoma City is another case: FEMA lists it on the 2024 IRC but not current, and its note shows the timing problem every reader of this data should keep in mind. The 2024 codes there took effect on 09/14/2026; the 2018 residential code ran until 09/13/2026, so the city’s 2025 houses were permitted under that.

By hazard

Tornado country has the lowest share on current codes.

What this means if you’re buying a newer house

Ask which code it was permitted under.

A house built in the last few years isn’t automatically built to the latest code. The code that applied is the one adopted by whoever issued the permit, on the date it was issued. Three things will tell you more than the age of the house:

  • The permit record. It shows the issuing office and the date, and often the inspections that were passed. Here is how to look up building permits by address.
  • Final sign-off. A permit that was issued but never closed is worth a question before closing. What to do about an open permit explains the usual fixes.
  • Your own inspector. A new-construction inspection can still find problems a code inspection didn’t look for. What a home inspection covers lists what to expect.

Method

How the match was made.

Permit counts are from the U.S. Census Bureau’s Building Permits Survey, 2025 annual place files, which report new privately owned housing units authorized by each of 20,070 permit-issuing offices in the 50 states and DC: cities, towns, townships, and counties for their unincorporated land. We use single-family units, the 1-unit column, as “new houses”. The place files total 1,433,120 units of all kinds, against 1,431,616 in the Census county file for the same year.

Code ratings are from FEMA’s Building Code Adoption Tracking jurisdiction layer, the data behind FEMA’s BCAT portal, pulled on 2026-11-25. Every row carries a data date of September 30, 2026. We use FEMA’s combined hazard rating and its residential code field as they are.

The two datasets share no ID, so each permit office was matched to a FEMA jurisdiction by state, county and name, in a fixed order: same name in the same county; a county’s unincorporated area to FEMA’s row for that county; a name found only once in the state; a FEMA name that starts with the office name. Where FEMA lists jurisdictions in a county but not the office itself, the office takes the county’s rating only if every jurisdiction FEMA lists there has the same rating and code. Houses matched this way cover 99.6% of the total. The rest, 3,393 houses, are mostly permits issued by state agencies directly, and are counted as unmatched rather than guessed. The method used for every office is in the data file.

Puerto Rico’s 0 permit offices are in the Census files and are left out of every figure here.

Limitations

What this does not show.

Timing. The permits are from 2025; the ratings are as of September 30, 2026. A place that adopted a newer code in 2026 is rated on that newer code, though its 2025 houses were permitted under the old one. Codes rarely move backward, so the 46.4% figure is more likely to understate the share permitted under a not-current code than to overstate it.

Adoption is not enforcement. A rating describes the code on a government’s books, not how well it was inspected or how any house was built. Permits are authorizations, not completed houses, and manufactured homes, which follow a separate federal standard, are not in the Census count.

Estimates. The Census Bureau imputes permits for offices that did not report every month; 89.8% of the houses here were directly reported, the rest imputed. A county figure covers every permit office in the county, so it can blend very different codes, and it says nothing about any one house.

Questions this data answers

What percentage of new homes are built to current building codes?

Of 913,081 new single-family houses permitted in the US in 2025, 53.0% were permitted by local governments that FEMA rates as having current hazard-resistant codes, meaning the 2021 or 2024 International Residential and Building Codes with no weakening amendments. 46.4% were permitted under codes FEMA rates as old or weakened, or where no code is adopted. FEMA's ratings are as of September 30, 2026; the permit counts are from the Census Building Permits Survey.

Which states have the most new homes built under old building codes?

By count, Texas (82,993 new houses in 2025) and North Carolina (65,303), followed by Tennessee (31,184). Every permit office in North Carolina works under the state code based on the 2015 IRC; FEMA's note says the 2021 update was adopted but its effective date was delayed by S.L. 2025-2.

Does Texas have a statewide building code for houses?

No. FEMA's tracking says Texas has no mandatory statewide building or residential code. Cities set their own, and Texas Local Government Code section 233.153 lets a county require new houses in its unincorporated area to conform to the International Residential Code published as of May 1, 2008, or the version used in the county seat, if the county adopts a resolution or order to apply it.

How do I find out which building code applies to my address?

Find out which government issues building permits for the address: the city if it is inside city limits, otherwise usually the county. FEMA's Building Code Adoption Tracking portal lists the residential and building code edition each jurisdiction has adopted. For a house already built, the permit record shows when it was permitted, and the code in force on that date is the one that applied.

Where can I download building code adoption data by county?
