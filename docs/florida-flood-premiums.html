Florida flood insurance: what owners pay now, and FEMA’s full-risk price

Every Florida flood policy from FEMA’s National Flood Insurance Program carries two prices: the premium the owner pays and FEMA’s own full-risk premium for the same home. We read all 1,118,513 Florida policies in force on 1 September 2026. For single-family homes, 46.8% are priced below full risk, and by law those premiums can rise by up to 18% a year until they get there.

What these numbers are. Medians and shares over real policies in an area, from FEMA’s own records, as of 1 September 2026. They are not a quote for any house: a premium depends on that home’s elevation, foundation, replacement cost and coverage. The number of years to full risk is a minimum, because a yearly increase can be smaller than 18%, and FEMA’s full-risk figure can itself change.

Key findings

  • Of 881,812 single-family National Flood Insurance Program policies in force in Florida on 1 September 2026, 46.8% were priced below FEMA’s own full-risk premium for the same policy.
  • For those Florida policies below full risk, the median gap was $864 a year, and at the 18% yearly legal maximum the median policy needs at least 4 years of increases to reach full risk.
  • In Collier County, Florida, 80.8% of 40,999 single-family NFIP policies were below FEMA’s full-risk premium, the highest share of any Florida county with at least 2,000 policies.
  • In ZIP code 32931 in Brevard County, Florida, the median single-family NFIP premium was $1,039 against a median FEMA full-risk premium of $6,485, and 77.4% of policies there need ten or more years of 18% increases to reach full risk.
  • Elevated single-family homes in Florida had the largest share below full risk of any foundation type: 69.8% of 51,209 policies, with a median full-risk premium of $3,729 against $1,045 paid.

Source: FEMA OpenFEMA NFIP Policies (v3), every Florida policy in force on 1 September 2026; analysis by Before Regret. Medians over policies in an area, not the price of any one house.

Two prices on every flood policy

FEMA records the premium charged and the premium the risk calls for.

FEMA’s NFIP policy records, published through OpenFEMA, include a field for the premium on each policy and a separate field for its “full risk premium”, which FEMA defines as the premium “based on its determined flood risk and the full cost to pay anticipated losses and expenses”. Since FEMA’s current pricing method reached all renewing policies in April 2022, a policy whose premium is below that figure moves toward it each year. FEMA’s page on single-family pricing says such a premium “will increase towards the full rate”, and that “annual increases will eventually stop once the full-risk rate is realized.”

The speed is set in federal law. Under 42 U.S.C. § 4015(e), the premium rate for a property “may not be increased by more than 18 percent each year”, with exceptions the statute lists, such as a policyholder raising coverage or lowering the deductible. A policy priced at half its full-risk premium therefore needs at least five years of maximum increases to get there.

The total a Florida owner pays each year is more than the premium. FEMA’s “policy cost” adds the reserve fund assessment, the federal policy fee and the HFIAA surcharge. Across 881,812 single-family policies the median policy cost was $1,044 against a median premium of $795.

Where the gap is widest

Southwest Florida and the Keys have the most policies still climbing.

In Collier County, 80.8% of single-family policies were below FEMA’s full-risk premium on 1 September 2026, the highest share of the Florida counties with at least 2,000 policies. Monroe and Lee follow. In Miami-Dade and Broward, the two counties with the most policies, the median gap for policies below full risk is far smaller.

In a few ZIP codes, today’s price is a small fraction of the full-risk premium.

ZIP code 32931, on the Brevard County barrier island, has the largest share of policies a decade or more from full risk among Florida ZIPs with at least 200 single-family policies: the median premium there was $1,039 and the median full-risk premium $6,485. Brevard as a whole looks ordinary next to that, with 43.3% of policies below full risk, which is why a county figure can mislead a buyer looking at one neighborhood. Elevated homes, build era and flood zone

Elevated homes sit furthest below full risk.

Among Florida single-family homes with flood policies, the 51,209 on elevated foundations had the largest share below full risk, 69.8%, and 28.9% of them need ten or more years of maximum increases. The policy records do not say why. They do say that raising a house does not, on these figures, mean its flood premium has finished rising, which matters to a buyer comparing an elevated home with one on a slab.

If you are buying a house in Florida

Budget for the full-risk premium, and ask about the seller’s policy.

  • Ask the seller for the current flood policy’s declarations page, so you see what the house costs to insure today, and ask an insurance agent to quote the same house as a new policy. The difference is your own version of the gap on this page.
  • Ask whether the policy can be assigned to you. FEMA’s pricing page says policyholders “are able to transfer their discount to a new owner by assigning their flood insurance policy when their property changes ownership.” Ask the agent which discounts on that policy would carry over.
  • Plan your budget on the full-risk premium for the ZIP, not today’s price, if the lookup shows most policies there below full risk.
  • Check the flood zone yourself: our guide to finding the flood zone for an address shows how, and what the zone letters mean explains A, V and X.

How we checked this

On 27 November 2026 we read every Florida record in FEMA’s OpenFEMA NFIP Policies dataset (version 3) that was in force on 1 September 2026: 1,118,513 policies, every one unique, matching the count FEMA’s API reports for the same filter. We kept single-family homes (FEMA occupancy codes 1 and 11) insuring one unit: 899,837 policies. We then left out 18,025 with no full-risk premium recorded, leaving 881,812. Each figure compares the premium field with the full-risk premium field; both exclude fees. Years to full risk are the fewest years of 18% increases that close the gap, with the full-risk premium held where it is.

One check decided whether the comparison means anything. If FEMA’s full-risk figure left out the discount a community earns through the Community Rating System, every discounted community would look below full risk, and more so the better its class, since a better class earns a bigger discount. That is not what the records show. In every CRS class with at least 1,000 policies, the median policy’s full-risk premium is within about 5% of what it pays: exactly equal in all but class 3, where it is 1.051 times the premium, and exactly equal in class 2, the best-rated class with policies in Florida. No ZIP or county figure is published from fewer than 30 policies; 181 Florida ZIPs fall under that and are left out.

What this cannot tell you: the premium for a particular house, why one group sits further below full risk than another, or how FEMA’s full-risk figures will move. Field meanings are FEMA’s own, from its OpenFEMA data dictionary.
