County-level data: hazard risk explains 20% of US home insurance premiums, state explains 44%

Dear Chad,

I've completed a national analysis that may suit the Insurance Journal national desk.

It pairs Census ACS table B25141 — what mortgaged households report actually paying for homeowners insurance — with FEMA's National Risk Index expected annual building loss, across all 3,093 US counties and 50.7 million mortgaged households. That covers 99.99% of every mortgaged owner-occupied home in the country.

Restricted to perils a standard HO-3 actually responds to, modelled hazard risk explains 20% of the county-level variation in premium. State of residence alone explains 44%. Counties sitting in the same risk band differ in price by a median of 2.3 times.

Two caveats I would want stated in any coverage. The ACS file spans 2019 to 2023 and centres near 2021, so these figures are the baseline the 2023-25 increases departed from, not current pricing. And the analysis is descriptive: it does not identify whether the state effect is rate regulation, reinsurance cost, litigation environment or market concentration.

SUBJECT:
In 11 states, counties with more modelled hazard pay lower homeowners premiums
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dear Susanne,

I've completed a county-level analysis that may suit Carrier Management's more technical readership.

The setup: Census ACS table B25141, reported homeowners insurance costs for mortgaged households, paired with per-peril expected annual building loss from FEMA's National Risk Index, across 3,093 counties and 50.7 million households.

Regressing log premium on log hazard risk, with risk restricted to the perils a standard HO-3 responds to, gives R-squared = 0.196. State fixed effects alone give 0.436. Adding risk on top of state moves the fit by under a percentage point.

The result I find hardest to explain is within-state. Inside a single regulatory regime, risk ought to sort price cleanly. The median within-state correlation across the 43 states with at least 15 counties is only +0.218, and in 11 states it is negative: Kansas -0.42, South Dakota -0.32, Oklahoma -0.24, Arkansas -0.23, North Dakota -0.22, Georgia -0.17, Missouri -0.15, Nebraska -0.07, Alaska -0.04, Pennsylvania -0.01, Ohio -0.00.

I want to be careful about what this does and does not show. It is descriptive. It does not separate rate regulation from reinsurance cost, residual market structure or litigation environment. The NRI models natural hazards only, so it says nothing about fire, water, theft or liability, which are the bulk of ordinary claims. And the ACS file centres near 2021.

If the within-state inversion is already understood in the actuarial literature, I would genuinely rather be told that than publish it as novel.

SUBJECT:
Three quarters of modelled US building loss falls outside the standard homeowners policy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dear Andrea,

I've published a county-level analysis of homeowners insurance, and one finding may be more useful to your readers than the pricing result it started as.

Using FEMA's National Risk Index and separating perils by whether a standard HO-3 responds to them, 76.5% of the expected annual building loss to US homes comes from perils the policy excludes. Inland flooding alone accounts for 59.2% of it; earthquake a further 16.1%.

The exposure concentrates in older housing. In the 158 counties FEMA scores at or above 95 for earthquake risk, 22,030,850 homes — 57% of the housing stock there — were built before 1980, predating the seismic provisions introduced in the 1976 Uniform Building Code.

For context on the pricing side: across 3,093 counties, hazard risk explains 20% of the variation in what households report paying, while state of residence alone explains 44%.

Sources are ACS 5-year 2023, centred near 2021, and the current NRI county table. The analysis is descriptive and does not attempt to explain the state effect.
