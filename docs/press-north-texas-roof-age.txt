THE SENTENCE THAT WILL GET MISQUOTED, AND THE ONE WE DELIBERATELY DID NOT WRITE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

"Half the roofs in Collin County are about to fail."

  The study does not say that and you should not let it print. Decade of
  construction is not roof age: a house built in 2005 may have been re-roofed
  twice since, and hail claims are one of the commonest reasons it would have
  been. What the concentration figure establishes is when the housing - and so
  the FIRST roof on it - arrived. It sets the clock. It does not read it.

"New houses in Collin and Denton cost the most in America to insure."

  This is the obvious version of the story and we cut it on purpose, so if a
  reporter reaches for it, here is why they should not. Collin and Denton do
  sit near the top for the share of households paying over $3,000 a year. But
  the Census reports premiums in absolute dollar BANDS, so a county of
  expensive houses puts more households over $3,000 whatever its weather does.
  Collin and Denton are affluent DFW suburbs. Hidalgo County has similarly new
  housing, is also in Texas, also gets hail, and has only 6.0% of households in
  the top band - because houses there cost less.

  Separating price from wealth needs median home values, which are not in this
  dataset, so the study makes no claim about what anyone pays. Saying that out
  loud is what makes the rest of the numbers trustworthy.

SUBJECT:
Half of Collin County's housing went up in one 20-year window - and it's in a hail corridor

Dear [Name],

I have been running a county-level analysis of US housing age against NOAA's
storm record, and the North Texas result is the one I did not expect.

50.9% of Collin County's 421,938 homes were built in the 2000s or 2010s.
Denton is 50.7%. Those are the sixth and seventh most concentrated housing
stocks among the 100 most populous US counties - most places built up
gradually, and these two did not.

That would be a curiosity on its own. What makes it a story is where it
happened. Denton recorded 355 hailstorms between 2015 and 2024 and Collin 283,
third and fifth of the hundred counties. Across Tarrant, Denton, Collin and
Dallas the ten-year total is 1,240.

Roofs go on when houses go up. In a county built gradually, roof replacement
is a rolling cost spread across every year. In a county built in one burst, it
is a cohort - and roof age is one of the few things a home insurer asks about a
specific property, with carriers moving to actual cash value, raising the
wind-and-hail deductible, or declining once a roof passes a certain age.

Screening all 100 counties for both conditions - at least 45% of housing in one
twenty-year window and at least 100 hailstorms - leaves three counties. Two of
them share a border, and they are Collin and Denton.

PITCH 2 - WEATHER OR CLIMATE DESK
The angle: Tarrant has more hail and does not have this problem
==========================================================================

SUBJECT:
Tarrant gets more hail than Denton. Denton has the harder roof problem. Here's why

Dear [Name],

A counterintuitive result from a county-level read of NOAA's Storm Events
database that I think is worth a look.

Tarrant County recorded 416 hailstorms between 2015 and 2024 - second among the
100 most populous US counties, and more than either Denton (355) or Collin
(283). On hail alone Tarrant is the story.

But hail frequency is only half of what determines whether a county faces a
synchronised roof-replacement problem. The other half is when the housing was
built. Tarrant's stock is spread out: 32.7% in its busiest twenty years. Collin
and Denton are at 50.9% and 50.7%. Tarrant has the hail without the cohort -
its roofs were not all installed at once, so they do not expire at once.

Run the screen across all 100 counties and only three have both concentrated
construction and heavy hail. Fort Bend County is more concentrated than either
North Texas county and recorded 33 hailstorms. Clark County, Nevada is built
the same way and recorded 28. Synchronised construction is common;
synchronised construction under hail is not.

One methodological note rather than letting you find it: I use raw hail counts,
not hail per square mile. Across these counties event density correlates with
population density at Spearman +0.681, because NOAA logs reports and a storm
nobody witnesses is often never written down.

IF A REPORTER COMES BACK
==========================================================================

"How many roofs are actually past their service life?"
  Unknown, and the study says so. The ACS records when a structure was built,
  not when its roof was last replaced. Anyone publishing a count of failing
  roofs in Collin County is estimating. Do not become their source for it.

"Isn't fifteen to thirty years a very wide range for a roof?"
  Yes, and that is the honest range - it depends on product, installation,
  ventilation and what the weather has done. The study quotes the range rather
  than a single number for that reason. If a reporter wants a firmer figure,
  send them to a roofing trade body or a carrier's own underwriting guidance
  rather than letting them attribute a specific number to us.

"Are you selling something?"
  Yes, and say so first: BeforeRegret sells a property report to home buyers.
  The study is free, the data is public domain, and the analysis does not
  depend on the product.

"Can I have Wake County, or my own county?"
  The CSV covers all 100. Wake, North Carolina is the third county that clears
  the screen - 45.0% concentration, 122 hailstorms - and that is a legitimate
  separate story for a Raleigh newsroom.
