--------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE TWO SENTENCES THAT WILL GET MISQUOTED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

"Pittsburgh has the cheapest home insurance in America."

  It does not say that and you should correct it before it prints. The ACS
  reports what households say they pay, in bands, with no published mean, so
  no county in this study has an average premium and none is calculated. What
  the data supports is a share: 58.8% report paying under $1,000, which is
  7th highest among the 100 counties examined. It also covers mortgaged
  households only - owners without a mortgage face no lender requirement and
  are not in the table at all.

"Allegheny is the third stormiest county in America."

  Third of the 100 counties in this study, which are large-population
  counties, not third of all 3,143. And NOAA's database records REPORTS of
  severe weather. A storm that damages nothing in an empty place often goes
  unrecorded, so the counts describe how often something damaging was written
  down here, not a physical storm frequency.

Volunteering both corrections in your first email is the fastest way to sound
like you know the dataset rather than like you are selling something.

PITCH 1 - HOUSING, REAL ESTATE OR CONSUMER DESK
The angle: what a cheap premium is actually buying
==========================================================================

SUBJECT:
Allegheny County's cheap home insurance isn't a bargain - it's a coverage gap

Dear [Name],

I have finished a county-level analysis crossing NOAA's storm records against
what Census respondents say they pay to insure their homes, and Allegheny
County came out as the sharpest outlier in the set.

Between 2015 and 2024 NOAA recorded 1,108 severe weather events in the county
- the third-highest of the 100 large counties I examined, against a median of
285. Yet 58.8% of Allegheny's mortgaged households report paying under $1,000
a year for homeowners insurance, and only 2.9% pay over $3,000. Oklahoma
County recorded fewer events over the same decade and eight times as many
households pay in the top band.

The explanation is not that insurers have mispriced Pittsburgh. It is what
the county's weather consists of. Allegheny's two most common event types are
thunderstorm wind (528) and flooding (330 floods and flash floods, 4th of the
100). A standard HO-3 policy covers the wind. It does not cover the flood -
that is a separate NFIP or private policy - so the premium is low partly
because the policy is not carrying the county's second-largest exposure.

There is a second uncovered risk in the same place. The EPA puts Allegheny in
radon Zone 1, its highest category, and 77.2% of the county's 604,558 housing
units were built before 1980, which is before radon-resistant construction
entered model codes. No homeowners policy covers radon mitigation either.

PITCH 2 - WEATHER, ENVIRONMENT OR SCIENCE DESK
The angle: the county's weather is not what people think it is
==========================================================================

SUBJECT:
Allegheny County logged 528 thunderstorm wind events in a decade - 2nd of 100

Dear [Name],

Pittsburgh does not have a reputation as a severe weather county, and the
NOAA record disagrees fairly strongly.

Over 2015-2024, Allegheny County logged 1,108 severe weather events. Broken
down: 528 thunderstorm wind events, which is second among the 100 large
counties I examined; 279 flash floods and 51 floods, fourth; 115 hailstorms;
12 tornadoes. The median county in the study recorded 285 events in total.

The composition is the interesting part. Allegheny's profile is wind and
water, not the hail and tornado activity that dominates the southern plains -
which is exactly why it does not register culturally as a storm county
despite the counts.

I should flag a methodological point rather than let you find it: I do not
normalise by area, and the reason is measurable. Across these 100 counties,
events per square mile correlates with population density at a Spearman
coefficient of +0.681. NOAA logs reports, and an unwitnessed storm is often
not logged, so density largely maps where people live. Raw counts relate only
weakly to land area (+0.156) and population (+0.113), so raw counts are what
the study uses.

SUBJECT:
Storm frequency and home insurance price are barely related - here's the county file

Dear [Name],

Across the 100 most populous US counties, how often severe weather is
recorded in a county tells you almost nothing about what its residents pay to
insure their homes. The rank correlation between recorded storm events and
the share of mortgaged households paying over $3,000 a year is -0.101.
Effectively nothing, and slightly negative.

Allegheny County is the cleanest illustration: third-highest storm count of
the hundred, sixth-lowest share paying in the top premium band.

I think the honest read is not that insurers are getting it wrong. It is that
the perils driving the count in places like Allegheny - inland flooding above
all - are written outside the homeowners policy, so the premium and the
weather are measuring different things. A national analysis I published in
August found roughly three quarters of FEMA's modelled building loss falls on
perils a standard HO-3 excludes, which is the same finding at national scale.

IF A REPORTER COMES BACK
==========================================================================

"Can you give me an average premium for Allegheny County?"
  No, and neither can anyone else from this source. ACS B25141 publishes
  banded counts, not a mean. Anyone quoting a county average homeowners
  premium is using modelled or quoted rates from a private vendor, which is a
  different measure from what households report paying. Say so - it is the
  answer that makes the rest of your numbers trustworthy.

"Are you selling something?"
  Yes, and say so plainly: BeforeRegret sells a property report to home
  buyers. The study is free, the data is public domain and free to reuse, and
  the analysis does not depend on the product. Volunteering this is better
  than having it discovered.

"Does this mean people in Pittsburgh are underinsured?"
  The data cannot say that. It shows what households report paying and what
  the county's recorded hazards are. Whether an individual household is
  underinsured depends on their policy, their deductible, whether they carry
  flood cover separately, and their property - none of which is in this file.
  The defensible statement is narrower: the county's two largest exposures
  are both outside a standard policy.

"Can I have the data for my county?"
  The CSV covers all 100. If their county is not among them, say so rather
  than estimating it.
