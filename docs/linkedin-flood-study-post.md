## Post body

The state with the worst flood risk in America has the least flood insurance.

Not "one of the least." Fiftieth out of fifty.

West Virginia carries the highest expected annual flood loss of any state — $2.71 per $1,000 of building value. Its take-up rate is 10.3%: 6,324 NFIP policies against 61,606 homes inside a mapped flood zone.

I pulled FEMA's county-level data on flood-zone homes and NFIP policies in force, across 2,304 counties and 3.75 million flood-zone homes, to see whether coverage tracks danger anywhere.

It doesn't.

Regressing county take-up on county flood risk gives an R² of 0.004. State of residence alone gives 0.225 — more than fifty times as much. Where you live predicts whether you're covered. How much flooding you face does not.

What surprised me most:

→ In the typical county, one home in seven inside a flood zone carries a policy. Median take-up is 15%.

→ In 705 counties, fewer than one in ten. In 68 counties, no policy is in force at all.

→ The national number looks far healthier — 39.8% — but Florida alone accounts for 566,635 of the 1.5 million policies. The aggregate is carried by a handful of mature coastal markets.

→ South Carolina has the highest take-up in the country at 68.7%. It ranks 43rd of 50 on risk.

Two limits worth stating plainly. This counts NFIP only, and private flood cover has grown, so true coverage is somewhat higher than these figures by an amount this data cannot measure. And take-up is not purely a free choice — federally backed mortgages require flood insurance inside a mapped zone, so part of what varies across states is mortgage mix and enforcement, not only what buyers decide.

A standard homeowners policy does not cover flood. That is the design, not a loophole. It is just that across most of the country, almost nobody bought the policy that does.
