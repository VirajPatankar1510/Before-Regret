Each pitch below is a different story, not the same story with a different
name at the top. Pick the one that matches the desk you are writing to and

WHAT TO FILL IN: only the name. There are no verified addresses in this file

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE THREE NUMBERS, AND THE ONE THAT GETS MISQUOTED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  17,049   dams classified high hazard potential (of 92,766 in the inventory)
   2,791   of those rated Poor or Unsatisfactory              16.4%
   2,476   of those with no emergency action plan on file     14.5%
     636   in BOTH groups

636 is the intersection and it is the number to lead with. The version that
gets written by mistake is "over 2,000 dams in poor condition with no
emergency plan" - that is 2,476 and 2,791 run together and describes neither
group. If it comes back to you in a draft, correct it before it prints. A
correction you volunteer buys more credibility than the story costs.

"High hazard potential" is a statement about consequences, not condition. It
means failure is expected to cause loss of life. Reporters conflate it with
"dangerous dam" constantly, and getting that right in your first email is the
fastest way to sound like you know the dataset.

PITCH 1 - NATIONAL INFRASTRUCTURE OR PUBLIC-SAFETY DESK
The angle: what the inventory does not know
==========================================================================

SUBJECT:
636 US dams are rated poor or unsatisfactory and have no emergency action plan

Dear [Name],

I have finished a county-level read of the Army Corps' National Inventory of
Dams, and the finding I would lead with is not the one I expected.

Of 17,049 dams classified high hazard potential - meaning failure is expected
to cost lives - 2,791 are rated Poor or Unsatisfactory and 2,476 have no
emergency action plan on file. 636 are in both groups. That overlap is the
story: condition and planning are recorded as separate fields, so a dam can
be structurally sound with no plan, or have a plan and be failing, and only
636 are both at once.

The number underneath it is arguably worse. A further 3,784 high-hazard dams
- 22.2% - carry no condition rating at all. Not a bad rating: no rating. Not
rated, not available, or blank. For more than one high-hazard dam in five,
the public record does not say what condition it is in.

Two things that cut against the alarming version, and I would want both in
any coverage. 77.4% of high-hazard dams do have an emergency action plan. And
the median high-hazard dam has a last inspection date of 2023 - among those
rated Poor or Unsatisfactory, the median assessment date is also 2023. These
records exist because an active inspection regime produced them. The story is
what the file shows, not that nobody is looking.

PITCH 2 - NORTH CAROLINA STATE DESK
The angle: NC leads the country on this measure
==========================================================================

SUBJECT:
North Carolina has more dams in poor condition without an emergency plan than any other state

Dear [Name],

North Carolina tops a national list I would rather no state topped, and the
county detail is available.

In a county-level read of the Army Corps' National Inventory of Dams, North
Carolina has 120 dams that are both rated Poor or Unsatisfactory and have no
emergency action plan on file - the highest count of any state. New Mexico is
second with 80, Texas third with 70.

The wider state picture: 1,678 North Carolina dams carry a high hazard
potential classification, 247 of those are rated Poor or Unsatisfactory, and
635 have no emergency action plan recorded. Wake County alone accounts for 11
of the overlap group, out of 208 high-hazard dams; Macon County has 7 out of
33, which is a far higher share of a much smaller inventory.

Worth saying plainly: high hazard potential describes consequences, not
condition. It means a failure is expected to cause loss of life given what is
downstream. North Carolina's large count partly reflects that it simply has a
lot of dams in the inventory.

PITCH 3 - NEW MEXICO / EL PASO-LAS CRUCES REGIONAL DESK
The angle: concentration, not volume
==========================================================================

SUBJECT:
30 of Doña Ana County's 41 high-hazard dams are rated poor with no emergency plan

Dear [Name],

Most state-level dam stories are about big states with big inventories. New
Mexico is the opposite, and that is what makes it a story.

New Mexico has 255 dams classified high hazard potential - a small inventory
by national standards. But 80 of them are both rated Poor or Unsatisfactory
and have no emergency action plan on file. That is second in the country
behind North Carolina, which has six and a half times as many high-hazard
dams.

The concentration is sharper still at county level. Doña Ana County has 41
high-hazard dams, and 30 of them are in that overlap group - roughly three in
four. Grant County has 8 of 15. I have not found another county in the
national file with a comparable share.

The honest caveats: high hazard potential is a statement about what is
downstream, not about condition, and the inventory does not publish the
reasoning behind any condition assessment. Nationally the median high-hazard
dam was last inspected in 2023, so these ratings come from a regime that is
active rather than absent.

PITCH 4 - WASHINGTON DC METRO DESK
The angle: it is next door
==========================================================================

SUBJECT:
Half of Prince George's County's high-hazard dams are rated poor with no emergency plan

Dear [Name],

A national dataset with a very local number in it, on your side of the river.

Prince George's County has 23 dams classified high hazard potential, meaning
failure is expected to cause loss of life. 12 of them - just over half - are
both rated Poor or Unsatisfactory and have no emergency action plan on file.
Maryland's statewide figure is 26, so nearly half the state's total sits in
one county in the DC suburbs.

For scale: 636 dams nationally are in that overlap group, spread across 37
states and 2,274 counties that have any high-hazard dam at all. Twelve in a
single suburban county is a concentration worth a phone call to the Maryland
Dam Safety programme.

What I would not write without checking: an emergency action plan is a
procedural document - notification order and usually inundation mapping - and
"none on file" is a record-keeping fact, not proof no planning exists.
Condition and plan status are separate fields in the inventory, which is
exactly why the overlap is worth reporting rather than assuming.

IF SOMEONE REPLIES
==========================================================================

Answer the same day. The single most useful thing you can offer is the county
rows for their patch, and it takes two minutes - filter the CSV by state and
send it as an attachment, not a link.

If they ask whether a specific dam is dangerous, do not answer it. The
inventory records a condition rating and a plan status; it does not assess
risk of failure, and the study says so. Point them at the state dam safety
office, which is the body that actually holds that judgement. Declining that
question is what makes the rest of your numbers trustworthy.
