# Thanksgiving Is the Peak Day for Home Cooking Fires. Here's What to Check.

The National Fire Protection Association puts it plainly: Thanksgiving is the peak day of the year for home cooking fires, at more than three times the daily average. In 2024, US fire departments were called to an estimated 1,139 home cooking fires on Thanksgiving Day alone, 321 percent of an ordinary day. Christmas Day and Christmas Eve came second and third.

The cooking advice is well known: stay in the kitchen. The checks below are about the house itself, and you can do them a few days ahead, before the kitchen fills up.

## The smoke alarm nearest the kitchen

NFPA's placement advice is that smoke alarms should be at least 10 feet (3 meters) from the stove. Inside that distance, ordinary cooking sets them off.

A national in-home study, carried out for the Consumer Product Safety Commission and published in September 2024, found that half the alarms it checked were closer than that. It looked at the 703 smoke alarms nearest the kitchen, whether in the kitchen itself, a dining room, a living room or a hallway:
- 53% were within 10 feet of the stovetop;
- 13% were less than 6 feet away;
- the 46% at a proper distance were usually 11-20 feet away.

Distance matters for a reason beyond noise. In the same study, 42% of households had a smoke alarm go off in the past year when there was no fire, and 77% of those said cooking set it off. Among households that knew not all their alarms worked, 27% had disabled them on purpose, by pulling the battery or disconnecting the unit. A separate 2026 survey of 2,008 adults for UL Standards & Engagement found 17% of holiday cooks admit to removing or disabling a smoke alarm over frequent false alarms.

An alarm that goes off every time the oven door opens is the one most likely to be found with its battery out on the day it's needed. If yours sits close to the kitchen, you have two fixes:
- **Move it** to 10 feet or more from the stove, if the room allows.
- **Change the type.** NFPA names photoelectric alarms as the best type near kitchens. Newer multi-criteria alarms carry the words "helps reduce cooking nuisance alarms" on the packaging.

A hardwired alarm is a job for an electrician, according to NFPA, so moving one is not a Wednesday-night project.

## How old is it?

Take the alarm down and read the back. NFPA's rule is that every smoke alarm is replaced 10 years after its manufacturing date, whatever the test button says.

People are not good at guessing this. In the 2024 study, only 51% of households were right about the age of all their tested alarms. Where a date could be read, the alarms ran from 1988 to 2023, and the average was 2015 across 528 devices. An alarm made in 2015 is already past its 10 years.

The same study pressed the test button on 1,314 devices:
- 9% didn't work even after a battery fix was tried;
- another 13% worked only after the battery was dealt with.

Pressing the button before Thursday is the cheapest check on this list. If you are hosting at someone else's house, ask whether they have checked theirs. Our [fall maintenance checklist](/guides/fall-home-maintenance-checklist-new-homeowners/) covers the rest of the alarm routine for the year.

## What the kitchen circuits can take

Thanksgiving is the one day a year many kitchens run a roaster, a slow cooker, a stand mixer and a coffee maker at once. The International Residential Code (IRC E3703.2, drawn from the National Electrical Code's 210.11(C)(1)) requires a minimum of two 20-ampere circuits for the kitchen and dining receptacles. That is a rule for new work; it doesn't change a kitchen wired decades ago.

Before the day, open the panel and look at the breaker labels. Find out which counters share a circuit, and which are on a circuit of their own. Then spread the heating appliances across them.

A breaker that trips mid-roast is the circuit protecting itself, not a fault to work around. The fix is to move an appliance to another circuit, not to run an extension cord to a different room. The [power strip and extension cord guide](/guides/daisy-chaining-power-strips-holiday-decorations/) explains why plugging one into another doesn't add any capacity.

## If something does catch fire

NFPA's own steps, in order:
- **On the stovetop:** "Smother the fire by sliding the lid over the pan and turn off the stovetop." Leave the lid on until the pan has cooled completely, and never throw water on a grease fire.
- **In the oven:** turn off the heat and keep the door closed.
- **If it doesn't go out, or you aren't sure:** get everyone out, close the door behind you, and call 9-1-1 once you're outside.

That first step only works if a lid is within reach, so set one beside the stove before you start. Ranges and cooktops account for 51% of home cooking fires, according to NFPA's averages for 2020 to 2024, and unattended equipment is a factor in 25% of them.

## The fryer question

NFPA's position on oil turkey fryers is blunt: "Turkey fryers that use cooking oil are not safe." Its suggestion, if fried turkey is the tradition, is to buy one ready-cooked or use a fryer that doesn't use oil.
