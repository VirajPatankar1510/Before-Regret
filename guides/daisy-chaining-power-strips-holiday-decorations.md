# Nearly Half of Holiday Decorators Admit to Daisy-Chaining Power Strips or Cords. Here's Why It Matters.

The number comes from UL Standards & Engagement's 2026 holiday survey of 2,008 US adults. Among people who decorate, 46% admit to daisy-chaining: plugging a power strip into another strip, or into an extension cord, to reach the tree, the mantel or the porch. The same survey found 62% of Americans plan to decorate this season.

Most of those chains never cause trouble, which is partly why the habit survives. The reason it matters is less about any one evening and more about two facts that don't change with the season: what a power strip is tested to do, and how much one circuit can carry.

## What a power strip is listed for

UL, whose standard power strips are tested to, files them as "relocatable power taps". Its published guidance on them is direct:

> "Relocatable power taps are intended to be directly connected to a permanently installed branch circuit receptacle. Relocatable power taps are not intended to be series connected (daisy chained) to other relocatable power taps or to extension cords."

The same guidance says they are not a substitute for fixed wiring, and their cords are not meant to pass through walls, windows, ceilings or floors. That second line catches a lot of December setups. A strip's cord shut in a window frame so the porch lights can reach an indoor outlet is outside what the strip was tested for, before anything is even chained to it.

## A chain adds outlets, not capacity

Every outlet in a house sits on a branch circuit with its own breaker. The International Residential Code's summary table for branch circuits (Table E3702.14, which mirrors NEC Table 210.24) puts the maximum load at 15 amps on a 15-amp circuit and 20 amps on a 20-amp circuit. Multiply by 120 volts and that is 1,800 watts or 2,400 watts for everything plugged into that circuit, in every room it serves.

A second strip doesn't change that ceiling. It adds more sockets, all of them feeding back through the first strip's cord and plug, and all of them landing on the same circuit. That first cord now carries the tree, the village, the inflatable and whatever else joined the chain, which is the use UL's listing leaves out.

So the fix is about where things plug in, not about buying a longer chain:
- **One strip, one wall outlet.** If the room needs more sockets than that, use another wall outlet.
- **Split the heavy things.** Look at the breaker labels in the panel and put the big displays on outlets that aren't on the same circuit.
- **Outdoor lights go in an outdoor outlet.** 83% of decorators in the same survey said they make sure outdoor decorations are plugged into weatherproof outlets.

## The tag on the light string

Light strings carry their own limit, separate from the circuit's. UL certifies decorative holiday lighting to UL 588. For strings meant to stay up all year, a supplement added in 2017 requires a marking that reads:

> "CAUTION: This string light is rated ___Watts; do not overload. Connect other string lights end-to-end up to a maximum of ___ Watts total."

If a set has a tag like that, the filled-in blanks are the ceiling for sets joined end to end, and they tell you more than any rule of thumb.

There is also a time limit people rarely hear about. The National Electrical Code (Article 590) permits decorative holiday lighting strings to be used for up to 90 days. Holiday sets are temporary wiring by design. Lights you mean to leave on the porch rail all year should be ones listed for that use.

## What the fire records show

NFPA counts an average of 835 home fires a year that start with decorations other than Christmas trees, causing 3 deaths, 29 injuries and $18 million in damage. Christmas trees are counted separately: an average of 143 home fires a year between 2020 and 2024, and electrical distribution or lighting equipment was involved in two in five (40%) of them.

None of those figures isolates daisy-chained strips as a cause, and we haven't seen a national dataset that does. What the survey tells you is how common the habit is; what UL's listing tells you is that it falls outside how the product was tested.

## If you're looking at houses this season

Decorations show you where a house runs short of outlets. A cord through a window to light the porch suggests there's no outlet out there. A strip behind the sofa feeding another strip says the same about the living room. Neither is a defect, but both are worth a note on the walkthrough, and they're the kind of thing that's easy to miss once the lights come down.
