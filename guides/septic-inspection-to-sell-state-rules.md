# Do You Need a Septic Inspection to Sell? State Rules

The EPA says that "in many states, a septic system must be inspected with the transfer of real estate", but it doesn't say which states. Search for a list and you'll find posts that disagree with each other about the same states. So we read the laws themselves. Here's what each one actually requires, who has to arrange it, and the deadlines that catch people out.

## The rules, state by state

We read these on 10 November 2026. This is not a list of all 50 states. It covers the states we could read at the source. A state that isn't here may still have a rule, and counties or towns often add their own.

| State | What the law requires | Who arranges it, and when | If the system fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Massachusetts** (Title 5, 310 CMR 15.301) | An inspection by an approved System Inspector | At or within two years before transfer of title, or three years if the system was pumped every year. If weather prevents it, within six months after, with written notice to the buyer. The buyer gets the full report | Upgrade within two years of discovery (310 CMR 15.305), unless a shorter or scheduled period is set |
| **Iowa** (Iowa Code § 455B.172(11)) | An inspection by a state-certified inspector before any transfer of a home with one to four units | Before transfer. The county recorder won't record the deed without the report, or, if weather prevents it, a binding commitment from the buyer to inspect | Brought to current standards by the seller, or by the buyer if they agree |
| **Arizona** (Ariz. Admin. Code R18-9-A316) | A transfer-of-ownership inspection by a qualified inspector on the state's form | The seller hires the inspector, within six months before the transfer | The report records deficiencies and repairs. The rule we read doesn't set a repair deadline |
| **Maine**, shoreland property only (30-A M.R.S. § 4216) | An inspection by a state-certified inspector, plus the seller's written statement of any malfunction in the past 180 days | **The buyer**, before purchase, or within 9 months after if weather prevents it | Repair or replace within one year after transfer |
| **Rhode Island**, cesspools (R.I. Gen. Laws § 23-19.15-12) | A cesspool must be replaced with a septic system or connected to sewer. If nobody knows what the house has, an inspection is required before sale | The inspection happens before the sale. Buyers get a 10-day inspection period unless the parties agree otherwise | Replacement within 12 months of the sale |
| **Minnesota** (Minn. Stat. § 115.55, subd. 6) | A **written disclosure**, not an inspection: how sewage is handled, a map of the system, any known abandoned system, what the seller knows about compliance, and a copy of any past inspection report | The seller, before the purchase agreement is signed | A seller who knowingly fails to disclose can owe the buyer the cost of bringing the system into compliance, plus attorney fees |

**Reported, but we couldn't read the law:**
- **Delaware.** A state news release is reported to say that regulations from January 2014 require an inspection before property transfers.
- **New Hampshire.** Its shoreland septic rule (RSA 485-A:39) is reported to have changed in 2024, so that the buyer arranges an evaluation.

Both states' official sites blocked our connection, so treat these as leads to confirm with the state, not facts.

## What trips people up

**Sometimes the buyer is responsible, not the seller.** Maine puts the shoreland inspection on the person buying. In Iowa, when weather prevents an inspection, the buyer signs the commitment to inspect and becomes responsible for any required work. If you're buying, check which side your state's rule lands on before you rely on the seller to handle it.

**Old reports can count, within limits.** Massachusetts accepts a report up to two years old, or three with yearly pumping records. Maine skips the inspection if the seller has a certified report from the last 3 years, or if the system was installed in that time. Ask for the paperwork before booking a new one.

**Passing isn't judged the same everywhere.** Iowa says a system that is treating wastewater properly doesn't have to meet current construction standards. A failing one has to be brought up to them. Massachusetts gives two years to upgrade a failing system. Rhode Island's deadline of 12 months is about cesspools, not septic systems in general.

**Disclosure isn't inspection.** Minnesota's law requires the seller to tell you what they know, including handing over any old inspection report. It doesn't require a fresh inspection. A lender, town or county may still ask for one, so ask your county health department what applies to the address.

If you're not sure whether the house is on septic at all, start with [how to tell city sewer from septic](/guides/city-utilities-vs-septic-water-sewer-costs/). A standard home inspection usually doesn't cover the septic system; here's [what to book instead](/guides/home-inspection-include-septic-system/).
