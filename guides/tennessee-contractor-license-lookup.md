# Tennessee Contractor License Lookup: Check the Dollar Limit

A Tennessee contractor license comes with a price tag on it. The state's [Verify Public Search](https://search.cloud.commerce.tn.gov/) shows whether a license is active, but the line most homeowners skip is the monetary limit: the biggest single job that license allows. A builder whose license stops at $150,000 can't legally sign a contract more than 10% above that, however good their reviews. We ran the search on 14 November 2026, and this page walks through what we found.

## Below $25,000, the state may not license anyone

The Board for Licensing Contractors has regulated contractors since 1931, but its main license only starts at a job size. A contractor needs one to bid on, or agree a price for, a project of $25,000 or more. Trade subcontractors (electrical, mechanical, plumbing, HVAC and roofing) need one when their share reaches $25,000, and masonry subcontractors at $100,000.

Smaller jobs depend on where you live:
- **Home improvement license.** Residential remodeling from $3,000 to $24,999 needs one, but only in the nine counties that adopted the law: Bradley, Davidson, Hamilton, Haywood, Knox, Marion, Robertson, Rutherford and Shelby. The definition in T.C.A. § 62-6-501(4)(A) is wide. It reaches driveways, pools, porches, fences, roofing and even painting.
- **Limited electricians and plumbers.** The LLE and LLP licenses cover trade work under $25,000, and only where a city uses the state Division of Fire Prevention for permits and inspections. Plumbing maintenance under $500 is exempt. A city can have its own licensing requirement instead, so the board itself tells people to check with the local government first.

So a bathroom remodel in that price range in Davidson County calls for a home improvement license. The same job in a county outside the list may need no state license at all.

## Reading the record: find the limit first

![The Modifiers box of a Tennessee contractor record, listing classification BC with BC-A unlimited and BC-b(sm) at $750,000.00, and an aggregate monetary limit of unlimited](/images/tennessee-license-record-monetary-limit.jpg "The Modifiers box from a real record we opened on 14 November 2026. We cropped it to this box, which carries no name, number or address.")

A record opens with a Summary (license number, status, expiration date and original licensure date) and a city and county. The useful part is lower down, under **Modifiers**:
- **Classification codes.** BC-A is residential building, BC-B commercial, BC-C industrial, CE electrical, CMC-A plumbing and CMC-C HVAC. Match the code to your job. A plumbing contractor's license doesn't cover building your addition.
- **A limit per classification.** The record above allows unlimited residential work but caps small commercial work at $750,000.
- **The aggregate monetary limit.** This is the cap per project or contract, and there's no cap on how many projects a contractor takes on. Splitting one job into phases to fit under the limit isn't allowed. The board allows a 10% tolerance over it.

The limit is a money test, not a skill rating. The board sets it at 10 times the lesser of the company's working capital or net worth, plus experience. An unlimited license needs $300,000 of each. One license type is stricter: the Restricted Residential Limited License (BC-A/r) covers residential work under $125,000 for builders who took a course at a community college or vocational school instead of the exams. It gets no 10% tolerance, and the contract or permit value, land cost and profit included, has to fit under the limit.

Two more habits help. The record prints the time it was retrieved, so a saved copy shows when you checked. And since 17 September 2026 the department has stopped mailing license certificates; contractors print their own. A printed certificate tells you less than the live record.

## Expired licenses stay in the results

Tennessee's search keeps old records. Filtering by the contractors board and license type alone returned 62,695 results for us, many marked Expired or Application Expired. The first page alone had licenses that expired in 1998 and 1999. Read the status on every line, not just the name.

A name search runs across all of the department's boards at once. Our search for a large homebuilder brought back 4 records, two of them real estate licenses. Use the board, license type and status filters on the left to cut the list down. Each result shows a Matched On line saying which field it matched.

## The person who passed the exams

Every license has a qualifying agent: the owner, officer or full-time employee who took the exams for the company. You check them in a separate tool, the board's Verify a Qualifying Agent dashboard, rather than in the main search. A qualifying agent can serve only one company as an employee. They can qualify a second company only if they own the majority of it. If the person who walked your site is named as the qualifying agent on a different company's license, ask how that works.

## Bond, insurance and out-of-state licenses

- **Contractors** carry general liability insurance scaled to their monetary limit, plus workers' compensation unless exempt. Since 1 July 2026, a contractor can post a surety bond of at least 50% of their limit instead of filing a CPA-reviewed or audited financial statement.
- **Home improvement contractors** post a $10,000 bond.
- **Licenses from other states don't carry over.** The board says plainly that a license from another state isn't enough to operate in Tennessee.

## Building your own house

Owners can build their own home without a license, with one permit every two years, as long as the house isn't for sale, lease or rent (T.C.A. § 62-6-103 and Rule 0680-1-.22). Anyone the owner pays more than $25,000 counts as a prime contractor and needs a license. The board warns homeowners never to pull a permit for a contractor. Doing so can relieve that contractor of most liability and insurance duties.

For a buyer, the same rule is a question for the seller: was the house built under the owner exemption? If so, no licensed builder answered for the work, and the exemption only ever covered a home the owner wasn't building to sell.

## What a complaint can and can't do

The board's power stops at the license. It can't get you a refund, award damages or order repairs; those take a civil suit, or the state Division of Consumer Affairs mediation program. What it can do is discipline. Rule 0680-01-.27 lists misconduct that can cost a license, including:
- failing to honor a warranty;
- failing to answer a customer about unfinished or poor work;
- pulling a permit for a job an unlicensed contractor is running.

Discipline is published in a monthly report covering every board in the department. January to October 2026 were posted when we looked.

## How we checked this

On 14 November 2026 we used Verify Public Search twice: once filtered only by board and license type, and once by organization name for a large homebuilder we don't name. We opened that company's record, and the screenshot is its Modifiers box.

We also read the board's pages on:
- each license type;
- complaints, the qualifying agent dashboard and the discipline reports;
- its help center articles on owner-builders, warranties and qualifying agents.

The statute itself is hosted on LexisNexis, so we cite only the section numbers the board's own pages give. The October discipline report downloads as a file rather than opening in a browser, so we didn't read it. We also don't know whether a record with discipline shows it on the record page.
