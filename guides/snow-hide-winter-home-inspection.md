# Buying a House This Winter? 8 Things Snow Can Hide From a Home Inspection.

A winter purchase comes with something summer buyers rarely deal with: an inspection done while part of the house is under snow. That isn't the inspector cutting corners. The rules written for home inspectors say, in plain words, what they don't have to dig out.

We read one of those rules in full: Washington's standard of practice for home inspectors, chapter WAC 308-408C, in the version that took effect this August. Its opening section sets the terms: "A home inspection is not technically exhaustive and does not identify concealed conditions or latent defects." Snow is about as common a concealed condition as there is. Each state writes its own standard, so the exact wording where you're buying may differ, but these are the eight places snow and cold get in the way.

## The eight

**1. The roof covering.** Washington's rule says the inspector is not required to "Remove snow, ice, debris or other material that obscures the roof surface or prevents access to the roof." Nor do they have to walk a roof they judge unsafe or easy to damage, which can describe an icy one. This item leads the list for a reason: our report engine puts a full roof replacement at $8,000 – $25,000+.

**2. Everything else up there.** The roof inspection also covers flashings, vents, skylights, the visible part of the chimney, and the gutters and downspouts. Snow that hides the shingles hides those too, and a gutter packed with ice can't be judged for slope or leaks at the seams.

**3. The slope of the ground against the house.** The site inspection includes "land grade, and water drainage directly adjacent to the foundation", and the inspector checks "For proper grading and drainage slope." Under snow, nobody can see which way the ground falls, and that is the thing that decides where meltwater goes in April.

**4. The bottom of the walls and the foundation.** Inspectors look at "visible, exposed foundations" and visible siding. Snow shovelled off the walk and piled against the house turns the lowest course of siding and the top of the foundation into the part that isn't visible. Section 030 of the rule says inspectors aren't required to move snow or ice at all.

**5. Driveways, walks, steps and patios.** Their condition is inspected where they touch the house. Cracks, uneven slabs and a step that has lifted are under the snow, or under a layer of grit and ice melt.

**6. Decks.** An attached deck is inspected; one standing away from the house isn't required to be. Snow shows you the railing and little else. Deck boards and stair treads can't be probed for soft wood when they're buried.

**7. The air conditioner.** The rule lists this among the things an inspector doesn't have to do: "Operate cooling system components if the exterior temperature is below sixty degrees Fahrenheit". The same clause covers running equipment when doing so might damage it. A heat pump can still be tested in heating mode, but a central AC may well be described and not run. Our engine puts HVAC replacement at $6,000 – $15,000.

**8. Plumbing in a winterized house.** An empty house may have had its water shut off and lines drained for the winter. The rule's plumbing section says inspectors don't have to inspect "Any system that is shut down or winterized." No water means no check of drains, flow or leaks under the sinks. Ask before the inspection is booked whether the water can be turned back on for it.

## Make the report say what it couldn't see

One line in Washington's rule works in the winter buyer's favour: "If the roof is not traversed, the alternate method used to inspect the roof must be reported." So read the roof section for how it was looked at, not just for the verdict. The same goes for every item above: a report that says an item wasn't inspected because of snow is giving you something to act on.

Here is what we'd ask for, item by item:

| Under snow or cold | Ask for | Where the rule covers it |
|---|---|---|
| Roof covering, flashings, gutters | how the roof was inspected, in writing; the roof's age and receipts from the seller | WAC 308-408C-090 |
| Grading and drainage | a note that grade couldn't be seen, if it couldn't | WAC 308-408C-170 |
| Foundation and lower siding | the same note for walls behind snowbanks | WAC 308-408C-070 and -080 |
| Driveways, walks, decks | which surfaces were covered | WAC 308-408C-080 and -170 |
| Air conditioner | whether it was run; the data-plate date | WAC 308-408C-130 |
| Plumbing in an empty house | whether water was on | WAC 308-408C-100 |

The same rule doesn't require an inspector to predict how long a roof covering has left, so that answer has to come from somewhere else. Our guide on [how to tell how old a roof is](/guides/how-to-tell-how-old-a-roof-is/) covers the records and the clues; for the cooling side, [what HVAC techs wish buyers knew about system age](/guides/hvac-techs-wish-buyers-knew-about-system-age/) explains the data plate. ENERGY STAR suggests considering replacement of an air conditioner or heat pump once it's more than 10 years old, and a furnace or boiler at more than 15.

## Can the seller fix it in spring instead?

The idea of holding money back until the weather turns comes from real rules, just not the ones most resale houses fall under.

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide (topic B4-1.2-05) does allow postponed improvements: the work must be finished within 180 days of the note date, the lender holds back 120% of the estimated cost, and inclement weather is named as a valid reason. That table is headed Requirements for New or Proposed Construction.

For an existing house, the guide splits problems in two:
- **Minor conditions** that don't affect safety, soundness or structural integrity can be escrowed after closing, at the lender's own discretion.
- **Items that do affect them** need a "subject to" appraisal, and the lender has to verify the repair before selling the loan to Fannie Mae. The guide's examples include active roof leaks and worn roof shingles.

So if a roof problem turns up once the snow clears, a holdback on a resale house is the lender's call, not something the rules hand you. Ask your lender before you count on one, and price the roof now rather than in March.
