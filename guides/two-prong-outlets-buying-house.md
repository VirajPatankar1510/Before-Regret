# Two-Prong Outlets When Buying a House: What to Check

Two flat slots and no round hole. In a house you're buying, the outlet itself is the small part. What matters is the wiring behind it, and the outlet's face can't tell you about that. So start with the wiring, then the paperwork, then the price.

## What the two slots tell you

Today's code doesn't allow new two-slot outlets on ordinary household circuits. The 2021 International Residential Code, section E4002.2, puts it in one line: "Receptacles installed on 15- and 20-ampere-rated branch circuits shall be of the grounding type." A grounding-type receptacle is the three-hole kind. So a two-prong outlet is an older installation that nobody has replaced since.

That code line covers what gets installed. It doesn't, on its own, tell you that an existing outlet has to come out before you close. Whether yours must be changed depends on your local building department and on what your insurer asks for. Both are worth asking about directly rather than guessing.

There are two quite different houses behind those slots:

- **The box is grounded, the outlet isn't.** Some older wiring runs in metal conduit or armored cable (the "BX" you'll see on inspection forms), and the metal box may give a ground path even though the outlet has no third hole.
- **There's no ground anywhere on the circuit.** Knob-and-tube wiring, for one, has no ground conductor at all.

You can't tell which one you have by looking at the outlet. An electrician can, by checking the box.

## The gray adapter in the seller's outlets

You'll often find a small two-to-three prong adapter plugged in, with a metal tab hanging off it. That tab is the whole point of the adapter. When the US Consumer Product Safety Commission announced a 1997 recall of these adapters (recall 97-066), its notice quoted the warning printed on them: "CAUTION...CONNECT TAB TO GROUNDED SCREW". The package said the adapter was "For connection of 3 pronged plug to a 2 slot grounded outlet."

Read that last word again: *grounded*. The adapter is made for the first kind of house, where the box is grounded and the cover screw connects to it. If the box has no ground path, the tab connects to nothing, and the third prong of whatever you plug in isn't grounded either.

That recall is a second reminder not to trust the plastic. It covered about 60,000 packs that were marked as UL listed when they weren't, and CPSC said they presented fire and shock hazards because of how they were built.

So what's the difference between an open ground and using a 2 to 3 prong adapter? Less than you'd think: they can end in the same place. An [open ground](/guides/open-ground-mean-electrical-inspection/) is a three-hole outlet with no ground behind it; an adapter on an ungrounded box is a two-slot outlet wearing a third prong. In both cases the round pin is connected to nothing.

## What the inspection and the insurance form write down

A home inspector may note two-prong outlets in passing. Where the house needs a 4-point inspection for insurance, the wording on the form matters more than the inspector's tone.

We read Florida's sample 4-point inspection form (Insp4pt 03 25) line by line. Under Electrical, a "Hazards Present" checklist includes a box for "Improper grounding". A separate wiring-type section asks the inspector to tick what's in the house, and "Cloth (Knob & Tube)" has its own box, apart from "NM, BX or Conduit". The form tells the homeowner that underwriting will rely on it, and that "This information only is used to determine insurability".

So the outlets are less important than which boxes get ticked. If you need a 4-point, ask for a copy of the completed form before your inspection deadline, and read the Electrical section yourself. If "Improper grounding" or the knob-and-tube box is ticked, take that page to your insurance agent and ask, in writing, what they need before they'll write the policy.

## When the house is old enough for knob and tube

In an older house, two-prong outlets are a reason to ask a narrower question: is any knob-and-tube wiring still live? Our report engine flags knob and tube for houses built up to 1955. It has no ground conductor, so every outlet on it is ungrounded, whatever is on the wall.

If it is live, the outlet question turns into a wiring question. Our engine puts a partial to whole-home rewire at $8,000 – $30,000+, and the [removal cost guide](/guides/knob-and-tube-removal-cost-permits/) explains what moves the price. Whether it has to be done before you close is covered on [its own page](/guides/knob-tube-wiring-have-be-replaced-before-closing/). If you're not sure what you're looking at in the basement or attic, start with [what knob-and-tube wiring looks like](/guides/what-is-knob-and-tube-wiring/).

## Fixing them is a separate job

The electrical code allows a few specific ways to replace an ungrounded two-prong outlet, and running a new ground wire is only one of them. We explain the options on the [open-ground page](/guides/open-ground-mean-electrical-inspection/) rather than repeat them here. If the house also needs GFCI protection in the kitchen, bathrooms or garage, the [GFCI guide](/guides/need-gfci-outlets-pass-home-inspection/) covers where.

For a buyer, the useful step is a written quote from a licensed electrician that names the method for each outlet, before your contingency ends. A quote that just says *replace outlets* doesn't tell you whether the problem is solved or covered up.

## What to ask before closing

1. Which rooms have two-prong outlets, and did anyone check whether those boxes are grounded?
2. Are there three-hole outlets on the same old circuits? If so, were they tested for an open ground?
3. Is any knob-and-tube wiring still energized, and where?
4. If a 4-point is needed: what was ticked under Electrical, and can you have the completed form?
5. What does a licensed electrician quote, in writing, for the method they'd use?
6. Has your insurer said, in writing, what they need?
