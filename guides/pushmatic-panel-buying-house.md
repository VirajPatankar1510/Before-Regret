# Is a Pushmatic Panel a Problem When Buying a House?

If the breakers behind the panel door are rectangular buttons you push rather than switches you flip, you're looking at a Pushmatic. The label may say Pushmatic, Bulldog or I-T-E. Our [panel brand guide](/guides/identify-electrical-panel-brand/) shows how to confirm the name from the label inside the door. This page starts after that, with the question that follows: does this panel change the deal?

## We looked for a recall and found none

On 19 November 2026 we queried the Consumer Product Safety Commission's public recall database for Pushmatic, by product name and by recall title. Both searches returned 0 records.

A search that finds nothing only means something if the search works. So we ran the same query for Challenger, another older panel brand. It came back with Challenger's 1988 replacement program for a batch of its GFCI breakers, which shows the database answers this kind of question.

No recall isn't the same as *this panel is fine*. It means there's no product-wide defect on record to point at, and the question moves to this particular panel: how old it is, what state it's in, and what your insurer says. That's a different position from a brand with a known record, so don't let anyone lump it in with [Federal Pacific](/guides/federal-pacific-stab-lok-panel-inspectors-flag/) or [Zinsco](/guides/will-zinsco-panel-fail-4-point-inspection/) without saying why.

## Parts are still sold

A common line about older panels is that you can't get breakers for them any more. For Pushmatic, that isn't true. We found Connecticut Electric's UBIP line listed by a US electrical supplier, described as "Classified for use in Pushmatic and Bulldog load centers" and ETL listed. It's a replacement made by another company, not an original part.

Whether a replacement breaker suits a given panel is the electrician's call, not yours. The point for a buyer is narrower: *no parts* isn't a reason, by itself, to ask for a new panel.

## What a 4-point form asks about the panel

On a 4-point inspection for insurance, the brand is one line out of several. The sample form used in Florida (Insp4pt 03 25) has a panel section that wants three things written down:

- **Brand/Model** of the main panel;
- **Panel age**;
- **Year last updated**.

Below those sit tick boxes for things like scorching, corrosion and tripping breakers, and the sample says the answers feed the decision on whether the house can be insured.

So two Pushmatic panels can read very differently on paper. One that's original to the house with scorch marks reads one way; one that an electrician has updated, with the year written down, reads another. Ask the seller whether any electrical work has been done, and whether there's a permit or invoice with a date on it.

## Ask your insurer before you negotiate

We don't publish lists of which insurers accept which panels. Those rules aren't public, they differ by state and they change. The only answer that counts is your insurer's, so get it before your inspection deadline:

1. Give your agent the brand and model from the label, the panel's age, and the inspection report or 4-point form.
2. Ask whether they'll write the policy with this panel in place, and on what conditions.
3. Ask for that answer in an email, not a phone call.

If the answer is yes, you may not need to do anything. If it's no, or *only if it's replaced*, you have a price to negotiate with. Our report engine puts a panel replacement at $1,500 – $4,000, and flags panel brand for houses built from 1950 to 1989. Get an electrician's written quote for this house, and use it in a repair request, a credit or a lower price. If you're buying in Texas, the [option period guide](/guides/option-period-negotiating-home-repairs/) explains how that window works.

## What an electrician should look at

A home inspector notes the brand. An electrician can tell you more, and that's worth paying for before your deadline:

- signs of heat, scorching or corrosion inside the panel;
- whether the panel and the service size still suit how the house is used;
- whether a breaker has been replaced, and with what;
- a written price to replace the panel, if it comes to that.

## How we checked this

On 19 November 2026 we searched CPSC's recall records for Pushmatic two ways, by product name and by recall title, and got 0 records each time; the Challenger search was our control. That day we also went through the panel lines of Florida's sample 4-point form and a supplier's listing for the Connecticut Electric UBIP breaker, and took the cost and year range from our report engine's panel-brand rule as it stood when this page went live.

Three things we couldn't pin down. We found no primary test data on how Pushmatic breakers hold up over decades, so there's no claim about that here. One insurer's public help page sat behind a login, and we left it alone. And the breaker maker's own site redirected somewhere unrelated, so the supplier's listing stands in for it.
