# Leaving Home for Thanksgiving? Don't Ignore These 7 Things Before You Leave.

Thanksgiving often means a house left empty for a few days, just as the heating season gets going. A few days is plenty of time for a cold snap to arrive while nobody is home to notice.

The reason to bother goes beyond the plumber's bill. In a January 2026 notice, Massachusetts' Division of Insurance put it this way: "Most homeowners’ insurance covers “sudden and accidental” water damage from a burst pipe only if the home was properly heated and not left unoccupied." The same notice says 48.6% of homeowners' claims in the state in 2024 were for non-flood water losses and freezing-related damage.

## Before you lock the door

**1. Leave the heat on, at 55°F or higher.** That's the Red Cross's floor for a house left empty in cold weather. Keep the setting the same day and night, too: a schedule that drops the temperature overnight keeps doing it while you're gone. If the thermostat has an away mode, check what temperature it actually holds.

The building code explains why the heat matters so much. In areas with a winter design temperature of 32°F or lower, the International Residential Code (IRC P2603.5) allows water pipes in exterior walls, attics and crawl spaces only if they're protected from freezing "by insulation or heat or both." Where the protection is heat, turning the heating down takes it away.

**2. Open the cabinet doors under the sinks.** Kitchen and bathroom both, so warmer air reaches the pipes behind them. If anyone stopping by will have small children with them, move cleaners up out of reach first, as the Red Cross reminds.

**3. Close the garage door** if water lines run through the garage. It's the Red Cross's advice, and the easiest one to forget on the way out.

**4. Finish the outdoor taps.** Massachusetts' advice is to shut off and cover the faucets used for garden hoses. If you haven't done the full routine this year, our [fall checklist](/guides/fall-home-maintenance-checklist-new-homeowners/) has the Red Cross's steps in order.

**5. Hold the mail.** USPS Hold Mail is free and runs from 3 to 30 days, and you can request it online up to 30 days ahead. It holds everything, letters and packages, for everyone at the address. If someone is staying behind, their post stops too.

**6. Leave a key with someone close by.** Keeping the heat on protects you only while the heat stays on. A furnace that quits or a power cut on the second day is the one case none of the steps above covers. Someone who can look in, check that the thermostat still reads what you set, and phone you is the backstop.

**7. Read two parts of your policy before you go.** Find where it talks about freezing, and how it defines a home that is unoccupied or vacant. Those are the words that decide a frozen-pipe claim, and they are easier to read now than after one.

## What two state regulators say

We read the winter guidance from two state insurance regulators side by side. They agree on the habit and differ on the wording:

| | Massachusetts Division of Insurance | Connecticut Insurance Department |
|---|---|---|
| Frozen or burst pipes | covered "only if the home was properly heated and not left unoccupied" | "Most policies cover pipe replacement and water damage" |
| The exception they name | a home not properly heated, or left unoccupied | "coverage may not apply if you turned off the furnace for the winter without winterizing the home" |
| Their caveat | check your coverage with your insurer or agent | "Policies may vary in coverage" |

Two things stand out. Neither page says how many days away counts as unoccupied; that definition lives in your own policy. And Connecticut's line is a reason not to improvise: turning the heating off to save money while you're away, without winterizing the house, is exactly the situation it singles out.

## When you get home

![A house in cross-section labelled with the places the Red Cross lists for frozen pipes: outdoor hose bibs, the garage, the attic, the basement or crawl space, kitchen cabinets, exterior walls with little or no insulation, and where the water service enters through the foundation.](/images/where-pipes-freeze-first.webp "Drawn by Before Regret from the American Red Cross's frozen-pipe guidance.")

Turn on a tap before you unpack. The Red Cross's rule of thumb: if only a trickle comes out, suspect a frozen pipe. Look first against exterior walls and where the water service comes in through the foundation.

If one has frozen:
- Keep the faucet open, so water can run as the ice melts.
- Warm the pipe with a hair dryer, a heating pad, or a space heater kept away from anything that burns. Never an open flame.
- If you can't find the frozen section, can't reach it, or can't thaw it, call a licensed plumber.
- Check the other faucets in the house. One frozen pipe can mean others.

If something has already burst, Connecticut's claim advice is practical: photograph the damage, make the repairs needed to stop it getting worse, hold off on permanent repairs until your insurer has inspected, and keep every receipt.
