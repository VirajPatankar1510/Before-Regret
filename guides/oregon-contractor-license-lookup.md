# Oregon CCB License Lookup: What a Contractor's Record Shows

Oregon's Construction Contractors Board (CCB) runs the lookup at [CCB License Search](https://search.ccb.state.or.us/search/). Search with the contractor's CCB number if you have it. Oregon makes contractors print it on their advertising, so it should be on the website, the flyer or the business card. We ran the search on 13 November 2026 and opened a real record. This page goes through what each part means, and the few places where people read it wrong.

## An Oregon license is a business test, not a skills test

The CCB says this itself, and it changes how much a license is worth: "Oregon does not require contractors - other than plumbers, electricians and a few other specific trades - to have training in construction."

To get licensed, one person from the business does 16 hours of training and passes an open-book exam of 80 multiple-choice questions. The pass mark is 70 percent. The exam covers law and business practice, not building.

So an active CCB license tells you the business is bonded and insured, and that the CCB can hold it to account. It tells you nothing about whether the crew can hang a door. For that you still need references, and photos of past jobs like yours.

## Finding the right business among look-alike names

A name search is broad. When we searched for one large firm, it returned 20 records: the firm's own current and past licenses, mixed in with unrelated small businesses that shared the surname. One license number showed up on two rows with different statuses, Active on one and No Longer Associated on the other.

In that single list we saw five statuses:
- Active
- Not Active - Expired
- Not Active - Expired after a lapse
- Not Active - Voluntarily Surrendered
- No Longer Associated

Anything other than Active means that license can't be used for your job.

The opposite mistake is just as easy. The search page warns that a missed name may be "a discrepancy in the spelling or punctuation of the name," so a blank result doesn't prove the contractor is unlicensed. Try a shorter name, or ask for the number.

Three smaller things we noticed:
- Records refresh every 6 hours.
- An open record lists "See also" license numbers: other licenses tied to the same business, which is how you spot a company that has traded under several.
- Result pages have no web address you can save. Take a screenshot on the day you sign.

## The ten-year history on the summary page

![The history part of an Oregon CCB license summary: Unpaid Debt with unpaid claims and civil penalties at $0.00, then Complaint History, Disciplinary History and Administrative Suspensions, each covering the past 10 years and each answered No](/images/oregon-ccb-record-history-sections.jpg "Part of a real CCB record summary we opened on 13 November 2026, cropped to the history sections. The business name and license number are left out.")

The first page after you click a license number answers four questions, each covering the past 10 years:
- **Unpaid Debt:** claims and civil penalties the contractor still owes.
- **Complaint History:** whether anyone filed a complaint (a claim) against them.
- **Disciplinary History:** civil penalties or suspensions.
- **Administrative Suspensions:** whether the CCB suspended the license for lack of bond or insurance.

A row of "No" is a good sign, with one limit. It covers 10 years and this license number only. A contractor who closed one company and opened another starts the new number clean, which is another reason to read the "See also" line.

## Check that the endorsement says Residential

Click "Learn more about this business" for the detail page. The line that matters most for a homeowner is the endorsement type. The CCB's endorsement chart sets out what each one allows:

| Endorsement | What it covers | Residential bond | Insurance per occurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residential General Contractor | Any number of trades on a house | $25,000 | $500,000 |
| Residential Specialty Contractor | One or two unrelated trades; three or more on one property only if the contract is $2,500 or less | $20,000 | $300,000 |
| Residential Limited Contractor | Part-time and handyman work: no contract over $5,000, and under $40,000 of business a year | $15,000 | $100,000 |

A commercial endorsement covers commercial buildings. The record we opened belonged to a large commercial firm, and its bond section showed a Commercial bond with an amount but "None" on the Residential row. That firm is fully licensed and still has no residential bond for a homeowner to claim against. Working outside the endorsement is one of the violations the CCB investigates.

In Oregon, handyman work needs a license too: the CCB lists it with roofing, painting and plumbing as work that does. Gutter cleaning, pressure washing for cleaning, and debris clean-up don't.

## Three more lines on the detail page

**Lead-based paint.** The record shows whether the business holds a Certified Lead-Based Paint Renovation license, which the record says is "Required to bid and work on pre-1978 residential structures." If your house is that old and the line says NO, that business shouldn't be bidding on the job.

**Workers' compensation.** The record lists coverage, but the CCB isn't told automatically when that coverage changes. If the contractor has employees on your job, confirm it with the Oregon Workers' Compensation Division.

**Electricians and plumbers.** Their licenses aren't on this record. The CCB search page links to the Building Codes Division for electrical and plumbing licenses, so run that search too for those trades.

## When a job goes wrong, the bond is slower than people expect

Hire someone unlicensed and you lose both the bond and the CCB's help mediating the dispute. With a licensed contractor, the path on residential work runs like this:

1. Mail the contractor a 30-day pre-complaint notice by certified mail, to their address on record with the CCB. If they refuse it or it comes back, you can still file once the 30 days are up.
2. File the complaint within one year of the date the work was substantially completed, or the date it stopped.
3. Pay the $50 processing fee when the CCB asks for it, not before.
4. Go to mediation. The CCB doesn't decide who was right. It reports that about 70% of disputes settle there.
5. If mediation fails, go to court or arbitration. The bond pays only on a judgment or an arbitration award, and only once the contractor hasn't paid it.

Two details catch people out at step 5. Small claims court has a $10,000 limit, and the bond doesn't cover attorney fees, so a bigger claim in circuit court can cost more than it recovers. And payout takes at least 60 days from when the CCB receives the certified judgment. If several claims arrive within the same 90-day period, payment can be held until all are resolved. The bond is a fixed amount shared among everyone with a claim, so filing promptly matters.

## Buying a house that was just remodeled

Two Oregon rules matter more to a buyer than to the person who hired the contractor.

**You probably can't use the CCB for the seller's contractor.** The CCB's own FAQ says that when the work was done before the sale and the defects appear afterwards, "neither party may file a valid complaint unless there is an assignment in the sale agreement documents that links the new owner to the contractor." If the seller is handing over a recent roof or remodel, ask your agent or a real estate attorney about that assignment before closing. Afterwards it's too late to add.

**Liens on a flipped house.** The Homebuyer Protection Act (ORS 87.007) covers new homes, and existing homes with at least $50,000 of improvements, additions or remodeling within three months before the sale. A contractor selling such a home has to protect the buyer from construction liens. The CCB lists the ways a contractor-seller can do that:
- title insurance that doesn't exclude lien claims;
- 25 percent of the price held in escrow, or a bond or letter of credit for 25 percent;
- lien waivers or releases;
- closing after the lien filing deadline, usually 75 days.

The seller fills in a Notice of Compliance with the Homebuyers Protection Act. Ask for it, and run the seller through the CCB search too.

The same search page has a separate Home Inspectors search. Oregon certifies inspectors, and a certified inspector must own or work for a CCB-licensed business, so your inspector should come up in both searches.

## Before you sign anything

Any agreement over $2,000 has to be in writing. Put the contractor's CCB number in it, exactly as the record shows it. You can also cancel an initial contract for work on your home with written notice before midnight of the next business day (ORS 701.310). That right doesn't apply where work has already substantially begun.
