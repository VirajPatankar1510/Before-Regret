# Can Your Insurer Drop You Over Roof Age? The Rules by State

In late 2026, headlines said Texas had just banned insurers from dropping homes over an old roof. The Texas rule isn't new, and Texas isn't the only state with one. A few states now write roof age into law, a few more protect older homes in general, and the wording differs in ways that matter once a renewal letter arrives. We read each law and put them side by side.

## Six laws, side by side

We read these on 10 November 2026. This isn't a survey of every state. It covers the laws we found and could read at the source, so if your state is missing, it may still have a rule. Ask your state insurance department.

| State | What the law says | Roof named? | Route for an older roof | In force |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Florida** (Fla. Stat. § 627.7011(5)) | No refusing to issue or renew solely because of the age of a roof under 15 years old | Yes | At 15 or older, you can have it inspected at your expense before the insurer requires a new roof. If the inspector finds 5 or more years of life left, age alone can't be the reason | Policies issued or renewed on or after July 1, 2022 |
| **Virginia** (Va. Code § 38.2-2114(K), 2026 Acts ch. 657) | No refusing, cancelling or not renewing an owner-occupied home solely over the age of an asphalt shingle roof under 15 years old, or without letting the owner or buyer prove its age | Asphalt shingle only | At 15 or older, an inspection at your expense before a new roof can be required. A report of 5 or more years of life left means replacement can't be a condition, and condition alone can't be the reason | January 1, 2027 |
| **California** (Ins. Code § 676.14, 2026 Stats. ch. 693) | No refusing to issue or renew solely over roof age if you get an independent inspection confirming at least 5 years of life left | Yes | You arrange and pay for the inspection | January 1, 2028 |
| **Texas** (28 TAC § 21.1006) | No declining to write a policy based on the age of the property or its components, and roofing is on the list | Yes | None set out. The insurer can still decline over physical condition | Already in force; clarifying changes proposed September 29, 2026 |
| **Maine** (24-A M.R.S. § 3057) | No cancelling, refusing to issue or renewing solely on the dwelling's age without considering its current condition | No, the dwelling | None set out | Since 2003 (P.L. 2003, c. 671) |
| **Minnesota** (Minn. Stat. § 72A.20, subd. 13) | Refusing to renew or declining to write solely because of the age of the main structure is an unfair practice | No, the structure | None set out. Price may reflect the age of parts whose age affects the risk of loss | In the 2026 statutes |

On Texas: the agency's own proposal says the changes "do not change the rule's meaning or application." The prohibition was already there. What the September proposal does is move roofing, wiring, plumbing and the other components squarely into the ban, and add siding. Comments closed on 9 November 2026. We found no notice yet that the changes have been adopted. The agency's announcement also says the rule covers not renewing, though the rule's text speaks of declining to write a policy.

## Florida and Virginia: the same numbers, different promises

Both laws use 15 years and 5 years, and it's easy to read them as the same rule. They aren't quite.

**Florida protects an older roof on age.** If an authorized inspector finds 5 or more years of useful life, the insurer may not refuse to issue or renew "solely because of roof age."

**Virginia protects an older roof on condition and replacement.** Its age protection is written for roofs under 15 years old, and for owners who weren't given the chance to prove the roof's age. Past 15, the protection works differently. With an inspector's report of 5 or more years of life, a new roof can't be made a condition of the policy, and the insurer can't refuse solely over the roof's condition. If you have an asphalt roof older than 15 in Virginia, ask your agent in writing which part of the law your renewal falls under.

**Who can inspect differs too.** Florida's inspector must be "approved by the insurer" and be one of a list: a licensed home inspector, a building code inspector, a licensed contractor or roofing contractor, an engineer, an architect, or someone else the insurer recognizes. Virginia lists a licensed home inspector, a licensed residential contractor and a professional engineer without that approval wording, plus anyone else the insurer recognizes. In Florida, check that your inspector is on the insurer's approved list before you pay for the visit.

## How the law counts a roof's age

Florida and Virginia count age the same way. The clock starts on the last date 100 percent of the roof was built or replaced to the code of the time. If the roof was redone in stages, the clock starts on the date of the first partial replacement, once later work finished the whole surface. A roof replaced in two halves, years apart, is as old as the first half.

Virginia also says which proof is enough. It accepts any of these from the owner or the buyer, and the law calls them "sufficiently determinative" of the roof's age or condition:

- receipts or contracts from the installation or replacement;
- building permits for the work;
- an authorized inspector's report estimating the age, or confirming 5 or more years of life.

That's the paperwork worth collecting in any state. If the seller can't produce it, here's [how to prove a roof's age for insurance](/guides/prove-roof-age-for-insurance/) from permits and other records.

## What none of these laws stop

Every rule above turns on the word "solely" or on age alone. That leaves real room for the insurer:

- **Condition.** Texas keeps the right to decline over "the physical condition of the property." Maine's bar applies only when an insurer acts on age "without consideration of the current condition of the property." Virginia doesn't require cover for a roof with unrepaired damage, defects, deterioration or similar problems an inspector notes. And even a finding of 5 or more years of life still lets a Virginia insurer require repair of "damaged, deteriorated, or deficient portions."
- **Price.** Minnesota lets rates reflect the age of parts of the house whose age affects the risk of loss. Virginia's nonrenewal section says nothing in it "prohibits any insurer from setting rates in accordance with relevant actuarial data."
- **Age as one factor.** California's rule says it doesn't stop an insurer "taking into consideration the age of the roof."
- **Other lawful reasons.** Florida keeps an insurer's right to refuse "for other lawful reasons," and its roof rule doesn't apply to mobile home policies.
- **Coverage terms.** Virginia lets an insurer cut coverage, raise a deductible or drop an endorsement, but only on "specific, documented risk characteristics" of the property, never just to get around the roof rule.

## If a nonrenewal letter arrives

The notice rules differ as much as the roof rules.

- **Virginia:** the notice must give at least 30 days and the specific reason. Within 10 days of getting it, you can ask the Commissioner of Insurance in writing to review it. The policy stays in force during the review, unless the reason is unpaid premium.
- **Minnesota:** the notice must give 60 days and the specific reason. You can appeal any nonrenewal to the commissioner of commerce, and the policy continues until the appeal ends.
- **California, from 2028:** the insurer must explain the grounds in plain language and send the non-aerial images it relied on. If you dispute the roof's condition, you can ask for an on-site inspection.

For what to send back, see [what to do after a nonrenewal over a roof photo](/guides/home-insurance-nonrenewal-roof-photo/).
