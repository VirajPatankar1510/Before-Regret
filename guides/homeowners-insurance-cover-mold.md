# Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Mold?

The question an adjuster asks about mold isn't really about the mold. It's about the water that caused it: where it came from, how fast, and how long it went on. Get that part straight and you can predict most of the answer before you file anything.

## The cause of the water decides it

The Texas Department of Insurance sets out the line more plainly than most policies do. Here is its guidance, organised by cause:

| Where the water came from | What the Texas regulator says |
|---|---|
| Sudden and accidental, such as a burst pipe, a toilet overflow or a broken washer hose | Generally covered, and "if mold develops on a damaged item, it would be covered" |
| A gradual leak or seepage | Not covered by most home policies, "and that includes damage from mold" |
| A flood | Not covered, because home policies don't cover floods. That takes a separate flood policy |
| Cleanup and testing after the damaged item is removed | Not included in most home policies, though "your insurance company may let you add coverage for cleanup caused by a covered loss" |

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners says the same thing nationally. Most homeowners policies offer "limited or no coverage" for mold, you may be able to buy an endorsement for it, and "a homeowners policy isn't a maintenance contract". That last point is why the slow leak under a sink loses. It's treated as upkeep the owner should have caught.

Your own policy's wording is what counts, and it differs between insurers and states. When you read it, look for the words fungi, mold or rot, and for any separate dollar limit that applies to mold.

## Speed matters twice

Mold grows fast. The Texas regulator says it "can start growing within a day after a leak", and the EPA's advice is to dry water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours. Its rule is short: "The key to mold control is moisture control".

Speed also matters for the claim. The Texas guidance says you usually must report water damage that was hidden from view within days of first seeing it. Stop the water, start drying, photograph the damage before repairs, keep damaged items until the adjuster has seen them, and tell your insurer as soon as you can.

For small patches, the EPA says most people can clean up an area of less than about 10 square feet themselves. Anything bigger, or growth after heavy water damage, is a job for a professional.

## Buying a house that had mold

Texas law spells out two things that are useful to a buyer.

**The seller has to hand over the paperwork.** Under Texas Occupations Code § 1958.154, a licensed remediator must give the owner a certificate of mold remediation within 10 days of finishing the work. If the owner later sells, they must give the buyer a copy of every such certificate issued for the property in the five years before the sale. If you're buying a Texas house with any history of mold, ask for these by name.

**The certificate also protects your insurance.** Texas Insurance Code § 544.303 says an insurer may not make an underwriting decision based on previous mold damage or a mold claim if the property was remediated, as shown by a certificate establishing with reasonable certainty that the underlying cause was fixed. That rule also applies if an independent assessor or adjuster later inspected the property and found no evidence of mold damage. That section doesn't apply to the state's wind pool, its FAIR Plan, or surplus lines insurers.

**Check who did the work.** The same Texas chapter, § 1958.155, bars one license holder from both assessing and remediating mold on the same project. A certificate signed off by the company that also did the cleanup is worth questioning.

Outside Texas, there may be no certificate to ask for. The questions still apply: what caused the mold, who fixed the cause, and is there paperwork showing it's gone?
