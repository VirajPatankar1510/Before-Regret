# Buying a House With Unpermitted Work: What You Take On

Start with a question most buyers skip: did this work need a permit in the first place? Some of what shows "no permit on file" never required one, and the rest sorts into problems of very different sizes. Settle that first, then decide who fixes what before you close.

## Did it need a permit at all?

Most US towns build their rules on the International Residential Code, adopting an edition and amending it locally. Section R105.1 of the 2021 edition requires an owner to get a permit before building, enlarging, altering or changing the occupancy of a house, or before installing or altering its electrical, gas, mechanical or plumbing systems. Section R105.2 then lists what is exempt. We read both lines and sorted the common cases:

| What you see in the house | Permit under the model code? |
|---|---|
| New paint, flooring, tile, cabinets or countertops | No, it's finish work |
| A fence up to 7 feet high | No |
| A retaining wall up to 4 feet, not holding back extra load above it | No |
| A one-story detached shed or similar up to 200 square feet | No |
| A deck up to 200 square feet, no more than 30 inches above the ground, not attached to the house and not at the required exit door | No |
| A fixed leak or cleared clog | No, but replacing a concealed drain or supply pipe counts as new work and needs one |
| An addition, an attached deck, a finished basement, a garage turned into a room, new circuits, moved plumbing, a new furnace | Yes |

Two cautions. The exemption list says plainly that skipping the permit doesn't excuse work that breaks the code. And your city may use a different edition or have changed the list, so the building department's answer for that address is the one that counts.

## What passes to you at closing

Under the model code it is unlawful to *occupy* a building that violates it, not only to build one (R113.1). The building official can serve a notice of violation on the person responsible for the building's occupancy and order the violation fixed (R113.2). Once you own the house and live in it, that person is you. The seller who did the work is out of that picture, and whatever you can recover from them is a separate question for state law.

Fixing it usually means an after-the-fact permit. Section R108.6 says anyone who starts permit-required work without a permit owes a fee set by the local authority, on top of the normal permit fees. The inspector can only approve what they can see, so ask early what they'd need exposed. The steps are laid out in [how to legalize an unpermitted deck](/guides/legalize-unpermitted-deck/), and they apply well beyond decks.

An open permit is a different problem from no permit. Under R105.5 a permit becomes invalid if more than 180 days pass between inspections, so a remodel permitted years ago and never finished may need a fresh permit before anyone can sign it off. Checking that history is covered in [looking up a house's permit history by address](/guides/look-up-building-permits-by-address/).

## How the lender's appraiser treats it

For loans sold to Fannie Mae, the Selling Guide (B4-1.3-05) is specific: if the appraiser finds an addition without the required permit, they must comment on the quality and appearance of the work and its impact, if any, on market value. That is not an automatic refusal. It does mean the unpermitted space is judged on its own merits, and if the appraiser decides it adds less value than you are paying for, the appraisal can come in below the price. We haven't checked FHA or VA rules for this page.

## What the seller has to tell you, and when they don't

Disclosure rules are state law. We read two states whose forms are printed in the statute itself:

| | Texas, Property Code §5.008 | Washington, RCW 64.06 |
|---|---|---|
| What the form asks | Whether the seller is *aware* of additions, structural changes or repairs made without necessary permits or not to code at the time | Whether there were conversions, additions or remodeling, then whether *all* permits and *all final inspections* were obtained, with a "Don't know" box |
| Sales with no form | 11 kinds, including foreclosures, estate and trust administrators, and never-occupied new homes | Foreclosures, estate personal representatives, bankruptcy trustees, some family transfers, or a buyer who waived it |
| Your exit if it arrives late | Terminate for any reason within seven days of receiving it, if the contract came first | Rescind within three business days of receiving it, unless the contract says otherwise |

Two things follow. "Not aware" and "Don't know" are honest answers from a seller who bought the house with the work already done. And the foreclosure and estate sales that skip the form entirely are exactly where you'll have to find out yourself. Washington's question about final inspections is worth asking in every state: a permit that was pulled but never signed off is the open-permit problem above.

## Settling it before you close

Once you know what you're dealing with, there are three clean outcomes:

- **The seller legalizes it before closing.** Ask for the finaled permit, meaning the record that it passed final inspection, not just a permit number.
- **A credit or lower price,** sized from a real quote, and you handle the permit after closing.
- **You walk away** under your inspection or due-diligence contingency.

To get there, call the building department with the address and ask four things: does this work need a permit, can it be permitted after the fact, what would the inspector need to see, and what is the fee. Then get a contractor's or engineer's price for whatever they say. Ask the insurer quoting your policy, in writing, how it treats the work. While you're on the city's site, [check the address for open code violations](/guides/check-code-violations-property-online/), which follow the property the same way.

## Already closed?

The searches that bring people here include "i bought a house with unpermitted work", and one asking who is responsible if the city makes the new owner take a structure down. Under the model code, notices go to whoever is responsible for occupying the building, which is the owner who lives there now. The after-the-fact permit route above is the same after closing as before it, just without the seller to share the cost. Whether you can recover anything from the seller depends on your state's disclosure law and what they knew, which is a question for a real estate attorney in that state.
