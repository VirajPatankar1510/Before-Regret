# Washington L&I Contractor Lookup: Registration, Bond, Claims

In Washington the check is L&I's [Verify a Contractor, Tradesperson or Business](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/) tool, run by the Department of Labor & Industries. It searches by name, by contractor or tradesperson license number, by workers' comp account, or by the business's WA UBI number. We used it on 13 November 2026 and opened a full record, then read the contractor registration law (RCW chapter 18.27) to see what each part of the record is worth to a homeowner.

## Registered, bonded and insured, but never tested

Washington doesn't license general contractors by exam. It registers them. L&I regulates 63 specialties, and each one needs registration. To register, a contractor files a bond and proof of insurance, and keeps both in force.

That makes the record a financial check rather than a skills check. It can tell you whether there's a bond to claim against and whether customers have sued over the work. Whether the crew is any good at tile or framing is something you still find out from references and past jobs.

## Use the registration number from the bid

Searching by name casts a very wide net. Our two-word business name returned 1018 results. They mixed construction companies with individual electricians, plumber trainees and unrelated businesses from other states.

The filters down the side help:
- "Only construction companies and tradespeople", or construction companies alone;
- Electrical or Plumbing;
- Active license or registration;
- "Any violations?", split into workplace safety or health, and license violations.

The number is quicker. A Washington registration number mixes letters taken from the business name with digits and a star. Copy it exactly from the bid or the Notice to Customer described below.

Unlike some states' lookups, the detail page has its own web address. Save the link along with the bid.

## Six years of lawsuits, debts and violations

![The history lines of a Washington L&I contractor record: Savings, Lawsuits against the bond or savings, L&I Tax debts and License Violations, each reporting none during the previous 6 year period](/images/washington-lni-record-six-year-history.jpg "From a real L&I record we opened on 13 November 2026, cropped to the history lines. Business name, people and numbers left out.")

Below the bond and insurance, four short lines carry most of the useful history. Each looks back over the previous 6 years:
- **Savings:** a savings account deposited in place of a bond.
- **Lawsuits against the bond or savings:** customers, workers or suppliers who sued to get paid from the bond. This is the closest thing on the record to a complaint history.
- **L&I tax debts:** the record itself adds that "some debts may be recorded by other agencies," so a clean line here covers L&I only.
- **License violations:** citations under the registration law.

Further down are workplace safety and health inspections, listed by date and job site, and the workers' comp accounts. If the business will send employees to your house, L&I's own prompt applies: check that the business is up to date on workers' comp premiums.

The registration block near the top shows the status. On an active record, it reads "Meets current requirements" under Active. It also lists the specialties, with a "What can they do?" link that explains each one. A specialty contractor is registered for its trade, not for whole-house remodels.

## The bond line can show two amounts

The current bond is $30,000 for a general contractor and $15,000 for a specialty contractor (RCW 18.27.040). If the bond is canceled, the registration is suspended automatically. Insurance works the same way, with minimums of $50,000 for property damage, $100,000 for injury to one person and $200,000 for more than one.

Don't be thrown if the record shows a smaller bond. The record we opened listed two, both marked "Until Canceled": $12,000 received in 2002 and $30,000 received in 2025. The amounts went up in the law, and a contractor registered as of June 30, 2024 stays compliant until its next renewal. What matters is that the record says Active and shows a current bond.

## How the bond is shared when money runs short

The bond belongs to every customer, worker, supplier and tax office with a claim against the contractor, not to you alone. If the lawsuits add up to more than the bond, the statute pays them in this order:

1. the contractor's employees and laborers;
2. breach-of-contract claims, which is where a homeowner's claim sits;
3. subcontractors and suppliers;
4. state taxes;
5. court costs, interest and attorneys' fees.

There is one protection for homeowners: claimants other than residential homeowners can take no more than half the bond. A homeowner suing on the bond has two years from when the work was substantially completed or abandoned. The lawsuit is filed in superior court, and the papers are served on L&I.

## The homeowner recovery program (from July 2026)

From July 1, 2026, Washington added a second layer (RCW 18.27.410). If you win a final judgment against a registered contractor over work on your primary residence, go against the bond first, and part of the judgment still goes unpaid, you can apply to L&I. The application is due within 90 days after the court case ends.

Its limits matter:
- It pays actual damages only, up to $25,000 per contractor per parcel, or the unpaid amount if that's less.
- It pays no attorneys' fees or court costs.
- Applications are paid in the order L&I receives them. If the account runs short, there's a waitlist.
- The law calls the payments discretionary, so qualifying doesn't mean you'll be paid.

It's new, and we haven't seen it pay a claim. The law also only reaches registered contractors, which is one more reason to check the record before signing.

## The Notice to Customer you should be handed

For work on a house (four or fewer units) where the bid or contract is $1,000 or more, the contractor must give you a signed disclosure before starting (RCW 18.27.114). It names the registration number, the bond amount and the expiration date, so you can check it against the Verify record.

The notice is unusually frank. It says, in capitals, "THIS BOND OR DEPOSIT MIGHT NOT BE SUFFICIENT TO COVER A CLAIM THAT MIGHT ARISE FROM THE WORK DONE UNDER YOUR CONTRACT." It warns that your property may be liened if a supplier or subcontractor isn't paid. And it tells you that you can ask for original lien release documents from each supplier or subcontractor, and can hold back a set percentage of the price as retainage.

A contractor who skipped the notice can't bring a lien claim on that contract. An unregistered contractor can't sue in Washington's courts to get paid for work that needed registration.

## A flipped house, and the $500 line

**Flips.** Owners working on their own property don't have to register, but the exemption ends when the work is done "for the purpose of selling, demolishing, or leasing the property." Someone who improves property they've owned for less than twelve months in order to sell or lease it isn't exempt either. If you're buying a recently renovated house, the person who did the work should show up in the Verify tool.

**Small jobs.** Work under $500 in total on one project counts as casual or minor and doesn't need registration. The exemption doesn't apply to part of a larger job, or to a bigger job split into several contracts under $500.
