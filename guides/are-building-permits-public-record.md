# Are Building Permits Public Record? What You Can See

A building permit record usually shows more than people expect: the permit number and type, what the work was, the dates it was applied for, issued and finished, and often the contractor, the parcel number and the inspections. On the City of San Diego's OpenDSD, a 2012 plumbing permit at City Hall listed the application, issuance, first inspection and completion dates, the scope of work and the parcel number, all with no sign-in. What you can't always get is a copy of the plans, and for older houses the record may only exist on paper.

## What the public records laws say

Building permits are public because the office that issues them is a public agency, and each state has its own public records law. We read two of them.

**California.** Government Code § 7922.525 says public records "are open to inspection at all times during the office hours of a state or local agency and every person has a right to inspect any public record, except as otherwise provided." Section 7922.525 also requires the agency to release the parts of a record that aren't exempt, after deleting the parts that are.

**Florida.** Florida Statutes § 119.01 is broader still: "all state, county, and municipal records are open for personal inspection and copying by any person."

California's law gives the right to every person, and Florida's to any person, not only to the owner of the property. In practice a buyer, a neighbor or a contractor can look up the same permit.

California's deadline is often misread. Under Government Code § 7922.535, an agency has 10 days to decide whether your request covers records it can release and to give you an estimated date. The 10 days is for that answer, not for the records themselves, and in unusual circumstances the agency can extend it by up to 14 days with written notice.

Permit searches work on any address, including the house next door. If you want to know whether a neighbor's project has a permit, look up their address the way you'd look up your own. ## Certificates of occupancy, inspections and applications

A certificate of occupancy is a building department record too, and some cities file it with the permits. At Dallas City Hall the oldest record in the city's permit search was a certificate of occupancy, dated 09/12/2019, sitting in the same list as the permits.

Inspection results are public in many permit searches, but don't expect every permit to carry a full log. Pinellas County showed a deck permit's red tag in 2008 and its later approval. Phoenix showed an electrical permit's failed and passed final inspections from 1996. Yet another Phoenix permit, and a San Diego permit with a recorded completion date, both showed an empty inspection list. A permit search shows inspections where the office loaded them, so read the issue and completion dates as well.

Applications that never became permits can be public as well, depending on the system. Phoenix's search hides temporary and unissued permits until you switch a setting on, and San Diego's list mixes issued, completed, cancelled and abandoned records. An application on a house's record that was cancelled or never issued is worth a question to the seller, because the work may have gone ahead anyway. [Finding unpermitted work before you buy](/guides/find-unpermitted-work-before-buying/) covers what to look for.

## Building plans and site plans are the exception

Plans are where public access narrows, and California's rule is the clearest example. Health and Safety Code § 19851 keeps the official set of plans at the building department: anyone can view it there, but it "may not be duplicated in whole or in part except (1) with the written permission" of the professional who signed the plans and of the owner, original or current. For a buyer the current owner is the seller, so ask for the signature during escrow, before closing ends the seller's reason to help.

Florida's public records law has a building-plans exemption too, but its wording is narrower than people assume. Florida Statutes § 119.071(3)(b) exempts plans of a "building, arena, stadium, water treatment facility, or other structure owned or operated by an agency." A private house isn't owned or operated by an agency, so that clause isn't the one that would keep a homeowner's plans back. Ask the county or city what it holds for the address before assuming either way.

## Older permits are public, but often on paper

Online permit records only reach back so far. In our searches the oldest online record ranged from 1980 in Austin to 2015 in Tampa. Records from before that are still public. They just take a visit or a request.

San Diego shows how the paper years work in one big city. Its building department holds permits from before 2003 only in paper form, open for anyone to look through and copy at $0.25 a page, and it has no permit records older than 1955 at all. Keep a records request to documents. A public records process is for getting records, and San Diego's own page warns it won't be used to answer questions, such as whether a particular job was permitted.

We haven't read the public records law of every state, so our answer stops at generally, yes. Most states follow the same principle, but the exemptions differ, and the office that holds the permit is the one that decides.
