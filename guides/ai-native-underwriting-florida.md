# AI Underwriting and the 60-Day Window Florida Buyers Still Have

On 8 September 2026, a Florida reciprocal insurer called Orange Insurance Exchange announced it had gone live on Equinox, an AI-native policy administration platform built by Solstice Innovations. Three products opened for new business on 1 September: HO-3 for homeowners, HO-6 for condo owners, and DP-3 for dwelling fire. Commercial lines follow in early 2027.

That is a vendor announcement about a mid-sized carrier in one state. It matters to buyers for a narrower reason than the phrase "AI-native" suggests, and it is worth being precise about which parts of the buying process it changes and which it does not.

## What the Platform Actually Does

Equinox runs rating, issuance, endorsements, renewals, claims, billing, and forms on one system, with what Solstice describes as AI embedded in the workflows rather than layered on top. Standing up three products at once instead of staggering them across release cycles is the capability being advertised.

The consumer-facing piece sits at quote. Per the announcement, the platform validates and enriches property data at the moment a quote is generated, which cuts agent data entry and improves submission accuracy. In underwriting, it prioritizes referrals by risk signal and classifies inspections, loss runs, and correspondence.

Two sentences in the release deserve more attention than the headline.

The first: every capability operates within Orange's underwriting guidelines, with human oversight maintained for binding decisions. The second is from Solstice CEO Travis Pine, describing AI "supporting the underwriter instead of replacing them." Orange CEO Don Matz frames the benefit as configurability — his underwriting team responding to the book without waiting on a development calendar.

This is automation of administrative review, with the judgment call left in place. It is a real efficiency gain and it is not the removal of humans from underwriting. Any account of this announcement claiming a file gets decided before a human sees it is describing something the companies specifically said they did not build.

## The Window Is Statutory, Not Technological

There is a persistent claim circulating that AI-native underwriting has killed the traditional 30-day grace period during which a new policy gets reviewed by a person.

Two things are wrong with it.

There is no 30-day period. In Florida the relevant provision is § 627.4133, and the number is 60 days. During the first 60 days after coverage takes effect, an insurer retains broad cancellation rights on a new policy. After 60 days, cancellation narrows to specific grounds: material misstatement, nonpayment, failure to comply with underwriting requirements the insurer established within those first 60 days, or a substantial change in the risk. Written notice of 20 days is generally required.

And it is not dead, because no software vendor can end it. That window exists in statute. A carrier changing its policy administration platform does not alter what the Florida Legislature wrote, and Orange's announcement makes no claim that it does.

What compresses is the quote, not the buyer's protection. Faster quoting is genuinely useful when you are trying to bind coverage before a closing date.

## What Does Change For a Buyer

The meaningful shift is upstream of the underwriter, and it has been underway for years.

When a platform enriches property data at quote, it is pulling third-party data about the address before anyone at the carrier examines the file — construction characteristics, prior loss history, regional hazard exposure, roof condition scored from aerial imagery. The house is evaluated against data the buyer has never seen and often does not know exists.

The consequence is not that a buyer loses a review period. It is that a buyer can be surprised by the first quote. A property that seems unremarkable during a showing can come back rated on a data profile the buyer had no way to anticipate, and in Florida the variables that drive that profile are well known: roof age and shape, windstorm exposure by county, the construction cohort the house belongs to, and prior claims attached to the address.

None of that is secret. It is simply not visible from the curb.

## The Pressure Behind It

Context, because the timing is not a coincidence. The analytics firm Cotality projects homeowner premiums rising roughly 8% in 2026 and another 8% in 2027, about 16% across two years, with insurance now consuming around 9% of the typical American homeowner's monthly housing payment.

One caution about connecting that to this announcement. Rising premiums are frequently paired with carriers withdrawing from markets, and Orange is doing the opposite here — launching three products into Florida, which is added capacity in a state that has been short of it. Automated data enrichment is used both to price risk more tightly and to write business a carrier would otherwise decline. Treating every efficiency gain as a mechanism for dropping policyholders reads a single vendor deal as a trend it does not establish.

## What To Do Before You Bind

Get a real quote before your contingency deadline, from a carrier that has run the address. This is the whole game. A quote is the only test that returns a number, and it surfaces the data profile before you are committed rather than after.

Ask what drove the price. Agents can usually see which factors the submission flagged. Roof age is the most common, and it is the one most often fixable with documentation the seller already has.

Get the roof's paper trail from the seller: the invoice, the warranty registration, the closed permit for the tear-off where the jurisdiction required one. A verified installation date beats an estimated one, and estimates are what automated enrichment produces in the absence of records. If the seller cannot produce it, the permit record is public — see our guide to [looking up building permits by address](/guides/look-up-building-permits-by-address/).

Understand what the first 60 days mean if you are buying in Florida. The policy binding at closing is not final in the sense buyers assume. A carrier can act on what it finds during that period, including on an inspection ordered after binding. That is not new, and it is not a consequence of any platform, but it catches people who believed the coverage question was settled at the closing table.

Know the roof-scoring problem. Carriers assess roofs from aerial imagery, and regulators in a dozen states have documented adverse actions taken on images that were outdated, obstructed, or of the wrong building.
