# Insurer Profits Tripled. Rate Increases Nearly Stopped. Coverage Still Got Harder.

The U.S. property and casualty industry posted an estimated net underwriting gain of $31.7 billion in the first half of 2026, against $11.6 billion in the same period a year earlier. Net income after taxes reached $77.8 billion, up 53% from $50.9 billion. Policyholders' surplus climbed to $1.3 trillion from $1.13 trillion at midyear 2025.

Those figures come from Verisk and the American Property Casualty Insurance Association, published 8 September 2026. The combined ratio improved to 92.7 from 96.5, which the organizations describe as one of the strongest half-year underwriting performances in recent history.

The obvious reading is that carriers are extracting record profits while squeezing homeowners. The data does not support that reading, and the version it does support is more useful to anyone buying a house this year.

## Where the Money Came From

Start with the comparison year. The first half of 2025 absorbed the Los Angeles wildfires, and the Verisk/APCIA report attributes the weak 2025 result directly to those catastrophe losses. Measuring 2026 against a wildfire year overstates the improvement.

Incurred losses and loss adjustment expenses fell 4.8% in the first half of 2026, against a 5.1% increase a year earlier. Lighter catastrophes, not harder pricing, moved the underwriting number.

The net income figure is doing something else again. Net investment gains rose to $59.6 billion from $49.0 billion. A large share of that $77.8 billion is the return on invested float in a high-rate environment — a bond portfolio outcome, not a premium outcome.

And the $31.7 billion belongs to the whole industry: personal auto, commercial, liability, homeowners. It is not what homeowners carriers made. The same report describes casualty lines as continuing to deteriorate, with excess and umbrella liability, commercial auto and others under pressure from claim severity, nuclear verdicts, and medical cost inflation.

Verisk's own Saurabh Khemka cautions against reading the result as a sign that underlying risk has eased, pointing to roughly $171 billion in average annual modeled insured catastrophe losses globally, with about $117 billion of that in the United States.

## Premiums Are Decelerating, Not Accelerating

This is the part that most contradicts the popular account.

Net written premium growth fell to 2.1% in the first half of 2026, down from 5.2% a year earlier and a recent peak of 10.8% in the first half of 2024. APCIA's Robert Gordon says premium increases have moderated to below general inflation and below building materials and labor costs. Property lines softened broadly.

Separate research from S&P Global Market Intelligence puts numbers on the homeowners line specifically. The U.S. effective approved homeowners rate change ran about 13.6% in 2024, about 6.3% in 2025, and about 1.8% through July 2026.

Rate increases have not intensified. They have very nearly stopped.

In Florida, Georgia and Louisiana — states APCIA credits with legal system reforms — Gordon says many policyholders have begun seeing reductions in auto and homeowners rates, expected to deliver hundreds of millions of dollars in premium relief.

## The Fragmented Phase

S&P GMI's term for what replaced the national rate reset is a fragmented phase, and it is the most practically important idea in either report.

The national slowdown is an average, and the average conceals opposite movements. Minnesota ran about 17.8% in 2025 and about 1.6% so far in 2026. Colorado went from about 16.6% to about 0.8%. North Carolina, meanwhile, has seen increases. S&P GMI's reading is that some states and peril classes have absorbed enough rate to catch up while others still sit behind adequacy, producing stabilization in the headline number alongside continued pressure where loss patterns have not normalized.

Their conclusion is worth quoting on its own terms: the 2026 cycle belongs to a fragmented, state-by-state answer set built on localized catastrophe experience, regulatory timing, underwriting appetite, and earned performance rather than on a uniform national resolution.

For a buyer, that means the national headline tells you nothing about your address. Two houses in different states, or in different peril zones of the same state, are on different trajectories.

## Where the Tightening Went

Here is the mechanism that matters, and S&P GMI names it directly. The decline in headline rate change reflects the byproduct of broad-based non-rate actions: higher deductibles, rate tiering based on property age and condition, and better risk selection.

Read that again with a buyer's eye. When a carrier stops raising the posted rate but shifts to selecting which properties it writes and on what terms, the pressure has not disappeared. It has moved from a number on a renewal notice to a decision about whether your house qualifies, and at what deductible.

That change is invisible in rate filings and highly visible at the moment you try to bind coverage on a specific property.

Two of the three named levers are about the house itself rather than the market. Property age and condition tiering means the construction cohort a house belongs to, and its current state, now drive placement more than they did when carriers were simply pricing everything upward. Better risk selection means the same thing from the other direction.

Increasingly from aerial imagery, which is a documented weak point — regulators in more than a dozen states have issued bulletins after finding adverse actions taken on images that were outdated, obstructed, or of the wrong building. ## What This Means If You Are Buying

The affordability problem has not been solved. The Verisk/APCIA report says affordability remains a challenge as construction costs and claim severity continue to affect rebuilding costs. What has changed is the form the problem takes.

A buyer in 2026 is less likely to be shocked by a rate increase and more likely to be shocked by a deductible, a condition requirement, or a decline. Three consequences follow.

Get a real quote on the specific address before your contingency expires. National averages are now actively misleading, because the fragmentation is the whole story. A quote is the only test that returns a number for your house.

Read the deductible, not only the premium. When carriers take non-rate actions, the deductible is the first lever. A wind or hail deductible expressed as a percentage of dwelling coverage can be many multiples of a flat all-perils figure, and it does not show up in a premium comparison.

Treat roof age and condition as a coverage question, not a maintenance question. Age-and-condition tiering is one of the three levers S&P names. Get the seller's roof invoice, the warranty registration, and the closed permit for any tear-off — the documents, not the recollection.
