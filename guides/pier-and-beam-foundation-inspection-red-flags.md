# Pier and Beam Foundation Inspection: Red Flags for Buyers

A pier-and-beam house sits on posts and beams over a crawl space instead of on a slab. That's good news for an inspection: the structure is open to view in a way a slab never is. The catch is that someone has to go down there and look. So before you read any red flag in the report, find the line that says where the inspector stood.

## Read the vantage-point line first

Texas writes this into its rules. The state's standards of practice for inspectors require the report to state "the vantage point from which the crawl space was inspected". *Entered and traversed* and *viewed from the access opening* are both honest answers. They don't describe the same inspection.

Both Texas and Washington, the two state rules we read in full, let an inspector stay out of a tight crawl space. In Texas, that's one with less than 18 inches of headroom, or an opening smaller than 24 inches wide and 18 inches high. Washington's version is a little stricter about what counts as tight. It covers headroom under 18 inches beneath the joists or 12 inches beneath the girders, an opening smaller than 18 by 24 inches, and any crawl space that would need digging out. Washington inspectors also don't have to enter a flooded one, and the report has to say why an area was left out.

None of this is cutting corners. It's what the rules allow. But it changes what you've paid for. Before you book, ask the inspector whether they enter crawl spaces, and ask the seller's side how high it is under there. Our own report raises this exact point for older pier-and-beam houses in the Texas counties we track. Its advice is to have someone physically enter the crawlspace, not look in from the hatch, and report on moisture, rot and the piers.

If the report says the crawl space wasn't entered, you haven't had a foundation inspection yet. You've had a look at the floors upstairs.

## Red flags you can see from upstairs

The Texas rule lists signs an inspector can point to when giving an opinion that a foundation isn't performing well. You can look for most of them on a showing:

- doors that bind, sit out of square or won't latch;
- sloping floors;
- separations in the framing or at the frieze board, the trim where the wall meets the roofline;
- masonry cladding that is cracking, buckling or leaning.

Any one of these has other explanations. A door can stick because of humidity, and a floor can slope because of how it was framed decades ago. Together, or in one part of the house, they're the reason to make sure someone goes underneath.

## Red flags under the floor

Washington's rule says what an inspector who does go in should report: the condition of posts, piers, beams, joists and subfloor; signs of flooding and moisture; wood rot; and conditions that invite pests. Texas lists the same parts, adding sills, and adds ventilation or drainage that isn't working. The building code gives you numbers to hold those findings against.

![Cross-section of a pier-and-beam crawl space with the 2021 IRC clearances and the red flags to look for. Floor joists 18 inches or more above the soil and girders 12 inches or more (IRC R317.1); access opening at least 18 by 24 inches through the floor (IRC R408.4); a vent within 3 feet of each corner (IRC R408.2); a vapor retarder covering the soil. Red flags: standing water, wood rot at the sill, wood too close to the soil, missing vapor retarder, debris and wood scraps left on the ground](/images/pier-and-beam-crawl-space-clearances-red-flags.webp "Drawn by Before Regret from the 2021 International Residential Code, sections R317.1 and R408.")

- **Wood close to the soil.** IRC R317.1 requires decay-resistant or treated wood where joists are closer than 18 inches to the ground, girders closer than 12 inches, and wooden posts closer than 8 inches. Ordinary framing that close is a moisture and rot question to put to the inspector.
- **Water.** Standing water, mud lines on the piers, or a damp, musty crawl space. IRC R408.6 says that where groundwater can rise to within 6 inches of the floor at the perimeter, the crawl space floor should be as high as the ground outside, unless there's an approved drainage system. A crawl space dug lower than the yard collects water.
- **Rot at sills and joist ends.** Washington's rule tells inspectors to probe wood where they suspect deterioration. Soft wood at the sill, where the frame sits on the perimeter wall, is the classic spot.
- **No ground cover.** Washington inspectors must report missing or inadequate vapor barriers on an earth floor. Bare soil puts moisture into the space all year.
- **Debris.** IRC R408.5 wants vegetation, organic material and leftover wood forms cleared out. Wood scraps on the soil are food and shelter for pests.
- **Pest signs.** In Washington, a home inspector can report pest-conducive conditions but can't name the insect without a separate structural pest licence. Treat any suspected insect damage as a reason for a [wood-destroying insect report](/guides/wdo-report-who-supposed-order/), not a verdict.

One honest caveat about those code numbers. They're the 2021 rules for new houses. An older house wasn't built to them, so a shortfall isn't automatically a code violation. It's a yardstick for asking the right question.

Posts or piers that look newer than the rest usually mean someone has already done work down there. Ask the seller for the records. Our guide to [buying a house that's already had foundation repair](/guides/should-buy-house-s-already-had-foundation-repair/) covers what those records should show.

## Vented or sealed: check which one it is

Crawl spaces come in two kinds, and the code treats them differently. A seller may advertise an *encapsulated* crawl space. That's a selling point only if the whole system is there.

A **vented** crawl space under IRC R408.2 needs at least 1 square foot of vent opening for every 150 square feet of floor, with a vent within 3 feet of each corner. A plastic ground cover lets that drop to 1 square foot per 1,500. We worked it for a 1,500 square foot crawl space: 10 square feet of vent without a ground cover, or 1 square foot with one.

A **sealed** crawl space under IRC R408.3 needs more than plastic on the ground. The ground cover has to be continuous, with seams overlapped 6 inches and taped, running at least 6 inches up the wall and sealed there. Then the space needs one of these:

- an exhaust fan running continuously at 1 cubic foot per minute for every 50 square feet;
- conditioned air from the house at the same rate;
- a dehumidifier sized to the maker's specifications.

For the same 1,500 square feet, that's a 30 cubic-foot-per-minute fan running all the time.

The common half-done version is a sealed-up crawl space with the vents closed and plastic down, but no fan, no supply air and no dehumidifier. Ask the seller which of the three it has. Then ask the inspector to confirm it's running.

It raises the crawl space check for houses built through 1965 in 13 Texas counties on our expansive-clay list, where pier-and-beam was common. If the inspection finds movement, a [structural engineer](/guides/when-foundation-crack-need-structural-engineer/) is the next call. An engineer has no repair to sell you, and a written opinion is what you'll want if you go back to the seller.
