# Does Home Insurance Cover Your Water or Sewer Line?

Before the insurance question comes a question many owners get wrong: whose pipe is it? In each of the cities whose rules we read, the city owns the main under the street and the owner is responsible for almost everything between that main and the house, in some cities including the connection to the main itself. When that pipe cracks or fills with roots, the repair is yours to arrange and pay for.

## Who owns which part of the line

We read what four city utilities say about it:

| City | Water side | Sewer side |
|---|---|---|
| Philadelphia (Water Department, March 2026) | Once water leaves the City-owned main, it's the customer's: the ferrule (the valve on the main), the service pipe, the curb stop and its box, and the supply pipe into the house. The meter belongs to the Water Department, but the owner must keep it accessible and protected | The owner alone is responsible for the main house drain, the curb trap, the lateral to the public sewer, and the "slant", the connection to the sewer itself |
| San Diego | not covered by the sewer fact sheet we read | "The entire length of the sewer lateral," from the house to the connection with the City main under the street, "is your private property" |
| Torrance, California | not covered by the policy page we read | The owner maintains and repairs the lateral, including "the connection to the public sewer main" |
| Orem, Utah | The city says homeowners "are responsible for their lateral sewer and water line repairs" and that many residents had wrongly believed the city was | (same) |

Two details from those pages are worth knowing.

In Philadelphia, owners whose lines aren't working can be served a Notice of Defect, which requires a repair within a set period. In San Diego, if you lose all sewer service because of a blockage or break in the part of the lateral under the street, the City may fix it at no charge, but only after your plumber files a report. And not if the problem turns out to be on your side of the property line, or was caused by roots or grease that weren't cleared. The City says it will "under no circumstances dig on private property."

Your city's rule may differ. Search for your water or sewer utility's name plus "lateral" or "service line responsibility" to find it.

## Why the standard policy usually doesn't pay

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners, the body of state insurance regulators, puts it plainly in its consumer guide. A homeowners policy "isn't a maintenance contract", it insures against perils like fire, wind and hail, and it "doesn't pay to repair items that simply wear out." A clay sewer lateral that has cracked with age, or a cast iron line that has corroded through, is the kind of slow failure that falls on the owner.

The NAIC also notes that most homeowners policies offer limited or no coverage for sewer or drain backups, and that you may be able to buy an endorsement for that. A backup endorsement is about sewage coming *into* the house. It's a different thing from fixing the pipe in the yard, so check which one a policy actually offers.

## What a service line add-on can include

Some insurers sell an add-on, usually called service line, buried utility line or underground utility line coverage, that pays to repair the buried lines themselves. Terms vary by insurer and state. To show what one looks like in practice, we read one insurer's published FAQ for its endorsement (dated July 2018). It:

- **covered** failure of a buried line from freezing, wear and tear, deterioration, collapse, tree roots, digging and animals;
- **included** water, sewer and drainage lines, and also buried electrical, phone, internet and heating lines;
- **did not cover** a line that was only blocked ("there is no direct damage"), sewage or drain backup, flood, earth movement, damage from the house's foundation shifting, or cleanup of sewage;
- **paid** up to a stated limit, with the policy's own deductible applying.

That blockage exclusion matters more than it sounds. San Diego's fact sheet says drainage problems are commonly caused by an obstruction or blockage in the lateral, and clearing roots or grease is the owner's maintenance. A plumber clearing a blockage is a maintenance call, not a covered break.

If you're offered one, ask for the endorsement form and check five things:
1. Which lines it covers: water, sewer, or both.
2. Which causes it covers, especially wear and tear and tree roots.
3. Whether digging up and restoring the yard, driveway or sidewalk is included.
4. The limit and the deductible.
5. When it starts: ask whether it can be added mid-policy or only at renewal.

## What's at stake if the line fails

These are the ranges our free report uses for a sewer line, from the same rules that generate it:

| | Range in our report |
|---|---|
| Camera scope of the sewer line | $300 – $500 |
| Spot repair | $1,500 – $5,000 |
| Full replacement | $4,000 – $15,000+ |

If you're buying, the camera scope is the cheapest way to know which of those you're facing before you own it.
