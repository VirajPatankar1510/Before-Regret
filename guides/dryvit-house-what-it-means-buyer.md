# Listing Says Dryvit? What It Means for a Buyer

A listing or inspection report may never use the word "EIFS." It may say "Dryvit" instead.

Dryvit is a manufacturer. Its own site calls it "the manufacturer of Outsulation® systems" and says it "brought the Exterior Insulation and Finish Systems industry to North America in 1969." So the name points toward EIFS, a layer of foam with a thin coating on top that people often call synthetic stucco. Pointing toward it isn't proof, and the maker is only the first of several things a buyer needs to know.

## What the name can and can't tell you

**It names a maker, not a wall.** Dryvit's site lists more than a dozen Outsulation EIFS systems. It also sells StucCoat, which it describes as "complete code-compliant stucco assemblies" applied "in a single- or multiple-coat system," and notes that StucCoat can "even be used as a stucco patch." A house finished with Dryvit products could have any of these on it.

**The word on a listing is a description, not a product label.** The agent or inspector may have read the brand off old paperwork, or may simply be describing how the walls look. Before you rely on it, [check the wall type with your own hands](/guides/eifs-vs-stucco-how-to-tell/).

**It says nothing about condition.** A brand can't tell you whether the sheathing behind one particular wall is dry.

## The system type is the real question

Under today's model code, the 2021 IRC (Sections R703.9.1 and R703.9.2), the barrier type, the kind with no built-in drainage, is meant only for concrete or block walls. On wood framing the code calls for a drainage version with a water barrier behind the foam. Dryvit's own FAQ draws the same distinction. One of its questions reads "How Do Rainscreen Systems compare to Barrier EIFS for Moisture Management?"

For homes from the 1990s and early 2000s, our report engine flags the risk that older barrier-type walls held water against the sheathing, out of sight. That is a question about the type of system and how it was installed, whoever made it.

So the useful questions are not "is it Dryvit?" but these: which system, put on when, and is the wall dry behind it?

## Four steps before you commit

1. **Settle what the walls are.** A tap and a firm press tell you a lot ([the hands-on checks](/guides/eifs-vs-stucco-how-to-tell/)).
2. **Get the paperwork.** Request the original builder's documents, any re-cladding invoices and any earlier moisture report from the seller. A document naming the exact system and installer tells you more than the brand name ever will.
3. **Book the moisture survey early.** Our report engine puts it at **$400 – $800 for a moisture survey by an EIFS-qualified inspector**. Use the questions in [picking an EIFS specialist](/guides/how-to-choose-eifs-stucco-inspector/) when you call around.
4. **Decide on the readings, not the label.** [What the survey results mean for your offer](/guides/buy-house-with-eifs-stucco/) covers proceeding, asking for a repair or credit, and walking away.
