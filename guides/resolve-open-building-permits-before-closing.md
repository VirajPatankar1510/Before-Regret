# Resolving Open Building Permits Before Closing

An open permit is usually the easier of the two permit problems a house can have. Someone applied, the work was approved, and the file just never got its final inspection. Work with no permit at all is a different matter, covered in our guide to [buying a house with unpermitted work](/guides/find-unpermitted-work-before-buying/). This page is about getting an open or expired permit closed before you close, and what happens if it isn't.

One search that brings people to our Miami-Dade guide reads "how to check for open permits on a home in coral gables". If you haven't found the record yet, start with [how to look up a house's permits by address](/guides/look-up-building-permits-by-address/), then come back here with the permit number and its status.

## Read the status carefully

Until a permit is marked final or closed, the building department hasn't finished with it. Expired doesn't mean closed: in Portland, for example, a permit expires after 180 days without an inspection approval, with the work still unsigned.

Watch for one trap. Portland's building bureau warns that its "Cancelled" status "has also historically been used to indicate an Expired or Void permit." A cancelled permit can look settled when it isn't. If the record isn't marked final or closed, ask the department what it means.

## Who can close it before you buy

Until the deed records, the seller is the owner, and building departments deal with the owner and the contractor on the permit. Austin's reactivation form has to be signed by the permit holder or the property owner "as listed in the appropriate county appraisal district's registry." Before closing, that's the seller, not you.

So the practical answer is to make it the seller's job, in writing, as part of your inspection response or contract terms: the permit closed, with the record showing it finaled, before closing.

Florida makes this easier than most places. Under Florida Statutes § 553.79(16), any owner can close a permit, even one they didn't apply for:

- by bringing back the original contractor;
- by hiring a different licensed contractor, who is liable only for their own work, not the original contractor's defects;
- or by acting as owner-builder.

The Florida statute also has two shortcuts. An expired permit whose work is substantially complete can be closed without a new permit, under the code that applied when the permit was first requested. And six years after a permit was issued, the department may close it without a final inspection if it finds no apparent safety hazards. Neither happens on its own, so ask the department whether either applies.

## If it's still open after you buy

This is where places differ most. We read the rules in three:

| | Florida (state law) | Austin, Texas | Portland, Oregon |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who answers for an old open permit | Not you, if you bought at arm's length for value: the department can't deny you a permit, cite you or fine you solely because a previous owner's permit wasn't closed | You: "The property owner is responsible for any expired permits on the property even if they did not own the property at the time the permit was issued" | Not stated in the guide we read |
| When a permit lapses | Not set in § 553.79 | Not stated on the page we read | After 180 days with no inspection approval |
| Reviving it | Owner can close it with the original contractor, a new one, or as owner-builder | Owner or listed contractor requests reactivation, for a fee | Within 180 days of expiring, for the original scope only |
| If it's too late | Can be closed without a new permit if the work is substantially complete | Not stated on the page we read | Void: resubmit, designed to current code, with fees at current rates |
| Sources | Fla. Stat. § 553.79(16)–(17) | City of Austin, Modify a Permit | Portland BDS program guide, revised March 15, 2019 |

Two things follow. In Austin, an open permit you inherit can also hold up your own projects: the city says an expired permit "may need to be resolved before issuing any new permits." And in Portland, waiting can turn a final-inspection visit into a new permit under today's code.

Florida's protection is narrower than it sounds. It stops penalties against you; it doesn't inspect the work. The department keeps its remedies against the previous owner and contractor, but the house you own still has work nobody signed off.

## Why the tax card doesn't settle it

A common check is to compare the assessor's record with the house. It's worth doing, because it shows what the county thinks is there. But it isn't proof that anything was permitted. As one township assessor in New Jersey puts it, "Not getting a permit for work done in your home does not prevent an assessment." A finished basement on the tax card tells you the county knows about the basement, nothing more.

## What title insurance does and doesn't cover

The 2021 ALTA Homeowner's Policy of Title Insurance, written for one-to-four family homes owned by individuals, excludes building and zoning laws in general (Exclusion 1.a), then carves one piece back in. Covered Risk 18 applies when you're ordered to remove or remedy part of the house "because any portion of Your existing structures was built without obtaining a building permit."

Read the limits before relying on it:

- **It has a deductible and a cap.** Covered Risk 18 is subject to "Your Deductible Amount and Our Maximum Dollar Limit of Liability shown in Schedule A", and each insurer sets those amounts. Ask for them.
- **What you knew is excluded.** Exclusion 3.b removes matters "Known to You and not disclosed in writing to Us by You prior to the date You became an Insured." If your inspection or permit search turned something up, disclose it to the title company in writing before closing.
- **Bad work isn't covered.** Exclusion 5 excludes the failure of the structures to have been built to the building code.
- **An open permit isn't named.** Covered Risk 18 speaks of work built *without* a permit. An open permit was obtained; it just wasn't finaled. The policy doesn't say whether that counts, so ask the title company in writing.

Title insurance is a backstop for a narrow risk, and getting the permit closed is the real fix.

## The order that works

1. Get the permit number, its status and its last inspection from the building department's records.
2. Ask the department what's left: a final inspection only, or more; and whether an expired permit can still be revived where the house is.
3. Put it in writing with the seller: the permit closed and finaled before closing. If that can't happen before your deadline, ask your agent or attorney about a price credit or a holdback, and get the department's answer about what's left before you agree to a number.
4. Before closing, give the title company anything you know, in writing, and ask which coverage your policy includes.
