# Burst Pipe? What to Do Right Now, and What Insurance Covers

A burst pipe keeps pouring until someone closes the valve that feeds the house. Everything else, from the plumber and the insurer to the wet carpet, can wait ten minutes. The valve can't. Here's where the main shutoff valve usually is and how to close it, then what to do about the electrics, the drying and the claim.

## Close the main shutoff valve

The main shutoff valve on a house is usually where the water supply pipe comes in, on the house side of the meter. Charlotte Water, the city utility in Charlotte, North Carolina, gives a useful rule of thumb by house type:

- **Basement or crawl space:** the main shutoff is probably in the basement, near the front wall, the one closest to the street.
- **Built on a slab:** look near the water heater or in the garage.

Closing the valve depends on its type. Turn a gate valve, the round wheel, clockwise. Turn a ball valve's lever until it sits across the pipe rather than along it. If you're reading this before anything has gone wrong, go and find the valve now and make sure it turns.

## Keep away from wet electrics

Water from a burst pipe can reach outlets, extension cords and appliances on the floor. FEMA's Ready.gov flood guidance applies to a burst pipe just as well: "Do not touch electrical equipment if it is wet or if you are standing in water." Ready.gov also says to turn off the electricity "if it is safe to do so". If reaching the breaker panel means walking through water, it isn't safe, so leave it to an electrician.

## Start drying within a day or two

Water from a burst pipe starts a drying clock the moment it lands. The EPA's advice is to dry water-damaged areas and items within 24-48 hours, and says that if they're dried in that window, mold won't grow in most cases. The EPA also lists where mold hides after a leak: the back side of drywall, the top of ceiling tiles, and the underside of carpets and pads. Those are the places a quick mop misses.

Whether mold that does grow is covered is a separate question, with its own answer. Our guide to [whether homeowners insurance covers mold](/guides/homeowners-insurance-cover-mold/) sorts that out.

## Will homeowners insurance pay for a burst pipe?

Homeowners insurance generally covers burst-pipe water damage when two things are true: the water came out suddenly, and the home was being looked after. The District of Columbia's insurance regulator says coverage usually exists where the policy covers damage from a "sudden and accidental" discharge from a plumbing source or system. The NAIC, the association of state insurance regulators, uses the same phrase.

Heat is the condition attached to burst-pipe coverage. DC's regulator says a policy usually requires that heat be maintained in the property, and some require a certain temperature even when the house is empty. The NAIC adds that if the insurer decides you neglected your plumbing system, the damage "may not be covered". For a vacant house with no heat, DC's advice is to shut off the water supply and drain the system and appliances.

Burst pipes are not a rare claim. Massachusetts' Division of Insurance reported that in 2024, 48.6% of homeowners' insurance claims in the state were for non-flood water losses and freezing-related damage.

Leaving the house for a few days in winter, when pipes are most likely to freeze, has its own checklist, including how insurers treat a home left unoccupied. It's in our [guide to leaving home over Thanksgiving](/guides/leaving-home-thanksgiving-checklist/), which also covers photographing the damage and keeping receipts for the claim.

## Why a burst pipe isn't a flood

A burst pipe floods a room, but it isn't a "flood" in insurance terms. The federal Standard Flood Insurance Policy (44 CFR Part 61, Appendix A(1)) defines a flood as a general and temporary condition covering "two or more acres of normally dry land area or of two or more properties (one of which is your property)", from rising water, surface runoff or mudflow. Water from a pipe inside one house meets none of that, so a flood policy isn't the one to call. The claim goes to your homeowners insurer.

## What to ask your insurer, in writing

Your own policy's wording, not a phone summary, decides a burst-pipe claim. We couldn't read the standard policy form for this page, so these are the questions to put to your own insurer:

1. Is the water damage covered as a sudden and accidental discharge, and what's the deductible?
2. Does the policy pay to open and repair the wall, floor or ceiling to reach the pipe, and does it pay for the pipe itself?
3. Is there a heating or occupancy condition, and does anything about this loss trigger it?
4. Do you want to inspect before permanent repairs start?

## If you've just bought the house

A pipe that bursts soon after you move in tells you something about the rest of the plumbing. Our report engine flags galvanized steel supply pipes for houses built up to 1969, and polybutylene supply pipes for houses built from 1978 to 1996. For either material, the engine puts a whole-home repipe at $4,000 – $15,000.

Ask the plumber what the burst pipe was made of, and whether the rest of the supply lines match. Our guide on [how to tell PEX, copper and galvanized pipes apart](/guides/identify-water-pipes-pex-copper-galvanized/) shows what to look for. If the answer is polybutylene, read [how to spot polybutylene pipes](/guides/spot-polybutylene-pipes-before-buying-house/) and [what it means for your insurance](/guides/polybutylene-pipes-home-insurance/). A leak in the pipe between the street and the house is a different question: [who covers the water and sewer service line](/guides/home-insurance-water-sewer-service-line/).
