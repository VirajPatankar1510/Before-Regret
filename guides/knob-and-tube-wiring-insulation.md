# Can You Insulate Over Knob and Tube? What the Codes Allow

This comes up two ways for a buyer. Either the attic of an old house already has insulation lying over the porcelain knobs, or you'd like to add insulation once you move in. In both cases the question is the same: who has signed off on the wiring under it?

## The base rule, in California's words

California's electrical code says concealed knob and tube "shall not be used in: Hollow spaces of walls, ceilings, and attics where such spaces are insulated by loose, rolled, or foamed-in-place insulating material that envelops the conductors." That's section 394.12(5). Berkeley prints it on page 2 of its knob-and-tube form, which the city last revised on 25 March 2026.

A national code doesn't bind anyone on its own, though. A 1988 Department of Energy memo to the weatherization program put it plainly: the National Electrical Code and its changes "do not apply until adopted by State and/or local electrical code authorities". The same memo named Massachusetts, Nebraska and Washington as states that had chosen modified approaches allowing insulation "when certain inspection and safety procedures are followed". That's why the answer depends on where the house is.

## Two states that allow it, and what each demands

| | California (exception to 394.12(5)) | Washington (WAC 296-46B-394) |
|---|---|---|
| Who checks the wiring | An electrical contractor licensed by the State of California | An appropriately licensed electrical contractor |
| What they certify | Good condition, no deterioration, proper overcurrent protection, no improper connections or splices | Good condition, no improper overcurrent protection, no insulation failure, no improper connections or splices |
| Where the paper goes | Filed with the local building authority, with a copy to the owner | In writing to the state department |
| Insulation allowed | Noncombustible, with no conductive material in or supporting it | Class I, flame spread of 25 or less; foam not allowed |
| Overcurrent protection | Must be proper | Breakers or Type S fuses, sized to the 60 degree column |
| On site | Caution notices in English and Spanish at every accessible area | No posting requirement in the rule |

Washington's version took effect on 1 April 2024. Cities can add steps on top. Menlo Park, for one, wants its Knob and Tube Certification Form submitted and approved before any insulation goes in, whether it's new insulation or a replacement.

## What sizing to the 60 degree column means

Both states tie the fuses or breakers to the wire size, so that an old circuit can't be loaded past what its wire can carry. In the 60 degree column that Washington points to, 14-gauge copper is good for 15 amps and 12-gauge for 20 amps.

That's why the weatherization forms below ask about every circuit's fuse or breaker before anyone approves insulation.

## What the electrician's sign-off looks like

Snohomish County PUD in Washington publishes the survey its electricians fill in before the utility will insulate a house. It asks three things outright: are the conductors adequate, are there any improper connections, and are the circuits overloaded. Then it lists each circuit with its fuse or breaker, amperage and wire gauge.

The part worth noticing is at the bottom. The electrician marks which areas are approved for insulating (walls, ceilings, floors or all areas) with initials, and the form says "Check marks are unacceptable". A survey can clear the attic and not the walls, so read it for what it actually approves.

## When nobody certifies it: dams, not blankets

Weatherization programs had to decide what to do with live wiring that no electrician had signed off. Iowa's 2015 program notice is one of the most specific answers we found:
- **In the attic,** the wiring is isolated rather than covered. A dam of unfaced fiberglass batts at least 14 inches wide, equivalent to R-38, keeps the blown insulation away from the wire path.
- **Above the joists,** a batt at least 6 inches wide goes under the wiring too.
- **In the walls,** the answer is simpler: walls with active, uncertified knob and tube "will not be insulated".

That notice dates from 2015 and may have been updated since. What it shows is the shape of a careful job: certified wiring can be covered, and uncertified wiring is kept clear of insulation.

## If the house you're buying is already insulated

Look at the attic hatch. In California, where insulation was installed around certified knob and tube, the caution notice in English and Spanish should be posted at every accessible area. Berkeley's version reads "WARNING: Knob and Tube Wiring Use Caution When Entering Attic". A sign means a certification should exist. Ask the seller for the copy, since the owner is meant to receive one.

No sign and no paperwork, with insulation over live wiring, is a different situation. Your inspector can tell you the wiring type, but not whether it's live. That needs an electrician: our guide to [telling active knob and tube from abandoned](/guides/is-knob-and-tube-wiring-still-live/) covers what they check. If the answer changes your plans, the [replacement costs and permit fees](/guides/knob-and-tube-removal-cost-permits/) are worked through separately.

We didn't read the current National Electrical Code text itself. The section numbers and wording above are California's and Washington's versions. Texas's weatherization guidance on knob and tube blocked our access, so it isn't covered here. Your state may follow the base rule, an exception like these, or something else, so ask your local building department before insulating.
