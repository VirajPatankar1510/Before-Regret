# Option Period Strategies: Negotiating Home Repairs

A long inspection report reads like a list of problems. Most of it is a list of notes. What you do in the next few days is pick the few items worth asking for, and pick the form of the ask, because a repair, a seller credit and a lower price reach you in very different ways.

In Texas this window is called the option period. Elsewhere it's usually an inspection contingency or due diligence period. How many days you have, what you paid for it and what happens when it ends are all set by your own contract, so read those lines before anything else. If you're deciding whether to leave the deal instead, that's a different question, covered in [when it's reasonable to walk away after an inspection](/guides/when-reasonable-walk-away-after-inspection/).

## Book the separate checks on day one

A general inspection leaves several common checks out unless you ask. Our report engine flags these as separate bookings:

- **Sewer scope.** Our engine notes that "most general home inspections do not include it unless you ask." For a house old enough to have a cast iron line, it's the one look inside the pipe you'll get.
- **Termite (WDI) report.** Also "most general home inspections do not include one unless you ask."
- **Level 2 chimney inspection.** For a house with a fireplace or wood stove, a separate engagement from the general inspection.

These take scheduling, and the window is short, so book them the day your contract is signed rather than after the general report arrives.

## Sort the report into three piles

A short request is harder to refuse than a long one, so sort before you ask:

1. **Safety and insurability.** Wiring the inspector calls hazardous, an old panel brand, active leaks, anything your insurance agent says could affect coverage. These are worth asking for even if they're cheap.
2. **Expensive systems near the end of their life.** Roof, HVAC, sewer line, foundation movement. These are where the money is.
3. **Maintenance and wear.** Caulking, a loose handrail, a dirty filter. Fix these yourself after closing. Leaving them off the request makes the first two piles harder to refuse.

## Repair, credit or lower price

This is the decision the rest of the negotiation turns on.

| | Seller repairs it | Seller credit | Lower price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who controls the work | The seller picks the contractor | You, after closing | You, after closing |
| When the money reaches you | No money; the work is done before closing | At closing, as a reduction in your closing costs | Through a smaller loan and down payment |
| Limits | Only what the written terms say | On a conventional loan: capped, and no more than your actual closing costs | No lender cap |
| Gives you cash for the repair | No | Indirectly: it frees cash you'd have spent on closing | No, but you borrow less |

The common advice is to always ask for a credit. It's often right, and here's the catch. Under Fannie Mae's rules for conventional loans, a seller credit can go toward "Borrower closing costs, including prepaids", not to you as cash, and it "must be equal to or less than the sum of the borrower's closing costs." On top of that, there's a cap based on how much you're putting down:

| Your loan-to-value (principal residence or second home) | Maximum seller credit |
|---|---|
| Greater than 90% | 3% |
| 75.01% – 90% | 6% |
| 75% or less | 9% |

Anything above the cap is treated as a concession that's deducted from the sales price. FHA, VA and other loan programs set their own limits, so ask your lender for yours before you name a number.

## Where the credit runs out

Put the cap next to real repair costs. On a $300,000 house bought with 5% down, the 3% cap allows a credit of up to $9,000, and only as much as your actual closing costs. With 20% down, the cap rises to 6%, or $18,000.

Now the ranges our report engine uses:

| Finding | Typical cost in our engine |
|---|---|
| Sewer line | Spot repair $1,500 – $5,000; full replacement $4,000 – $15,000+ |
| Roof | Full replacement $8,000 – $25,000+ |
| HVAC | Replacement $6,000 – $15,000 |
| Electrical panel | Replacement $1,500 – $4,000 |

A panel or a sewer spot repair fits comfortably inside a credit. A roof often doesn't, because even a credit within the cap can't exceed what you'd pay in closing costs. For the big items, a lower price is usually the cleaner ask: it has no lender cap, and it lowers what you borrow for the rest of the loan. For the smaller ones, a credit leaves you choosing the contractor and the timing.

Whatever you agree, make sure it's written into the contract and appears on the settlement statement. Fannie Mae counts money given to the buyer "outside of closing" and left off the settlement statement as an undisclosed contribution, which can make the loan ineligible.

## When a seller repair is the better ask

Some sellers won't move on price or credits but will fix things. A repair can work if the terms are specific:

- name the work, not the symptom (*replace the water heater*, not *address the water heater*);
- require a licensed contractor where your state requires one, plus permits where needed;
- ask for invoices and any transferable warranties before closing;
- check the work at your final walkthrough.

Vague repair terms are where repair negotiations go wrong. And once the seller signs, there are still ways a deal can unravel; see [whether a seller can back out after your inspection response](/guides/seller-back-out-after-accepting-inspection-response/).
