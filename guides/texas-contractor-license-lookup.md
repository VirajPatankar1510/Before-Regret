# TDLR License Search: Checking a Texas Contractor

Texas checks the trades, not the builder. The state's [TDLR License Data Search](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/LicenseSearch/) covers electricians, air conditioning contractors and a long list of other trades. Plumbers are on a separate board's search. Neither will tell you anything about the general contractor or remodeler running the job, because the state doesn't license them. We used both searches on 14 November 2026. Here's what they show, and where you have to look instead.

## Who Texas licenses, and who it doesn't

TDLR's search lists every license type it covers. It includes:
- Electricians;
- Air Conditioning Contractors;
- Mold Remediation Contractor;
- Solar Residential Retailer and Solar Residential Salesperson;
- Water Well Drillers.

It has no general contractor or home builder type. That gap has a history. Texas once had a state agency for home builders, the Texas Residential Construction Commission. The Sunset Advisory Commission records that it "was abolished and the Act expired on September 1, 2009", with its records sent to the Comptroller.

So for a kitchen remodel, the people you can check at state level are the electrician and the A/C contractor (both TDLR) and the plumber (the plumbing board). The company coordinating them may have nothing to look up.

## The search only shows active licenses

The search page says it covers "Active Licenses only". A license that has expired, lapsed or been revoked doesn't come up as expired. It simply doesn't come up. If a contractor gives you a number and it returns nothing, that's a reason to ask questions, not proof of a typo. TDLR prints its customer service number under every search for exactly this case.

Two more quirks from our search:
- **Name searches match the start of the name.** The results page shows what it did: "Before wildcards: [MISTER SPARKY]After sanitize: [MISTER SPARKY%]". So a short name finds every business whose name starts with it.
- **One brand can hold several licenses.** One franchise name returned 8 separate Electrical Contractor records, one for each location. Match the license number on your bid, not just the name on the truck.

## The record: a business, and the master electrician behind it

![A TDLR electrical contractor record showing license type EC, status Active, expiration and original issue dates, with a Master line naming the master electrician and that person's own license number and expiration; names and numbers blanked out](/images/texas-tdlr-electrical-contractor-record.jpg "Part of a real TDLR record we opened on 14 November 2026. Names, numbers and the phone were blanked out in our browser before the screenshot was taken.")

An electrical contractor's record shows:
- the license number, expiration date and status;
- the date the license was first issued;
- the DBA, the name the business trades under;
- a **Master** line naming the master electrician the contractor license runs under, with that person's own license number and expiration date.

That Master line is worth a second search. The company's license depends on that person, so check that their license is active too. Electricians come in several grades in the search (Master Electrician, Journeyman Electrician, Residential Wireman, Electrical Contractor and others), and the contractor license is the one that lets a business sell you the job.

## Discipline is in a different search

The record shows no complaints, no bond and no insurance. Disciplinary orders live in TDLR's separate Administrative Orders search. That search "covers Orders issued during the current and most recent past two fiscal years", and TDLR's fiscal year runs from 1 September to 31 August. When we looked, the earliest order it held was dated 1 August 2024, so older discipline won't show there.

## Plumbers are on another site

Plumbing licenses come from the Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners. Its Public License Search runs on a shared state platform and searches by name, license number, city or county. The law it enforces is plain: no one may do plumbing without the proper license and licensed supervision (Occupations Code § 1301.351).

## When the owner can do the work

- **Electrical:** Occupations Code § 1305.151 requires a license, but § 1305.003(a)(6) exempts work "performed in or on a dwelling by a person who owns and resides in the dwelling". That only holds where no city ordinance specifically regulates the work. TDLR's exemptions page repeats the warning that local rules may override.
- **Plumbing:** an owner may do plumbing "in the property owner's homestead" (§ 1301.051). Some rural properties are exempt too, but not for plumbing done as part of "new construction, repair, or remodeling" (§ 1301.052).

For a buyer, this cuts both ways. A seller may have legally done their own wiring or plumbing, and a state license search won't show it. A permit search is where that history turns up.

## Your city may register the builder

Cities fill some of the gap. State law lets them require inspections and issue their own licenses (§ 1305.201). Austin is one example. Before any construction under a permit, "trade contractors, general contractors, and homeowners acting as contractors must register" with its Building and Trade Contractor Services. A general contractor's name on an Austin permit means the city has a registration on file. Check your city's building department for its own version.

## A contract rule that outlived its agency

Texas Property Code § 41.007 still governs home improvement contracts on a homestead. It requires this warning, in at least 10-point bold:

> "IMPORTANT NOTICE: You and your contractor are responsible for meeting the terms and conditions of this contract. If you sign this contract and you fail to meet the terms and conditions of this contract, you may lose your legal ownership rights in your home. KNOW YOUR RIGHTS AND DUTIES UNDER THE LAW."

Leaving it out is a deceptive practice under the state's consumer protection act. An arbitration clause in such a contract also has to be in 10-point bold, or it isn't enforceable against the owner.

The same section still asks for the contractor's registration number from the Texas Residential Construction Commission, the agency that closed in 2009. That clause only applies "if the contractor is required to register", and no one has been since. If a contract cites a registration number, ask what it's for.

## How we checked this

On 14 November 2026 we ran TDLR's License Data Search for an electrical contractor by business name, using a large franchise brand we don't name. We opened one record, and the screenshot comes from it. We also read:
- TDLR's exemptions page and its Administrative Orders search page;
- the plumbing board's site and its search form;
- the Texas Occupations Code, chapters 1301, 1302 and 1305;
- Property Code § 41.007;
- the Sunset Advisory Commission's record on the Texas Residential Construction Commission;
- Austin's contractor registration page.

We didn't run a plumbing license search, and the plumbing board's complaint page blocked our access. We only read one city's rules; Houston, Dallas and San Antonio set their own.
