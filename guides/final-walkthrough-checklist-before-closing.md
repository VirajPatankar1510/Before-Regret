# Final Walkthrough Checklist: What to Do Before Closing

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's advice is short: "Before you sign any papers, do a final walk-through of the home," and "make sure everything that you and the seller agreed would be repaired has been repaired." The useful question is what else you're entitled to find when you walk in. The answer is in your purchase contract, not in a generic checklist.

To show what that looks like in practice, we read Texas's standard resale contract line by line: TREC No. 20-19, the form the Texas Real Estate Commission publishes, in its 4 May 2026 edition. Two things surprised us. The word "walk-through" doesn't appear anywhere in its 12 pages. The buyer's chance to look comes from paragraph 7A, which says the seller "shall permit Buyer and Buyer's agents access to the Property at reasonable times." And the contract is far more specific about what the seller must hand over than most checklists are.

Your state's contract will be worded differently, so bring your own and match each item to it.

## The checklist, matched to the contract

| Check at the walk-through | What the Texas contract says (TREC 20-19) |
|---|---|
| Agreed repairs are done, and you have the paperwork | Paragraph 7F: repairs done before closing, by licensed people where a license is required, with any required permits. The seller must give you "documentation from the repair person(s) showing the scope of work and payment" and transfer any transferable warranties |
| Lights, water, heating and cooling all run | Paragraph 7A: the seller must turn the utilities on and keep them on while the contract is in effect, so you can test everything |
| Nothing has changed since you last saw it | Paragraph 10A: possession is delivered "in its present or required condition, ordinary wear and tear excepted" |
| Fixtures stayed with the house | Paragraph 2B lists what conveys: appliances, ceiling fans, light fixtures, mailboxes, TV mounts and brackets, the heating and AC units, security and fire detection equipment, the water softener, garage door openers and landscaping |
| Keys, remotes and controls are there | Paragraph 2C covers door and mailbox keys, blinds and curtain rods, the stove, pool equipment, and the controls for garage doors and entry gates, including the seller's transferable rights to the apps that run them |
| Anything the seller kept is actually gone | Paragraph 2D: excluded items "must be removed prior to delivery of possession" |
| Smart devices are handed over | Paragraph 10B: the seller gives you, in writing, the access codes, usernames, passwords and apps for smart devices, and removes them from the seller's own phones and computers |

## Walking through the house

Go room by room, and test rather than look:

- **Repairs from your inspection.** Stand in front of each one with the repair list. If an electrician fixed an outlet, test it. If a plumber fixed a leak, run the water and look underneath. Then match the repair to the receipt. The paperwork is how you'll know who to call if it fails.
- **Every system once.** Run the heat and the AC, a few minutes each. Run water at every sink and shower, flush every toilet, and check under the sinks afterwards. Run the dishwasher and the garbage disposal briefly. Open and close the garage door with the remote.
- **Lights and outlets.** Flip each switch. A cheap plug-in outlet tester shows quickly whether outlets are working. If your inspection report flagged something like an [open ground](/guides/open-ground-mean-electrical-inspection/), check whether it was fixed or just left as it was.
- **New damage from moving out.** Look at walls, floors and door frames where furniture went out. Check the ceilings for stains that weren't there before, especially after a storm.
- **The outside.** Gutters still attached, no new damage to fences or the garage door, and the yard not used as a dumping ground for things the seller didn't want.

## If something is wrong

Don't sign until it's settled in writing. In the Texas contract, your options depend on what went wrong:

- **Agreed repairs not finished:** paragraph 7F lets you extend the closing date by up to 5 days for the seller to finish, or use the default remedies in paragraph 15.
- **Damage after the contract was signed** (fire or other casualty): under paragraph 14, the seller must restore the property by closing. If they can't for reasons outside their control, you can end the contract with your earnest money back, extend by up to 15 days, or take the house as it is, with the seller's insurance payout assigned to you (if their insurance company allows it) and a credit for the deductible.
- **Smaller problems** are often settled with a repair before closing or a credit at closing, which your agents write up as an amendment.

If you and the seller agree a credit, your lender will issue a corrected Closing Disclosure. Federal rules (12 CFR 1026.19(f)(1)(ii)) require you to receive the original at least three business days before closing. Under 12 CFR 1026.19(f)(2), a correction usually just has to reach you at or before closing. Only three kinds of change restart the three-business-day wait: the APR becomes inaccurate, the loan product changes, or a prepayment penalty is added. A seller credit isn't on that list by itself, but ask your lender whether the new figures change your APR.

If the problem is big, and you're unsure whether it's worth closing over, our guide to [when it's reasonable to walk away](/guides/when-reasonable-walk-away-after-inspection/) helps you weigh it. So does our guide to [whether a seller can back out after accepting your inspection response](/guides/seller-back-out-after-accepting-inspection-response/), if the seller is the one hesitating.

## What to bring

- Your purchase contract and every amendment, including the repair agreement.
- Your inspection report, so you can check items off.
- The seller's repair receipts and warranties, or a note of which ones you're still owed.
- A phone charger and an outlet tester. Take photos of anything you raise, with the time on them.

Book the walk-through as close to closing as you can, ideally after the seller has moved out, so you see the house as you'll get it.
