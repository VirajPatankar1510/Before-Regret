# How to Spot Polybutylene Pipes Before Buying a House

Start with the year the house was built. Our report engine flags any house built from 1978 through 1996 for a polybutylene check, because that's the stretch when the gray plastic supply pipe went into American homes. Outside those years it's unlikely. Inside them, somebody needs to go and look, and the looking takes less time than most buyers expect.

## How many homes fall in that window

We ran the numbers on the Census Bureau's latest county housing counts (American Community Survey, 2020-2024, table B25034). Census reports year built by decade, so the window can only be bracketed:

- **18.5 million homes, 12.9% of the 143.8 million in the 50 states and DC, were built in the 1980s**, a decade that sits wholly inside the window.
- **39.4%** were built from 1970 through 1999, the three decades the window touches. That's a ceiling, not an estimate.

Where the 1980s share runs highest, among counties with at least 50,000 homes:

| County | Homes built in the 1980s | Share of all homes |
|---|---|---|
| Hernando County, Florida | 27,331 | 29.5% |
| Highlands County, Florida | 16,685 | 28.5% |
| Worcester County, Maryland | 16,121 | 28.3% |
| Martin County, Florida | 23,127 | 27.8% |
| Virginia Beach, Virginia | 52,321 | 27.4% |
| Palm Beach County, Florida | 189,546 | 26.3% |

Seven of the ten highest-share counties on our list are in Florida. By raw count the order changes: Los Angeles County has the most homes from the 1980s (436,870), then Maricopa County, Arizona (318,055) and Harris County, Texas (292,528).

Read these numbers for what they are. Year built says when a house went up, not what's in its walls now; a mid-1980s house may have been plumbed in copper, or repiped since. The Census Bureau also warns that year built is "more susceptible to errors of response and non-reporting" than most of its questions, because people answer from memory. The table tells you how often the question comes up where you're buying. Only the pipes answer it for one house.

## Four places to look during a showing

You can't open walls at a showing, but supply pipe comes into the open in a few predictable spots. A phone flashlight is enough.

1. **The water heater.** Follow the hot and cold lines up from the top of the tank. The first stretch is often a metal flex connector; what you want to see is the pipe that connector joins, at the wall or ceiling.
2. **The main shutoff.** Find where the water line comes into the house, often in a garage, basement or utility closet, or low on an outside wall. Look at the pipe on both sides of the valve.
3. **Under the sinks.** Kitchen, every bathroom vanity, the laundry. Look at the pipe leaving the wall or floor and going into each small shutoff valve.
4. **The basement or crawl space ceiling.** Where the basement is unfinished, supply runs hang along the floor joists in plain view. It's the best look you'll get at the house's actual plumbing.

## What it looks like up close

- **It bends.** Polybutylene runs in gentle curves. Copper and CPVC are rigid and turn corners with fittings.
- **It's usually gray**, sometimes blue or black. Blue is also a common PEX colour, so a blue pipe needs a second clue.
- **The joints have crimp rings**: metal bands squeezed around the pipe over an insert fitting. Look at the insert too. Some are plastic, and those fittings failed as well as the pipe (more on that below).
- **There's often printing along the pipe.** Read it. Polybutylene pipe was made to ASTM D3309, and that specification covers "methods of marking," so original pipe was meant to carry a printed line. If it names polybutylene, PB or D3309, you have your answer. Faded or missing print proves nothing either way.

If you can't tell what you're looking at, our [guide to telling PEX, copper and galvanized pipe apart](/guides/identify-water-pipes-pex-copper-galvanized/) covers the look-alikes.

## Copper under the sink doesn't clear the house

This is where buyers get caught. When a vanity is replaced or a leak is fixed, the plumber often swaps only the short piece that pokes out of the wall, the stub-out, and joins it to whatever is behind the drywall. From the cabinet you see clean copper or PEX. A few inches in, it can still be the original pipe.

A home inspection won't necessarily catch it, and that's by design rather than carelessness. Texas spells it out more plainly than most states. Its rules have inspectors report the "visible material used for water supply lines" (22 TAC §535.231), and its general standard calls the inspection "a limited visual survey" that doesn't cover anything "buried, hidden, latent, or concealed" or use destructive testing (§535.227). Other states word their standards differently, but a visual inspection reports what it can see. A report that says "copper" because copper is what showed is accurate, and it still might not be the whole house.

So ask your inspector a narrower question: where exactly did you see each material? Copper noted at the water heater, the main shutoff and an exposed basement run tells you far more than copper seen under two sinks.

## When to call a plumber, and what to ask for

If the house falls inside the window and the pipe you can see is mixed, or all you saw were stub-outs, a plumber's visit before your inspection period ends is worth the fee. Ask them to:

- open the access panels behind tubs and showers, where original valve connections tend to survive a bathroom remodel;
- trace at least one line from a stub-out back toward the main, where a ceiling or crawl space allows it;
- write down the material found at each location, rather than "plumbing appears updated."

If the seller says the house was repiped, ask for the invoice, and ask whether it covered the whole house or one bathroom. "The pipes were done" isn't something you can show an insurer.

## Why it fails where you can't see it

Polybutylene rarely warns you from the outside. Researchers at Jana Laboratories and the University of Illinois at Chicago, in a paper on chlorine testing of PEX, write that "the early failure of polybutylene (PB) piping systems has been attributed to accelerated degradation due to chlorine in potable water," and that failures were reported in both the pipe and the acetal fittings used with it. The chlorine is in the water, inside the pipe. A clean, flexible exterior says little about the wall underneath.

It's also not what a plumber would put back. We read the water distribution pipe table in the 2021 International Residential Code (Table P2906.5). It lists CPVC, copper, PEX, PE-RT, polypropylene, galvanized and stainless steel, and several composite pipes. Polybutylene isn't on it, so where a city uses that code, a repair is done in a different material.

## If you find it

Our report engine puts a whole-home repipe at $4,000 – $15,000; a plumber's quote on the actual house replaces that range. Before your inspection or option period ends, get two things in writing: that repipe quote, and an insurance agent's answer on this address. The insurance side is usually the harder one, and our [guide to insuring a house with polybutylene](/guides/polybutylene-pipes-home-insurance/) goes through what underwriting weighs and how to ask for a real answer.
