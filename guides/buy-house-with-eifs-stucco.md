# Should You Buy a House With EIFS? What to Check First

It can be a reasonable buy. What makes it reasonable isn't the cladding itself but two things:
- **What a moisture survey finds behind it.**
- **When you get that answer.** It needs to arrive while you can still renegotiate or walk away.

A buyer who orders the survey in the first days of the inspection period has options. A buyer who learns about the walls after closing has a repair bill.

EIFS, sometimes sold as synthetic stucco, puts a thin coating over foam board. Our report engine flags it for a reason. It was used on some homes built in the 1990s and early 2000s. Early barrier-type installations could trap water behind the cladding, causing sheathing and framing damage that isn't visible from outside.

## Why your general inspection won't settle it

A general home inspection is built around what can be seen. Texas's rules for home inspectors are a clear example. 22 Tex. Admin. Code § 535.227 calls the inspection "a limited visual survey and basic performance evaluation." It says the inspection is "not intended to be a comprehensive investigation or exploratory probe," and does not require the inspector to use moisture meters. It also excuses "any method employing destructive testing that damages otherwise sound materials or finishes." Your state's rules will be worded differently, so ask your inspector what theirs cover.

Checking EIFS properly means probing through the finish, which is exactly what those rules leave out. So a clean general report on an EIFS house tells you about the surface, not about the sheathing behind it.

## Check these first, in this order

**1. Make sure it really is EIFS.** A listing may just say "stucco." A few hands-on checks point one way or the other: tap the wall, press it, and look at the cracks and the bottom edge. [Telling EIFS from traditional stucco](/guides/eifs-vs-stucco-how-to-tell/) walks through each one and its limits. If it turns out to be hard-coat stucco, the rest of this page doesn't apply.

**2. Find out when the walls went on.** Which type of EIFS is on the house matters more than anything visible. Today's model code (2021 IRC Sections R703.9.1 and R703.9.2) confines the barrier type to concrete and masonry and requires a drainage type, with a water-resistive barrier behind it, on wood-framed walls. A house clad before your town adopted that rule could have barrier EIFS over wood. Nobody can tell which from the street, so the build year and the date of any re-cladding are your first clue. The [EIFS-or-stucco guide](/guides/eifs-vs-stucco-how-to-tell/) has the code details.

**3. Ask the seller for paper.** Request any earlier EIFS inspection or moisture report, invoices for repairs or re-cladding, and any warranty that came with that work. An old moisture report is useful history, but it isn't a substitute for a current one.

**4. Book the moisture survey inside your inspection period.** The figure in our report engine is **$400 – $800 for a moisture survey by an EIFS-qualified inspector**. Probe testing drills small holes through the finish into the sheathing and seals them afterwards, so get the seller's permission in writing before the appointment. [What an EIFS stucco evaluation covers](/guides/standard-home-inspection-check-eifs-stucco-moisture/) explains the scans and probe readings, and how to read the results.

**5. Call your insurer before the deadline.** Ask the company you plan to insure with whether the cladding type affects your policy, and whether they want an inspection report. Get the answer in writing. It costs nothing to ask now, and the answer can change your math.

## Reading the result

**Dry readings and firm sheathing.** You can keep going. Keep the report with your closing papers. Ask the inspector which joints and sealants need watching, since EIFS depends on them.

**High readings in a few spots.** Water is likely finding its way in at particular places, and the report should say where: a window corner, say, or where a roof or deck meets the wall. Before your deadline, get a written repair scope and a quote from a qualified contractor. That document is what lets you ask the seller to repair, or to give you a credit.

**Wet or soft sheathing over large sections.** In the engine's words, repairs vary widely, and extensive sheathing replacement can exceed $10,000. Unless you have a contractor's written scope and a price that covers it, walking away is a reasonable answer.

## When walking away is the sensible call

- **The seller won't allow probe testing.** Without it, nobody can tell you what's behind the foam.
- **You can't get a survey before your deadline,** and the seller won't extend it.
- **The survey finds soft sheathing,** and you can't get a written repair scope in time.

Each is a timing problem as much as a house problem. That's why the order above matters: the earlier you book the survey, the fewer of these you'll face.
