# Is Your Knob and Tube Still Live? Active vs. Abandoned

Knob and tube in the attic can mean three different houses. In one, the old wiring was disconnected decades ago and left in place. In another, a few circuits still run on it while the rest of the house was rewired. In the third, it's still feeding the house. The inspection report often can't tell you which, and the difference is the whole question.

## What the inspector will and won't tell you

Home inspection rules are set state by state. Washington's are written into its administrative code (WAC 308-408C-110), and they show the usual split well.

The inspector must:
- describe "the type(s) of branch wiring used";
- report conductors "known to be potentially hazardous" and refer them to a licensed electrical contractor;
- check the operation, grounding and polarity of a representative number of switches and outlets in every room.

The inspector is not required to:
- put "any tool, probe or testing device into the main or subpanels";
- turn on circuits "that are not energized";
- operate breakers or service disconnects, or remove fuses.

So a report can honestly note knob and tube and refer it to an electrician without knowing whether a single wire of it carries power. That isn't the inspector dodging. It's the standard working as written.

## What an electrician can sign

Berkeley's knob-and-tube form, revised in March 2026, gives the electrician three boxes to choose between:
1. in good condition, "with proper over-current protection and no improper connections or splices";
2. "not in good condition and may not be covered without repair or rewiring";
3. "disconnected and is not live or has been removed".

The third box is the one a buyer hopes for. The form was written for insulation permits, but those three outcomes are exactly what you need to know too. Ask for the answer in that form, on the electrician's letterhead or license, not as a remark in an email.

Utility weatherization programs use the same idea. The form EWEB shares with four other utilities includes the line "All knob and tube wiring has been disconnected from power and is no longer in use", for an electrician to check or leave blank.

## Some knob and tube: the partial house

In a partial house, some circuits were rewired and others weren't. There, a yes-or-no answer isn't enough. You need it circuit by circuit.

Snohomish County PUD's survey shows how that's recorded. For every circuit in the areas being worked on, the electrician lists the circuit number, whether it's on a breaker or an S-type fuse, its amperage and its wire gauge, then signs as a journeyman electrician. A list like that tells you which rooms still depend on the old wiring and whether each circuit is protected at the right size. The EWEB form asks the same protection question: are knob-and-tube circuits on breakers or Type S fuses "of 15 amp capacity or less"?

## Why "live" decides what happens next

Disconnected wiring left in a wall is a different problem from wiring still in use. Iowa's 2015 weatherization rules show how much it matters. Live knob and tube that no electrician has certified "will not be covered or surrounded" by insulation, while certified wiring can be.

Our own reports work the same way. For houses built in 1955 or earlier, the knob-and-tube item asks your inspector "to confirm in writing whether any knob-and-tube is still energized". A dedicated electrician evaluation costs $150 – $350 by our report's figures. EWEB's form warns that electricians' prices vary a lot and suggests comparing before you choose one.

The written answer then feeds the next questions:
- **Insurance:** what the insurer will ask about. See [getting homeowners insurance with knob and tube](/guides/get-homeowners-insurance-knob-tube-wiring/).
- **Insulation:** whether you can add any. See [insulating over knob and tube](/guides/knob-and-tube-wiring-insulation/).
- **Cost:** how big a fix you're pricing. See the [removal cost and permit fees](/guides/knob-and-tube-removal-cost-permits/).

## What to ask for, in writing, before your deadline

- Whether any knob and tube is energized, and if so, which circuits.
- For each live circuit: the breaker or fuse, its amperage and the wire size.
- Any improper connections or splices found.
- The electrician's name, license number and the date of the visit.
