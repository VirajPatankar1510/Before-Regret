# Add-On Wood Furnace Insurance Requirements: What to Show

An add-on wood furnace is the second firebox in the basement: it burns wood and pushes its heat through the ducts of the oil, gas or electric furnace that's already there. No insurer publishes one rulebook for these, and underwriting varies by carrier and state. The good news is that the things an underwriter can ask about are checkable, and most of them are printed on the furnace itself or kept at the building department.

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners' 2022 consumer guide puts it plainly: in its list of household details that can affect what you pay, it includes "Having a wood furnace or wood stove". So expect the question on the application, and have answers ready before the agent asks. A freestanding stove in the living room is a different appliance, tested to a different standard; our [guide to insuring a house with a wood stove](/guides/home-insurance-with-wood-stove/) covers that one.

## What to have ready before you ask for a quote

| What to show | Where you'll find it | Why it carries weight |
|---|---|---|
| A testing-agency mark on the nameplate | The furnace's factory nameplate, next to the model and serial number | It's how anyone can tell the furnace was listed, and listed for what |
| The EPA permanent label | On the furnace, in a spot you can see after installation | Furnaces made on or after May 15, 2015 must carry one; it shows the month and year the unit was made |
| A permit and final inspection for the install | The town or county building department | Shows a code official looked at the clearances, ducts and flue |
| A chimney inspection | A CSIA-certified chimney sweep | The flue is shared risk; our report engine prices a Level 2 inspection at $250 – $600 |
| The owner's manual | The seller, or the maker's website | Federal rules tie how the furnace may be run to its label and manual |

Take clear photos of the wood furnace's nameplate and EPA label on your first visit or at the inspection. They answer most of the questions an agent will send back, and you won't get another easy look before closing.

## UL 391 is the standard written for add-on furnaces

UL 391 is titled the Standard for Solid-Fuel and Combination-Fuel Central and Supplementary Furnaces. Section 1.1 of its scope names add-ons directly: "Included are supplementary central furnaces intended for interconnection with forced-air central furnaces utilizing other fuels". The current edition, Edition 6, was published October 21, 2024.

The listing matters because an add-on joins two systems. The wood unit pushes heat into ductwork, a blower and controls that were built for another furnace. A furnace listed to UL 391 was tested for that arrangement. A wood stove was not: the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) sends solid-fuel room heaters to a different standard, UL 1482, in Section M1410.1.

The IRC also says what the nameplate has to show. Section M1303.1 requires a permanent factory nameplate with the maker's name, the model number, a serial number "and the seal or mark of the testing agency". If you find a maker and model but no testing-agency mark anywhere on the furnace, write that down. It's the single most useful fact to bring to an agent.

## A gap in the residential code that trips people up

Read the 2021 IRC's central furnace section and you'll find standards for oil-fired furnaces (UL 727) and electric furnaces (UL 1995), and no line at all for wood. That doesn't mean wood furnaces are unregulated. Section M1301.1 says installations "not addressed by this code shall comply with the applicable provisions of the International Fuel Gas Code and the International Mechanical Code", and the 2021 International Mechanical Code, Section 918.1, says "Solid fuel furnaces shall be tested in accordance with UL 391."

Two more IRC lines fill in the rest. M1302.1 says appliances must be "listed and labeled for the application in which they are installed and used", and M1402.2 says furnace clearances follow the listing and the maker's instructions. Put together: an unlisted, shop-built firebox has no listing for an inspector to measure clearances against. Your building department decides which code edition applies where the house is, so ask them, not us, how they handled the install.

## Reading the EPA label, and what a missing one means

Federal rules for wood furnaces live in 40 CFR part 60, subpart QQQQ. They cover forced-air furnaces manufactured on or after May 15, 2015 (§ 60.5472). Each one needs a permanent label at least 3.5 inches long and 2 inches wide, placed where it can be seen after installation, showing the month and year of manufacture, the model name and number, the certified emission value and the serial number (§ 60.5478).

The limits tightened in two steps. Furnaces first had to meet 0.93 lb/mmBtu of particulate (2016 for units under 65,000 BTU per hour, 2017 for larger ones). Since May 15, 2020, no forced-air furnace may be made or sold at retail unless it's certified to 0.15 lb/mmBtu (§ 60.5474).

Two rules in § 60.5480 bear on a house you're buying. Nobody may operate a covered furnace that lacks its permanent label, or remove that label. And it must be run as the label and owner's manual say, including burning only the fuels it's certified for.

A missing label isn't automatically a problem. A furnace made before May 15, 2015 was never covered, so it wouldn't have one. A furnace made after that date with no label is a different story. Ask the seller what year it went in, and look for the install date on the permit record.

## What we found when we searched EPA's list

On November 24, 2026 we searched EPA's certified wood heater database for central heaters, using the default filters. With only current models showing, it listed 41 central heaters, and only 6 of them were forced-air furnaces: 5 certified for cord wood and 1 for pellets. Ticking the box to include previously certified models raised that to 192 central heaters and 27 forced-air furnace entries, 18 of which are not marked as meeting the 2020 limit.

A short list of certified wood furnaces helps you. Type the model name from the furnace label into the search. If it doesn't appear, tick the previously certified box before drawing a conclusion. A model certified only to the older limit can't be sold new today, but the 2020 limit is written as a rule on making and selling furnaces, not an order to tear out one certified when it was sold.

## If the furnace has no mark, no label or no permit

Don't guess what the insurer will say about the wood furnace. Before your inspection period ends, send the agent the photos and the permit search result and ask for a quote on the house as it is, in writing. Underwriting varies by carrier and state, so get written confirmation from the insurer before relying on coverage.

If the answer is no, you have a few roads, and the seller is the one to ask about each: a price adjustment, a listed replacement, or having the add-on taken out by a licensed HVAC contractor under a permit. A chimney sweep's Level 2 report is worth having in hand either way, because NFPA 211 calls for one when a property changes hands.
