# Can You Get Homeowners Insurance With Knob and Tube?

There's no public rulebook to look this up in. Insurers set their own rules, they differ from company to company and state to state, and they don't publish most of them. So this page doesn't guess at what any insurer does. It covers what you can control: what the insurer will see, the document that answers its questions, and your rights if it says no after you've moved in.

## What the insurer will see in your inspection report

Assume the knob and tube will be named. Inspection standards generally make the inspector describe the branch wiring, and Washington's spell it out. Our page on [what inspectors and electricians can confirm](/guides/is-knob-and-tube-wiring-still-live/) quotes that standard line by line.

We treat it the same way in our own reports, where knob and tube on a house from 1955 back appears as an insurance red flag.

So the question isn't whether to mention it. It's what you hand over alongside it.

## The letter that answers an underwriter's questions

The closest thing to a standard format comes from three public knob-and-tube forms. Each was written for insulation or weatherization rather than insurance, but they ask the questions an underwriter is likely to care about:

| What the electrician confirms | Berkeley form #146 (2026) | EWEB utilities form (2014) | Snohomish PUD survey |
|---|---|---|---|
| Live or disconnected | Yes, one of its three boxes | Yes | By circuit |
| Condition of the wire and its insulation | Yes | Yes ("adequate integrity") | Yes |
| Improper connections or splices | Yes | Yes (exposed splices protected) | Yes |
| Fuse or breaker size | Proper protection, not specified further | 15 amp or less | Each circuit's amps and gauge |
| Who signs | Electrician with state license number and class | Electrician with license number | Journeyman electrician |

Ask your electrician for a letter that covers every row, on their letterhead, dated, with their license number. A letter like that answers whatever the insurer asks, rather than leaving the underwriter to assume the worst.

## The order that keeps your options open

1. **Get the electrician's letter.** Our report puts a dedicated evaluation at $150 – $350.
2. **Apply with full disclosure and the letter attached.** Ask each insurer, or an independent agent, for a written answer on the address, not a verbal one.
3. **Do both before your inspection deadline.** If an insurer will only write the policy once the wiring is replaced, there's still time to negotiate. Our guide to [mortgages on houses with knob and tube](/guides/mortgage-house-knob-and-tube-wiring/) covers what the loan rules say about the wiring itself.
4. **If the answer is a rewire,** price it properly. The [removal cost page](/guides/knob-and-tube-removal-cost-permits/) works through the permit fees.

## If you're already insured and the insurer changes its mind

State law sets the notice rules, and Washington's are clear. An insurer that won't renew a homeowners policy has to mail written notice at least 60 days before it expires, and the notice "must include the insurer's actual reason for refusing to renew the policy" (RCW 48.18.2901). A mid-term cancellation needs the same 60 days and the actual reason (RCW 48.18.290), except for non-payment, which needs 10 days.

That written reason matters. If it says knob and tube, you know exactly what to fix and what the next insurer will ask about. Other states set their own notice periods, so check with your state's insurance department.
