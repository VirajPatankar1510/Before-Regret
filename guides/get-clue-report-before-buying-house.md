# Can You Get a CLUE Report Before Buying a House?

The useful move is to get the request in front of the seller early, in writing, and to ask for two reports rather than one. Neither costs the seller anything, and what comes back shows the claims a future insurer may weigh when it prices your policy.

## What to ask the seller for

Send it through your agent, ideally before the inspection period starts, so there's still time to act on the answer. Something like:

> Please request your free property claims reports from LexisNexis (C.L.U.E.) and from Verisk (A-PLUS Property), and share copies with us by [date]. If either report lists a claim, please tell us what was repaired and who did the work.

Both companies are on the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's list of consumer reporting companies, which is where these contact details come from:

| Report | Kept by | Where the owner requests it | Phone on the CFPB listing |
|---|---|---|---|
| C.L.U.E. | LexisNexis Risk Solutions | [consumer.risk.lexisnexis.com](https://consumer.risk.lexisnexis.com/) | 866-897-8126 |
| A-PLUS Property | Verisk | [fcra.verisk.com](https://fcra.verisk.com/) | 800-627-3487 (Option 2) or 800-709-8842 |

The free copy isn't a favour from either company. The Fair Credit Reporting Act calls a company that keeps nationwide files on insurance claims a "nationwide specialty consumer reporting agency" and requires it to hand over a consumer's file once in any 12-month period, on request and without charge (15 U.S.C. § 1681a(x) and § 1681j(a)(1)(A)). That right belongs to the owner, so the request has to come from the seller's side of the deal.

## Why ask for the second report

Almost every buyer guide stops at C.L.U.E. The CFPB's list also carries Verisk's A-PLUS Property, which it describes as collecting insurance claims and loss history for homes, autos and personal property. LexisNexis says its C.L.U.E. property reports draw on claims data from more than 90% of insurers that write homeowners coverage. That's most of the market, not all of it.

We can't tell you how much the two databases overlap, because neither company publishes that. But asking for both costs the seller nothing more than a second request, and it gives a claim reported to only one of them a chance to show up.

## Reading what comes back

LexisNexis says its property reports show the date of loss, the cause of loss and the amounts paid. Three things catch people out:

- **A claim with nothing paid can still be listed.** Connecticut's Insurance Department explains that if an owner filed an actual claim and the insurer paid nothing on it, perhaps because the damage was under the deductible or wasn't covered, the claim may appear. A line with no payment still means something happened. Ask what.
- **A call asking about coverage shouldn't be listed.** The same department says insurers have been instructed by C.L.U.E. not to report inquiries about possible coverage. If the seller says "we only phoned to ask" and an entry is there anyway, that's something the seller can dispute (see below).
- **The lookback depends on whose page you read.**

| Source we read | How far back it says the history goes |
|---|---|
| CFPB listing for C.L.U.E. | seven years |
| Connecticut Insurance Department | up to 7 years |
| Utah Insurance Department | up to 5 years |
| CFPB listing for A-PLUS Property | not stated |

We'd plan on seven years, the figure on the federal listing, and treat anything older as simply unrecorded. Either way, a clean report covers recent years, not the life of the house.

## Why the seller's claims can follow the house to you

This is the part people don't expect. Connecticut's Insurance Department says there are no laws that specifically govern the use of a prior owner's loss history in deciding whether you can get coverage, and that a company may use it if it can show a relationship between that loss and the chance of a future loss at the home. So the claims on the report aren't only the seller's personal history, left behind at closing. They can be part of how your application is judged. That's one state regulator's description, and the rules where you're buying may differ.

What to do with it: give the report to whoever is quoting your homeowners policy, and get the quote in writing before your inspection or insurance contingency runs out. An insurer is one of the three parties allowed to see the report, so a written quote is the most direct test of whether the history changes anything for you.

## If a line on the report is wrong

Only the seller can correct the seller's report, but it's worth knowing how, since a mistaken claim may end up counted against you. Connecticut's department says the owner can report the error to LexisNexis, which contacts the insurer and reports back within 30 days. Utah's department adds that if the insurer doesn't respond within those 30 days, LexisNexis removes the information. Where an entry is accurate but misleading, the owner can add a personal statement that LexisNexis attaches to future reports.

## When the seller won't, or can't

A seller may not want to, or may not get the reports back before your deadline. Neither proves anything about the house. What you can still do:

- Ask in writing, through your agent, whether any insurance claims were filed on the property, what they were for, and what was repaired.
- Get your own insurance quote early in the contingency period, for the reason above.
- Lean on the inspection. A claims report lists claims; it knows nothing about a leak the owner paid to fix privately, or never fixed. If the seller mentions past water trouble, tell your inspector where it was.

A clean report also says nothing about the risk of where the house sits. For flood, the better record is the property's [flood zone, which you can look up by address](/guides/flood-zone-by-address/).
