# DPOR License Lookup: Check a Virginia Contractor's Class

Every Virginia contractor license carries a letter. On the [DPOR License Lookup](https://www.dpor.virginia.gov/LicenseLookup) it shows up as Rank: Class A, Class B or Class C, and that letter decides how large a contract the business may legally take. The Board for Contractors splits every license into two parts: the class sets the money value of jobs allowed, and the specialty sets the type of work. Check both against your bid. We ran the lookup on 16 November 2026, and what follows is what the record shows, what it leaves out, and what Virginia law adds.

## Match the letter to the size of your job

The classes come from the definitions in Va. Code § 54.1-1100. Each class has two tests: the value of one contract, and the total value of the firm's work over any 12-month period.

| Rank on the record | One contract or project | All work in any 12 months |
|---|---|---|
| Class C | over $1,000 but less than $30,000 | less than $250,000 |
| Class B | $30,000 or more, but less than $150,000 | $250,000 or more, but less than $1 million |
| Class A | $150,000 or more | $1 million or more |

Read the table from your contract price. A remodel priced at $30,000 or more is beyond a Class C license, and a new house at $150,000 or more needs Class A. This isn't a technicality. Under § 54.1-1115, bidding on or contracting for work "without the proper class of license" for its value is itself a Class 1 misdemeanor, the same as having no license at all.

## Running the search

The page at dpor.virginia.gov frames the real tool, which lives at dporweb.dpor.virginia.gov. It opens on a single search box that takes a name, a license number, an address or a license type. An Advanced License Search tab splits those into separate fields (license number, name, location, board and license type), and a third tab searches disciplinary actions.

When we searched the name of a large homebuilder, which we won't name, we got 2 records, both contractor licenses under the Board for Contractors. In the results, the license number is a button. Click it to open the record.

We cut out the name, trade name, license number and address rows between and above them.")

The record has a License Details tab and a Complaints tab. License Details lists:
- the name and any trade names (DBA);
- the license number, the license description and the firm type;
- the rank, which is the class;
- the address and the specialties;
- the initial certification date and the expiration date.

Two things are missing. There's no line that says Active, so the expiration date is what you read. And there's nothing about a bond or insurance. A small question-mark icon next to Rank and Specialties points to the definitions. The footer gives the time the data was last refreshed. Ours said Mon Nov 16 02:50:19 EST, so a license renewed or disciplined that morning might not show yet.

## The specialty has to fit the work too

A Class A letter doesn't let a firm do any kind of work. The record we opened listed two specialties, Commercial Building (CBC) and Residential Building (RBC). For an addition or a new house, a residential building specialty like that second one is what you'd expect to see. If the specialties listed don't describe your job, ask the contractor which one covers it.

Trade work has an extra layer. The same section that defines Class C says the board "shall require a master tradesmen license as a condition of licensure" for electrical, plumbing and HVAC contractors. Tradesmen hold their own licenses from the same board, and they're in the same search. When we typed Master Electrician into the box, the results were individual records with the license type Tradesman.

The person on your site may not have a license of their own, and that can be lawful. Section 54.1-1101 A 15 exempts someone doing work "valued at $25,000 or less per project on behalf of a properly licensed contractor" whose license is in the residential building, commercial building or home improvement building classification. The exemption doesn't stretch to work that needs an individual license; that still has to be done by someone who holds one. So the license to check is the one on your contract, plus the individual license of any tradesman doing the trade work.

## What the Complaints tab can and can't tell you

An empty Complaints tab is weaker evidence than it looks. DPOR is open about why:
- **Open complaints appear only late.** A case shows only after an investigation finds probable cause and reaches the adjudication stage. Earlier than that, state law (§ 54.1-108) keeps open cases out of public view.
- **Closed complaints go back to 1990,** but cases closed without disciplinary action are purged after three years.
- **"No Disciplinary Action" isn't a verdict against anyone.** DPOR says consumers "should not assume" such a closure means the business performed improperly. A case can close that way for lack of authority, lack of evidence, a withdrawn complaint or mediation.

What we saw didn't match that neatly. The record we opened still listed 5 closed cases dated 2009 to 2017, every one marked No Disciplinary Action. Treat the tab as a partial history: a disciplinary finding there means something, and a blank tab means only that nothing reached that stage or survived the purge.

## Read the contract against the record

Virginia's contractor regulations make a written contract part of the job. Under 18VAC50-22-260 B 8, residential work needs a legible written contract, signed by you and the licensee before work starts or any payment is taken. Subsection B 9 lists what it must contain, and three of those items are worth checking first:
- the contractor's name, address, license number, class of license and specialties, which should match the record line for line;
- a notice of the Virginia Contractor Transaction Recovery Fund and how to reach the board about a claim;
- your signed acknowledgment that you received DPOR's statement of consumer protections.

The rest covers:
- when work begins and the estimated completion date;
- the total cost, stated for the type of contract;
- the schedule of progress payments and the exact down payment;
- the materials and work you asked for;
- a promise to follow local permit, inspection and zoning rules;
- each side's cancellation rights;
- a rule that any change to cost, materials, work or completion date must be "in writing and signed by all parties."

A few more lines from the same regulation are worth knowing. Stopping work without good reason for 30 days or more counts as abandonment. The contractor must keep the contract and change orders for five years, and failing to get a required permit or inspection is a prohibited act.

On money, DPOR's own consumer guide suggests a first deposit of no more than 10% or $1,000, whichever is less, rising to 30% when the job needs custom-made or special-order items. If the deal was struck at your door, the guide adds that you have a three-day right to cancel a contract negotiated in your home.

## If the contractor turns out to be unlicensed

Virginia puts the pressure on the contractor. Working without a license, or without the proper class, is a Class 1 misdemeanor, with a fine of up to $500 for each day of the violation on top. Section 54.1-1115 C also limits what an unlicensed contractor can collect. They can't enforce the contract against you unless both of these are true:
- they gave substantial performance in good faith;
- they didn't actually know a license was required.

A contractor who let their license lapse can't easily claim ignorance. Failing to renew creates a rebuttable presumption that they knew.

The catch for you is the Recovery Fund. It pays for losses caused by the improper or dishonest conduct of a *licensed* contractor, so hiring an unlicensed one takes it off the table. Even with a licensed one, the fund is narrow:
- **You need a Virginia court judgment first.** It must rest on improper or dishonest conduct, and a plain breach of contract doesn't qualify. Board discipline alone doesn't satisfy the requirement either.
- **The bond comes first.** If the contractor was bonded, you must pursue the bond before the fund.
- **There's a deadline.** The claim form is due within 12 months after the judgment is final.
- **The amounts are capped.** One claim is capped at $30,000, and all claims against one contractor share $100,000 per biennium, prorated if they exceed it. The fund pays no interest or punitive damages.

The fund is paid for by licensed contractors, not taxes. Given how narrow it is, checking the class and specialty before you sign does more for you than any remedy after.

## Buying a house the owner built

The licensing law has an owner-builder exemption, and buyers meet it at closing. Under § 54.1-1101 A 7, a person can build or improve one primary residence they own, for their own use, in any 24-month period without a contractor's license. They still have to follow the Uniform Statewide Building Code.

Selling the house brings a duty with it. Section 54.1-1101 B says the owner-builder must get a certificate of occupancy before conveying the property. The one way around it is for the buyer to acknowledge in writing that no certificate was issued and agree to buy without one. If a seller hands you that acknowledgment to sign, it tells you two things: the seller built or improved the house under an exemption from licensing, and no certificate of occupancy was issued. Breaking the rule is a Class 1 misdemeanor, and a third conviction within 36 months is a Class 6 felony. For you, the practical step is simpler: ask for the certificate, or the reason there isn't one, before you sign.
