# Which Cities Require a Sewer Lateral Inspection at Sale?

A sewer lateral is the private pipe that carries everything from the house to the public sewer under the street. In many places it's simply the owner's problem when it fails. In a number of places around San Francisco Bay, it's also a closing requirement: no certificate, no transfer of title. Burlingame's code gives the reason: leaking laterals let storm runoff and groundwater into the public sewer.

If you search for a list, you'll find realtor and contractor posts that disagree with each other. One says the East Bay utility, EBMUD, has no point-of-sale rule. EBMUD's own pages say it does. So we went to each agency's own site and wrote down what it actually says.

## The rules, place by place

## What the differences mean for a buyer or seller

**A camera video isn't always enough.** In the EBMUD area and in Burlingame, the test that counts is a pressure or exfiltration test, witnessed by the agency. EBMUD says it does not accept camera footage instead. A sewer scope video you commissioned as a buyer is still useful for knowing the pipe's condition, and our guide to [what a sewer scope can reveal](/guides/sewer-scope-before-buying-house/) explains it, but it may not satisfy the city. South San Francisco is the opposite: it reviews a licensed contractor's video.

**The certificate can travel with the house.** Several places issue a certificate that stays valid for years: 10 years after a pass in Burlingame and South San Francisco, 25 after a full replacement. San Mateo exempts a lateral inspected within the last 5 years. If you're buying, ask whether a current certificate already exists. It can save the seller a test and you a delay.

**Who does the work is negotiable.** EBMUD says plainly that in a sale "it is up to the buyer and seller to negotiate" who gets the certificate. Morgan Hill lets the repair duty pass to the buyer on a notarized form. Berkeley, EBMUD and South San Francisco allow an extension past closing, with a deposit. Whatever you agree, write it into the purchase contract, including who keeps the deposit if the work runs over.

**The cost is a scope, then maybe a repair.** Our report engine's ranges for a sewer line are $300 – $500 for a camera scope, then $1,500 – $5,000 for a spot repair or $4,000 – $15,000+ for full replacement. City permit and test fees come on top. Whether insurance helps is a separate question; here's [what home insurance does for a water or sewer line](/guides/home-insurance-water-sewer-service-line/), which also compares who owns the pipe in four other cities.
