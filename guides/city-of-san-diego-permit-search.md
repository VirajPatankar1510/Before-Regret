# City of San Diego Permit Search: OpenDSD, Accela, Paper

A City of San Diego permit search depends on when the work was done. The city's [Development Services records page](https://www.sandiego.gov/development-services/records) sends you online for anything from 2003 on, to OpenDSD and Accela, and you should search both. Permits from 1955 to 2002 are on paper at Development Services, where you can look them up yourself without an appointment. For anything older, DSD keeps no records at all and points you to the County Assessor.

## Which system holds which years

| When the permit was pulled | Where it is | What you need |
|---|---|---|
| 2003 to now | OpenDSD, searchable by address | Nothing. No account. |
| No-plan electrical, plumbing and mechanical work, and rooftop solar, after January 16, 2018 | Accela, not OpenDSD | Nothing. No account. |
| 1955 to 2002 | Paper records at Development Services | A visit. Self-service stations, no appointment. |
| Before 1955 | Not held by DSD | Call the County Assessor's Office at 619-236-3771 or 858-565-5920 |

The second row is the one buyers miss. A water heater, a panel swap or a solar install done after early 2018 is the kind of job that goes through as a no-plan permit, and OpenDSD's own search page says those live in Accela. A clean OpenDSD history doesn't mean the recent work was unpermitted until you've checked Accela too.

Most houses here predate the online records. Census figures we ran put 80.5% of San Diego's 568,668 homes before 2000, with a median build year of 1979. For most of the houses you'll tour, the original construction permit, and maybe a remodel or two, sits in the paper years.

## Check the address is inside city limits first

San Diego the mailing address and San Diego the city are not the same place. Only 45.5% of San Diego County's homes sit inside the city, by the Census tables we pulled, and DSD's records page is blunt about the rest: "the City of San Diego has no jurisdiction over properties located outside the established San Diego City Limits." A house in an unincorporated area or in another city has its permits somewhere else, and a search here will simply come back empty. For those addresses, [our San Diego County guide](/guides/check-building-permits-san-diego-county-ca/) lists which agency to try.

## Searching OpenDSD by address

Open the Address Search tab, type the street number, and type only the base road name: for 7325 W. Santa Fe St., the site's own example is Santa Fe. Then press the small search icon beside the road name. A box pops up listing the roads that match, and you pick one. When we tried 202 C St, which is City Hall, the box offered C ST and W C ST, so a directional on the address really matters.

Results come back 50 to a page. Each row gives the approval number, the type, the status and a project title. Click the approval number for the detail page, which is where the answer is. For a 2012 permit at 202 C ST, the detail page listed four dates in its header (application, issuance, first inspection, completion), then the scope of work and the parcel number.

For each permit on a house, look for two dates: an issue date and a complete date. An issue date with no complete date means an open permit, so raise it with the seller. [Dealing with an open permit before you close](/guides/resolve-open-building-permits-before-closing/) explains the usual fixes. On the permit we opened, the Inspections list itself was empty even though the record showed a first inspection and a completion, so trust the header dates rather than expecting a full inspection log.

## Searching Accela without the prefix trap

Accela's General Search has Street No. From and To boxes, a Street Name box and a Street Type list using short forms like St, Av and Bl. Put the house number in both number boxes. Then set the Street Type, because the street name works as a prefix. Our City Hall search on 202 and "C" returned 59 records, including 202 Coast Bl and 202 Coolwater Dr. On a real house, check the address on every row before you read anything into it.

The oldest record Accela returned for City Hall was from 04/09/2019, a no-plan plumbing permit. The newest was a building permit dated 06/03/2026. One project from 12/22/2020 showed up in both Accela and OpenDSD, so the two systems overlap: search both and don't count a permit twice.

## Permits from 1955 to 2002 are on paper

DSD's public records page says it plainly: "Ministerial permits issued before 2003 are not maintained in electronic format but are available to inspect and copy." The records page says the self-service stations "are available without an appointment during regular hours of operation to conduct permit research or view records." Copies cost $0.25 a page.

For a house built before 2003, plan on a visit. Before you go, call the Customer Service Line at 619-446-5000, staffed weekdays 8 a.m.-3 p.m., and confirm current hours and location. A written public records request won't do the research for you. The city's page says the process "may not be used to answer specific questions", so ask for documents, not for a verdict on the house.

## Before 1955, DSD has nothing

"DSD does not maintain any records prior to 1955," the city says, and it sends you to the County Assessor's Office at 619-236-3771 or 858-565-5920. Census data puts 9.9% of San Diego's housing before 1950, and the true share before 1955 is higher, since the Census groups build years by decade.

For one of these houses, the original build won't have a DSD permit. Later work can, though. An addition from the 1980s belongs in the paper records, and a panel upgrade after 2018 belongs in Accela. Check each era separately instead of stopping when the first one comes up blank.

## Seeing the plans and getting copies

For a San Diego house, plans matter most when there's an addition, a converted garage or a second unit: they're the record of what DSD signed off on. The self-service stations don't cover them. The records page says requests to view or obtain building plans "require an in-person appointment with a team member," so book a Records Review appointment.

Copies of City of San Diego plans take more than an appointment. California Health and Safety Code § 19851 keeps the official set at the building department for viewing, and copying any of it needs written consent from the architect or engineer whose name is on the plans and from the building's owner, either the first one or today's. While you're in escrow, today's owner is the seller, so put the signature request in writing next to your other asks. If you can't get signatures, you can still look at the plans in person and take notes.

If you suspect a job skipped permits entirely, [spotting unpermitted work](/guides/find-unpermitted-work-before-buying/) shows where it tends to hide.

## How we checked this

On 2026-11-24 we read DSD's records page, its public records request page and its contact page, and we searched OpenDSD and Accela without an account. Our one test address was 202 C St, City Hall. No private address went into either system. OpenDSD showed a maintenance page that morning and was working again later the same day, so if you hit the outage page, try again later.

Housing counts and build years are from the Census Bureau's ACS 2020-2024 five-year estimates, B25034 and B25035, pulled for the city and the county, with the shares worked out by us. Treat them as estimates. A house's age also can't tell you whether its later work had permits.

Still unknown to us: when the city shifted its other permit types from OpenDSD to Accela, what a Created status means on OpenDSD, and the turnaround on a records request. We also didn't test the city's Permit Finder map, so it isn't covered here.
