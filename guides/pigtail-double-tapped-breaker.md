# Can You Pigtail a Double-Tapped Breaker?

A pigtail on a double-tapped breaker is a short length of wire that joins the two wires sharing one breaker screw, so that only one wire goes under the screw. If a home inspector flagged a double tap and the electrician's quote says "pigtail", this page covers what makes that the right fix and the four situations where it isn't. Our guide to [what a double-tapped breaker is](/guides/double-tapped-breaker-did-inspector-flag/) covers why inspectors flag it in the first place.

## Why a pigtail satisfies the code

A pigtail satisfies the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) because of two neighbouring rules in section E3406, both taken from section 110.14 of the National Electrical Code. Section E3406.10 says terminals for more than one conductor "shall be identified for the application". Two wires under a screw that isn't identified for two is the defect. Section E3406.11 says conductors "shall be spliced or joined with splicing devices listed for the purpose", with the splice insulated. A pigtail moves the joint off the breaker screw and into a listed connector, and leaves one wire on the terminal.

The pigtail's splice is allowed to sit inside the panel. Section E3907.1.1 permits splices in the wiring space of a panel as long as the conductors fill no more than 40 percent of any cross section of that space, and conductors plus splices no more than 75 percent. In an old, crowded panel that limit is real, and checking it is the electrician's job.

## Four cases a pigtail doesn't fix

A pigtail on a double-tapped breaker joins the wires. It doesn't change what the wires are or what they feed. These four situations need a different fix, or a closer look first.

**The two wires are different sizes.** The IRC's small-conductor table (section E3705.5.3) caps the breaker at 15 amps for 14 AWG copper wire, 20 amps for 12 AWG and 30 amps for 10 AWG. If a 14 AWG wire and a 12 AWG wire share a 20-amp breaker, pigtailing them leaves the 14 AWG wire on a breaker too big for it. The breaker has to suit the smallest wire on it, and the pigtail lead is a wire on that breaker too, so the same table applies to it.

**One of the circuits has to stand alone.** The IRC says a bathroom receptacle circuit "shall have no other outlets" (section E3703.4, with an exception for a 20-amp circuit serving a single bathroom). The laundry circuit may serve only receptacle outlets in the laundry area (E3703.3). The kitchen and dining area need at least two 20-amp receptacle circuits (E3703.2). If the double tap joins one of these to another circuit, or joins the two kitchen circuits on one breaker, a pigtail makes the shared circuit neater without making it allowed.

**The two wires are neutrals.** Two neutral wires sharing one screw on the neutral bar look like a double-tapped breaker but are a separate defect with its own rule. Section E3706.4 says each grounded conductor "shall terminate within the panelboard on an individual terminal that is not also used for another conductor". On our reading, a splice that joins two circuits' neutrals into one lead doesn't meet that wording either, since neither neutral then has a terminal of its own. The fix is a second hole on the bar.

**The wire is aluminum.** Section E3406.10 also requires terminals for aluminum conductors to be identified for it. Our report engine, citing the Consumer Product Safety Commission's Publication 516, notes that the CPSC recommends against pigtailing aluminum wire with ordinary twist-on connectors and treats it as a temporary repair at best. Our guide to [aluminum wiring repair cost](/guides/aluminum-wiring-repair-cost-before-closing/) covers the permanent repairs.

## When the breaker is already rated for two wires

Some circuit breakers are made to take two wires under one screw, and on those a double tap isn't a defect at all, because the terminal is identified for the application. Schneider Electric says its Square D QO and QOB breakers from 10 to 30 amps, in 1, 2 and 3 pole, are rated to accept 2 copper conductors under 1 terminal, in wire sizes #14 to #10. The same answer excludes QOT breakers and any breaker with the suffix GFI, AFI, CAFI, DF, EPD or EPE.

The QO exclusion is the case to watch for. When a standard QO breaker carrying two wires is swapped for its arc-fault or ground-fault version, the two wires that were fine before can't go under the new breaker's screw, and that's when the pigtail comes in. For any other brand, the breaker's own label or the maker's documents decide; we only read Square D's.

## If the panel has no room for another breaker

Moving one of the two wires to its own breaker is the other common fix, and it needs a free space in the panel. If there isn't one, the options narrow to whatever the panel is listed to accept, or a bigger panel. Our report engine puts a panel replacement at $1,500 – $4,000.

Permits vary with the fix. The IRC's permit exemptions (section R105.2) include the "replacement of branch circuit overcurrent devices of the required capacity in the same location" and "minor repair work". A new breaker in a new space isn't the same location, and whether a pigtail counts as minor repair is your building department's call, so ask it before the work if you're buying.

## What to ask for on the invoice

Ask the electrician who fixes the double tap to write these answers on the invoice, so the next inspector, or the next buyer, can see what was done:

1. Which fix: a pigtail, a separate breaker, or a breaker rated for two wires?
2. Are the two wires the same size, and does the breaker suit the smaller one?
3. Were the two circuits allowed to share a breaker?
4. Were the double-tapped wires hots or neutrals?
5. Was a permit needed, and was it pulled?
