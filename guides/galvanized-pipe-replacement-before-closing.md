# Galvanized Pipe Replacement Before Closing: Do You Need It?

Galvanized water pipe is steel pipe with a zinc coating, joined with threaded fittings, and it was a common supply pipe in houses built through 1969. Our report engine flags it for homes of that age and prices a whole-home repipe at $4,000 – $15,000. For a buyer, the age of the pipe isn't the real question. What matters is whether it still delivers water well, and whether anyone can make you replace it.

## Nobody in the code books will make you rip it out

The 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) doesn't order existing galvanized pipe out of a house. Section R102.7 lets a structure that existed when the code was adopted continue "without change", unless the code, a related code or the building official says otherwise. Section R102.7.1 says new work has to meet today's rules "without requiring the existing structure to comply". Galvanized steel pipe to ASTM A53 is even still a listed material, both for the water service and for the pipe inside the house (Tables P2906.4 and P2906.5).

The push to replace galvanized pipe comes from somewhere else: the pipe's condition, an insurer's or lender's questions, and the price you can agree with the seller. Your town may enforce a different code edition or its own amendments, so the building department has the final word on what applies where the house is.

## The flow check that tells you more than a pressure gauge

Galvanized pipe narrows from the inside as it rusts, and a narrowed pipe shows up as weak flow, not as low pressure on a gauge. A gauge screwed onto an outdoor tap with every faucet shut reads static pressure, and with no water moving, a narrowed pipe barely changes that reading. The restriction only shows once water runs.

The 2021 IRC sizes new plumbing to deliver set flows at each fixture (Table P2903.1). The code's flows are a design target for new pipe, not a pass mark for old galvanized pipe, but they turn a vague sense that the pressure is low into something you can time with a one-gallon jug on a showing:

| Fixture | Code design flow | One gallon should take about |
|---|---|---|
| Kitchen sink | 1.75 gallons a minute | 34 seconds |
| Bathtub | 4 gallons a minute | 15 seconds |
| Outdoor hose tap | 5 gallons a minute | 12 seconds |

Start with the tub furthest from where the water comes into the house. Then open a second tap and watch how much the first one drops. A jug that takes twice as long as the table, or a tub that slows to a trickle when the kitchen tap opens, is worth a plumber's look before your inspection period ends. A clogged aerator or a half-closed valve can do the same thing, so rule those out before blaming the pipe.

## Whole house, part of the house, or none of it

Florida's 4-point inspection shows how closely an insurer can look at this. Citizens' sample [4-Point Inspection Form](https://www.citizensfla.com/documents/20702/48575/4-Point+Inspection+Form.pdf) asks whether the supply piping is original to the home, completely re-piped or partially re-piped, with the year of the work, and Galvanized is one of the pipe types the inspector can tick. Citizens' [form](https://www.citizensfla.com/documents/20702/48575/4-Point+Inspection+Form.pdf) also tells agents not to submit applications for properties whose plumbing isn't in good working order. Our [guide to the Florida 4-point inspection](/guides/4-point-inspection-florida/) covers the rest of the 4-point form. Elsewhere, ask your agent what they need to know about the plumbing before you count on a quote, because underwriting varies by carrier and state.

A house with some copper or PEX and some galvanized has been partly repiped, and the galvanized runs that are left keep aging. Ask the seller for the plumber's invoice and the permit. Under Section R105.2 of the 2021 IRC, stopping a leak needs no permit, but replacing a concealed water pipe with new material is new work that needs a permit and an inspection. Our guide to [joining PEX or copper to galvanized pipe](/guides/connect-pex-copper-to-galvanized-pipe/) shows what a proper joint between new and old pipe looks like.

## The pipe from the street is a separate record

Federal lead rules treat the galvanized pipe inside a house and the service line from the street as different things. Under 40 CFR 141.2, a service line runs from the water main to the building inlet, and a galvanized service line that is or ever was downstream of a lead line, or sits downstream of a line of unknown material, is labeled "galvanized requiring replacement". Pipes inside the house aren't part of that inventory, so a clean record for the address says nothing about them. Our [lead service line guide](/guides/lead-service-line-lookup-by-address/) shows how to look the address up.

## Pricing the repipe before you sign off

A whole-home repipe runs $4,000 – $15,000 in our report engine, and a range that wide means you need a written quote for this house. Get it while your inspection or option period is open, and ask the plumber to say whether it includes the permit, patching the walls and the run from the meter.

With the number in hand you can ask for a lower price, a credit or a repair by the seller. Our [option-period guide](/guides/option-period-negotiating-home-repairs/) covers the limits lenders put on seller credits. A repair done by the seller before closing should come with the permit's final inspection, not just an invoice.

If the jug test was fine and your insurer has no questions, keeping the galvanized pipe for now is a reasonable call. Put the repipe in your budget the way you would an aging roof, and notice whether the water runs discolored when a tap first opens.

## How common galvanized-era homes still are

We counted US housing by year built using the Census Bureau's American Community Survey (2020–2024 five-year estimates, table B25034). About 51.0 million of the 143.8 million housing units in the country, or 35.5%, were built through 1969, the last year our report engine's galvanized check covers. In 279 of the 1,773 counties with at least 10,000 housing units, half or more of the homes are that old. St. Louis city, Missouri, has the highest share at 81.9%, followed by Nassau County (79.3%) and Queens County (76.3%) in New York.

The Census counts include apartments and mobile homes, and plenty of the houses were repiped long ago. They show how many homes are old enough to ask the question, not how many still have galvanized pipe.
