# CSLB License Check: How to Read a Contractor's Record

The lookup itself is free and takes a minute: the Contractors State License Board's [Check A License](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx) page. The harder part is knowing which search to use and what the record means once it opens. We ran the search ourselves on 10 November 2026 and read the license law behind each part of the record, so this guide follows the page from top to bottom.

## Search by the number, not the name

Every licensed contractor has to print the license number on contracts, bids and advertising (Business and Professions Code § 7030.5). Take it from the contract or bid you were given. A California number has no letters and is up to 8 digits.

The name search is where people go wrong. It isn't an exact match. It lists every business whose name starts with the letters you typed, 50 names at a time, and it mixes current names, trade ("dba") names and previous names together. When we searched a well-known business name, the same legal name came back on two different license numbers, one Active and one Expired, alongside several Cancelled and Expired licenses under similar names and then a run of unrelated businesses that happened to start with the same letters. Pick the wrong row and you are reading someone else's record.

If you only have a name, CSLB's own tips help: type the first 10 to 15 letters, leave off a leading "The" or "A", skip commas and periods, and put a person's last name first. Then match the number on the record against the one on your contract.

Two practical quirks:

- The database is down for maintenance from Sunday at 8 p.m. to Monday at 6 a.m.
- A record can't be bookmarked or shared as a link. When we opened a license page's address directly, the site sent us back to the search form. If you want proof of what the record said on the day you signed, take a screenshot or print it.

## The record, top to bottom

![The License Status section of a CSLB license record: "This license is current and active," followed by "All information below should be reviewed" and an Additional Status note about a blanket performance and payment bond](/images/cslb-license-record-status-section.webp "Part of a real CSLB record we opened on 10 November 2026, cropped to the status section. Business name, people and insurers removed.")

**Business information.** The name, any "dba" name, the address and phone of record, the type of entity, and the issue and expire dates. CSLB's consumer guidance suggests checking that the contractor has a real business address you could find later; this is the address of record.

**Status.** One line, such as "This license is current and active." Directly under it, the record says "All information below should be reviewed." Take that literally. Active is the start of the check, not the end of it.

**Additional status.** Easy to skip, and sometimes the most useful line on the page. On the record we opened, it said the license has a blanket performance and payment bond under § 7159.5(a)(8), which exempts that contractor from the down-payment limit. More on that limit below.

**Classifications.** The trades the license covers, such as C-10 electrical, C-36 plumbing, C-39 roofing or B general building. The license has to fit the job. A general building (B) contractor can't take a prime contract for a single trade other than framing or carpentry unless the job needs at least two unrelated trades, or the contractor holds that classification or subcontracts it to someone who does (§ 7057). A roof replacement wants a C-39 on the record, or a subcontractor who has one.

**Bonding.** Every licensee keeps a $25,000 contractor's bond (§ 7071.6). A homeowner contracting for home improvement on their own family residence can claim the full amount. Other claimants share a cap of $7,500 in total, and the rest is reserved for those homeowners (§ 7071.5). CSLB itself warns that the bond "is often not enough to cover multiple complaints made against it or your project if it's worth more than the value of the bond." Treat it as a backstop, not as cover for your job.

**Workers' compensation.** Either a policy with its dates, or an exemption. A contractor with employees has to carry it, and CSLB says that if an uninsured worker is hurt on your property, "you could be financially liable to pay for injuries and rehabilitation." Five classifications have to carry it even with no employees: C-8 concrete, C-20 heating and air conditioning, C-22 asbestos abatement, C-39 roofing and C-61/D-49 tree service. A roofer whose record shows no policy is worth a direct question. The record also lists workers' comp classification codes, with the board's own note that it "does not verify or investigate the accuracy of classification codes displayed."

## What a clean complaint record doesn't prove

Every record carries a link for complaint disclosure, and the law limits what it can show (§ 7124.6):

- A complaint appears only once it has been referred for an accusation, or referred for investigation of a probable violation serious enough to suspend or revoke the license. A complaint resolved in the contractor's favor never appears.
- A citation stays visible for five years after the contractor complies.
- An accusation that ends in suspension or a stayed revocation stays visible for seven years after it settles.
- A revocation that isn't stayed stays visible indefinitely.
- A letter of admonishment, which isn't formal discipline, shows for one or two years under the statute. CSLB's own definitions page says one year. The statute is the rule.

The record's disclaimer adds three more gaps. Only construction-related civil judgments reported to CSLB appear (§ 7071.17). Arbitrations aren't listed unless the contractor fails to comply with them. And "due to workload, there may be relevant information that has not yet been entered." So an empty complaint section means nothing serious has reached the disclosure stage. It doesn't mean nobody has ever complained.

## The rules that make the check worth doing

**An unlicensed contractor can't sue you for payment.** Under § 7031, a contractor has to be licensed for the whole job to collect in court, and the person who hired an unlicensed contractor can sue to get back everything they paid.

**The small-job exception is narrower than people think.** No license is needed for a job under $1,000 in total, labor and materials together, that needs no building permit (§ 7048, as amended effective 2026). It doesn't apply if the job was split into smaller contracts to get under the limit, if the person advertises as a contractor, or if they have anyone helping them. A handyman with a truck sign and a helper doesn't qualify, whatever the price. If the work needed a permit, that changes more than the license question; here is what you take on when [buying a house with unpermitted work](/guides/find-unpermitted-work-before-buying/).

**The down payment is capped.** On a home improvement contract it can't be more than $1,000 or 10% of the price, whichever is less, and after that the contractor can't take payment ahead of the work done or materials delivered (§ 7159.5). You can ask for a lien release with each payment. A contractor with a blanket performance and payment bond is exempt from the down-payment and progress-payment limits, which is exactly what that Additional Status line tells you.

**The salesperson needs a registration too.** Someone selling home improvement work for a contractor needs a Home Improvement Salesperson registration (§ 7153). The same Check A License page looks these up.

## If the record is yours

If you're a contractor, this page is what a careful client sees before signing. The record has a link to find out how to correct or update license information, and it is worth checking that your classifications, address and phone are current.

## How we checked this

- **What we did, on 10 November 2026:** ran CSLB's Check A License by business name and by license number in a browser, opened one active corporate record and its complaint disclosure definitions, and noted what each part of the record shows. The screenshot is cropped to the status section; we left out the business, the people named on it and the insurers.
- **What we read:** Business and Professions Code §§ 7030.5, 7031, 7048, 7057, 7071.5, 7071.6, 7124.6, 7153 and 7159.5 on the Legislature's site (read in a browser, because it refuses scripted requests), and CSLB's "How do I find the right licensed contractor?" page.
- **What we couldn't confirm:** how long CSLB takes to enter new actions; it publishes no figure. We didn't open records of individual contractors, so we haven't shown what a workers' comp exemption looks like on a record.
- **What we're not claiming:** anything about the business whose record we opened, or whether any license number you hold is valid. Only the live record can tell you that.
