# Can You Connect PEX or Copper to Galvanized Pipe?

Yes. Joining new pipe to old galvanized steel is routine, and the plumbing code spells out how. What the code cannot do is make the old pipe new, and that is the part that matters most if you are buying the house rather than fixing it.

## What the code says

The International Residential Code, in its 2021 edition, covers both joins in one place. Section P2906.18 says joints between different piping materials have to be made by one of the methods it lists, or with a compression or mechanical-sealing joint with an elastomeric seal, and installed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Two of those methods are the ones a house with galvanized supply lines needs:

| Joining | What P2906.18 requires |
|---|---|
| Copper to galvanized steel (P2906.18.1) | A copper alloy fitting or a dielectric fitting. The copper is joined to the fitting in an approved manner, and the fitting is screwed onto the threaded galvanized pipe. |
| PEX, or any plastic pipe, to another material (P2906.18.3) | An approved adapter fitting. |

The IRC is a model code. Your city or county may have adopted a different edition, amended it, or use a different plumbing code altogether, so the local building department has the final word on what is accepted where you live.

What the code does not allow is the shortcut: copper soldered or threaded straight onto galvanized with no fitting between them, or PEX pushed onto old pipe with something improvised. Those are the joints worth looking for.

## The part a good fitting does not fix

Galvanized steel supply pipe was common in houses built through 1969. It corrodes from the inside out, which shows up as reduced water pressure and discolored water long before it leaks. A correct transition fitting joins new pipe to old pipe properly. It does not stop the old pipe from continuing to corrode, upstream and downstream of the joint.

That is why a house with a few sections of PEX or copper spliced into galvanized lines is not a repiped house. It is a galvanized house with some repairs. The low pressure and rusty water can come back from the sections nobody replaced, and the bill for finishing the job is the one in your inspection report: a whole-home repipe runs $4,000 – $15,000.

## If you are buying a house with mixed pipe

Look where the pipe is visible: at the water heater and under the sinks. Our guide to [telling PEX, copper and galvanized apart](/guides/identify-water-pipes-pex-copper-galvanized/) shows what each one looks like.

Then ask three things:

- **Was the whole house repiped, or only sections?** Get the answer in writing, with the plumber's invoice if there is one.
- **Was the work permitted?** Mixed pipe joined without proper fittings is one of the signs our guide to [finding unpermitted work before you buy](/guides/find-unpermitted-work-before-buying/) tells you to look for.
- **What does your inspector see at the joints?** Ask them to note in writing any place where new pipe meets galvanized, and what fitting is there.

If most of the visible supply line is still galvanized, price the repipe before you remove your contingencies, not after.

## If you are the one making the connection

Use the fitting the code names for the join you are making, follow its manufacturer's instructions, and check which code edition your jurisdiction enforces before you start. The joint is the easy part. Before you tie a new run into galvanized, look at the condition of the pipe you are tying into: if it is already restricting flow, the new section will only ever be as good as the old pipe feeding it.
