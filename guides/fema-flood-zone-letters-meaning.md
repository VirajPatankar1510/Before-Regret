# What Do FEMA Flood Zone Letters Mean? AE, X, D and More

The letter on a flood map is FEMA's short code for two things at once: how likely the land is to flood, and how much FEMA knows about that flood. "AE" says the land sits in the 1-percent-a-year floodplain and FEMA worked out how high the water would rise. "A" says the same floodplain, but without that height. "D" says FEMA hasn't determined it. The letters come from a federal regulation, 44 CFR 64.3, and every FEMA flood map uses the same set.

## The A zones: river and rain floodplains

Zone A, AE and the older numbered zones A1 to A30 are all inside FEMA's Special Flood Hazard Area, the land FEMA expects "the flood event having a 1-percent chance of being equaled or exceeded in any given year" to cover. The difference between them is the detail on the map. In an AE zone FEMA published a base flood elevation, the height the water is expected to reach, so a surveyor can compare it with the house. In a plain A zone FEMA mapped the floodplain without that height, so the elevation question is harder to answer. Maps drawn since January 1, 1986 use AE instead of A1 to A30, so a numbered zone usually means an older map.

Two A zones describe shallow water. AH is shallow ponding, with a mapped elevation. AO is shallow sheet flow, and the map gives a depth number in feet in place of an elevation. Both are "between (1) and (3) feet" deep in FEMA's definition, and both are still inside the Special Flood Hazard Area.

Two more A zones are about levees and dams. A99 is land behind a protective system where "enough progress has been made on a protective system, such as dikes, dams, and levees, to consider it complete for insurance rating purposes". AR is the reverse: a levee that used to be accredited lost that status and is being restored. Both still count as high risk for the lender's insurance rule.

## The V zones: coastal waves

Zone V, VE and the older V1 to V30 are FEMA's coastal high hazard area, flooded by tides "with velocity". VE has a mapped base flood elevation, V doesn't. For a buyer the difference from an A zone shows up in construction: under 44 CFR 60.3(e), new and substantially improved buildings in a V zone must stand on pilings or columns with the lowest horizontal structural member at or above the base flood level, and fill can't be used to hold the building up.

## B, C and X: outside the high-risk area

Zone B and the shaded part of Zone X are "moderate flood hazard areas", between the 1-percent flood and the 0.2-percent (500-year) flood. Zone C and the unshaded part of Zone X are minimal hazard, above the 0.2-percent flood. B and C are the pre-1986 names; new maps use X for both and tell them apart by shading. No federal rule makes a lender require flood insurance in any of these zones, though a lender can still ask for it.

Outside the high-risk zones isn't the same as dry. Of the 2,592,023 flood insurance claims in FEMA's records that carry a zone, 688,661, or 26.6%, were on buildings rated in B, C or X. Paid single-family claims in Zone X averaged $34,965 for the building.

## Zone D: the risk hasn't been determined

Zone D is "Area of undetermined but possible, flood hazards" in 44 CFR 64.3. Zone D is not a low-risk zone. It means FEMA hasn't determined the flood risk for that land, so the map can't say either way. Zone D is also missing from FEMA's mandatory-purchase list in 44 CFR 64.3(b), so a lender isn't required to ask for flood insurance there.

Zone D isn't empty of floods either. FEMA's claims records hold 4,344 flood insurance claims rated in Zone D, and the 2,468 paid single-family claims among them averaged $17,200 for the building.

For a house in Zone D, the map gives you nothing to go on, so the useful questions move to the house and the seller: has it ever flooded, has the seller filed a flood claim, and does it sit low compared with the street and the nearest creek.

## Which letters trigger the lender's flood insurance rule

Under 42 U.S.C. 4012a, a federally regulated lender can't make, increase, extend or renew a loan on a building in a Special Flood Hazard Area unless the building carries flood insurance for the life of the loan. 44 CFR 64.3(b) lists the zones where that applies: every A and V zone, including A99 and AR. Not B, C, X or D.

A regulated lender also owes the buyer a warning. 42 U.S.C. 4104a requires a regulated lender to tell the buyer in writing that the building is in a special flood hazard area "a reasonable period in advance of the signing of the purchase agreement". If you're under contract on a house and only now hear it's in an AE zone, ask the lender when that notice should have reached you.

## The letter no longer sets the price

FEMA's own page on its current pricing method says it "does not use flood zones to determine flood risk". Since April 1, 2023, every National Flood Insurance Program policy is priced on the building itself: how often floods reach it, the kinds of flooding, distance to water, elevation and the cost to rebuild. The zone still decides the lender requirement and the building rules. For the price, get a quote for the address.

One thing worth asking the seller: FEMA says a policyholder can transfer their discount to a new owner "by assigning their flood insurance policy when their property changes ownership". If the house has a flood policy now, ask whether it can be assigned to you at closing before you buy a new one.

## Renovating in an A or V zone

The flood zone matters most when you plan a big project. FEMA's floodplain rules apply to "substantial improvement", any work costing 50 percent or more of the building's market value, and to a house with that much damage from any cause. Once a project reaches 50 percent in an A1 to A30, AE or AH zone, 44 CFR 60.3(c)(2) requires the lowest floor, including a basement, to be raised to or above the base flood level. In an AO zone the floor goes above the highest ground next to the house by the map's depth number, or at least two feet if the map gives none. Your city or county enforces this through its building permits.

If the map shows a regulatory floodway, the stretch of river channel and bank kept open to carry the flood, the rule is tighter still. 44 CFR 60.3(d)(3) has communities prohibit fill, new building and substantial improvements there unless an engineering analysis shows no rise in flood levels. Look for a floodway on the flood map before you plan an addition.
