# Fall Home Maintenance Checklist for New Homeowners

The most useful fall checklist for a house you just bought is partly written already. It's in your inspection report. Start there, then work through the general list below, which covers what official safety and energy guidance says to do before the cold arrives.

## Start with your inspection report

Generic checklists can't know what your inspector found. Pull out the report and look for anything in this table. Each row is something that matters more once the heating is on and the pipes are cold.

| If your report mentioned… | Do this before winter |
|---|---|
| The furnace or boiler's age, or "service recommended" | Book the heating check-up now. ENERGY STAR advises checking the heating system in the fall, before you rely on it. Our guide to [HVAC age and what technicians wish buyers knew](/guides/hvac-techs-wish-buyers-knew-about-system-age/) covers reading the data plate |
| A fireplace, chimney or wood stove | Have it inspected and cleaned before the first fire. The chimney standard, NFPA 211, calls for chimneys, fireplaces and vents to be inspected at least once a year, and that includes the chimney or vent a gas furnace uses. If it's a stove, our guide on [insuring a house with a wood stove](/guides/home-insurance-with-wood-stove/) covers the questions an insurer may ask |
| Missing, old or untested smoke or CO alarms | Fix this first; it's the cheapest item on the list. Details are in the safety section below |
| Areas "not inspected" because of storage or access, such as the attic, crawl space or garage | Go and look now that they're yours. These are often the unheated spaces where pipes freeze. Here's [what "not inspected due to storage" means](/guides/not-inspected-due-storage-mean-inspection-report/) |
| Fog between window panes, or condensation | Moisture between the panes of a sealed window means the seal has failed, so check the warranty. Condensation on the room side is usually about indoor humidity, which rises in winter |
| Water stains, a damp basement, or notes about grading or gutters | Make sure water is carried away from the foundation before the freeze-thaw season |
| Exposed pipes in a garage, crawl space or under sinks | Insulate them. The Red Cross lists these as the pipes most likely to freeze |

If a term in your report is unclear, our [Inspection Report Decoder](/tools/inspection-decoder/) explains common findings in plain English.

## Heating

- **Get the heating system checked.** ENERGY STAR recommends a yearly pre-season check-up by a contractor: cooling in spring, heating in fall. For a gas or oil system that includes the connections, gas pressure, burner combustion and heat exchanger.
- **Check the air filter once a month,** and clean or change it as needed, as ENERGY STAR advises. A filter you inherited from the previous owner is a good first one to replace.
- **Clear space around heat sources.** NFPA's heating safety advice is to keep anything that can burn at least three feet from the furnace, fireplace, wood stove or a portable heater.
- **Reverse the ceiling fans.** The Department of Energy suggests running them clockwise on a low speed in winter. It won't change the room's temperature, but it helps keep you warm.

## Fire and carbon monoxide safety

- **Test every smoke alarm.** The US Fire Administration says to test them monthly, replace batteries at least once a year (including the backup battery in hardwired alarms), and replace the whole alarm 10 years after its manufacture date. Check that date on any alarm you didn't install yourself.
- **Make sure there are enough alarms:** inside and outside each sleeping area, and on every level of the house.
- **Install carbon monoxide alarms** if the house doesn't have them. The Consumer Product Safety Commission advises CO alarms, battery-operated or with battery backup, on every level and outside sleeping areas.
- **Clean the dryer's lint filter and check the vent.** Failure to clean was the leading factor in home clothes dryer fires from 2018 to 2020, at 31% according to the US Fire Administration. Its advice: clean the lint filter before and after each load, clean behind the dryer, make sure the vent pipe isn't crushed or blocked, and check that the outside flap opens when the dryer runs.

NFPA notes that half of home heating fires are reported in December, January and February, so this section is worth finishing before December.

## Water and pipes

Water expands as it freezes, which is why pipes burst. Before the first hard freeze:

- **Outdoor hoses and taps.** The Red Cross's order is: remove, drain and store hoses; close the indoor valve that supplies each outdoor tap; then open the outdoor tap so it drains, and leave it open over winter.
- **Pipes in unheated spaces.** Look in the garage, attic, crawl space and under sinks on outside walls, and insulate both hot and cold pipes there. Pipe sleeves or UL-listed heat cable are the options the Red Cross mentions.
- **Water away from the foundation.** Natural Resources Canada's advice is to make sure the ground slopes away from the house and the gutters carry water away from it. Clear gutters and downspouts after the leaves fall.
- **If you travel in cold weather,** leave the heat on, set no lower than 55°F, as the Red Cross advises.

If the house has a sump pump, find it and make sure it runs before you need it. Our guide on [whether insurance covers a failed sump pump](/guides/homeowners-insurance-cover-failed-sump-pump/) covers the coverage question many new owners don't think to ask.

## Windows, drafts and condensation

- **Seal the gaps.** The Department of Energy's rule of thumb: caulk the gaps around fixed parts like window frames, and weatherstrip the parts that move.
- **Expect some condensation, and know what it's telling you.** Natural Resources Canada explains that condensation rises as it gets colder and indoor humidity goes up. On the room side of the glass it's usually a humidity question. Their tips are to turn a furnace humidifier down, make sure the dryer and bathroom and kitchen fans vent outside, and keep air moving across the glass.
- **New windows can make it worse at first.** Natural Resources Canada notes that many homeowners see more condensation after replacing windows, because the old, leaky ones were letting moisture out. If the seller just put in new windows, that's worth knowing.

## Storms and power cuts

If you plan to buy or use a portable generator, the Consumer Product Safety Commission is firm: never inside the house or garage, even with doors and windows open. Run it outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointing away.
