# Aluminum Wiring Repair Cost: What to Ask For Before Closing

Our reports put a permanent aluminum wiring repair at $2,000 – $8,000+, and an electrician's evaluation at $150 – $350. The engine behind those figures adds one more thing: the number depends on how many connections the house has. That's the useful part. Once you know the price is a count, you know what to ask the seller for, and you can tell a full quote from a cheap one that only looks similar.

This page assumes your inspector has already written *aluminum branch wiring* in the report. If you're still deciding whether a policy is possible at all, start with [insuring a house with aluminum wiring](/guides/get-home-insurance-aluminum-wiring/) and come back here for the money.

## The price is a count of connections

Aluminum wire inside the walls isn't the part that fails. The trouble is where it's joined to something. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's repair booklet, Publication 516, says a repair "should include every connection or splice involving aluminum wire in the home." It then lists them: outlets, dimmers, switches, fixtures, appliances and junction boxes. A few lines later it adds the panel box and anything wired straight to the circuit, such as a dishwasher, a heater or an air conditioner.

![Diagram of what an aluminum wiring repair should cover under CPSC Publication 516. In the repair: outlets, switches and dimmers, light fixtures, junction boxes, hard-wired appliances such as a dishwasher, heater or air conditioner, and the panel box. Not covered by Publication 516: the range, the clothes dryer, the service entrance cable, and copper-clad aluminum cable](/images/aluminum-wiring-connection-points-repair-scope.webp "Drawn by Before Regret from CPSC Publication 516 (June 2011).")

So two houses of the same size can sit at opposite ends of the range. A house with more rooms, more switched lights and more hard-wired equipment simply has more ends to repair. When you ask for a quote, ask for the connection count too. A number on its own tells you nothing; a number with a count lets you compare two electricians.

The diagram's right-hand side matters as much as the left. Publication 516's repairs don't apply to the heavier aluminum used for the range, the clothes dryer and the service entrance cable, and they don't cover copper-clad aluminum cable at all. If a quote includes those, ask what's being done there and why. If the report only mentions the big circuits, the house may not have aluminum branch wiring in the first place.

## Three permanent repairs, and what each does to the quote

CPSC approves "only three methods for a permanent repair." All three are real fixes. They differ in how much of the house gets opened up and who's allowed to do the work.

| Repair | What happens | What to know before you agree to it |
|---|---|---|
| Rewire with copper | New copper circuits are run, and the aluminum can be left abandoned in the walls | CPSC calls it the best method, and says that for many homes it's "impractical and/or prohibitively expensive." Easier where there's an open basement or attic to run new cable through |
| COPALUM | A short copper lead is joined to each aluminum end with a crimped sleeve, using a dedicated power tool that presses with upwards of 10,000 pounds of force | Only electricians trained by the maker are authorized to install it. CPSC says it's practical in a furnished, occupied home |
| AlumiConn | The same copper-lead idea, using a setscrew connector instead of a crimp | CPSC's "next best alternative" if COPALUM isn't available locally. It should still be done by a qualified electrician, with every connection covered |

In practice, the method your electrician offers depends on what they're trained and equipped for. Ask which of the three they're quoting, by name. If they can't say, that's your answer about the quote.

## The cheaper fixes a quote may offer instead

Publication 516 is unusually direct about the alternatives. It says two of them are "often recommended by some electricians because they are substantially less expensive" than COPALUM, and that CPSC doesn't consider either one a permanent repair.

- **Twist-on connector pigtails.** A copper lead joined to the aluminum with an ordinary twist-on connector. The booklet's heading for it reads "Not a Recommended Repair". It's acceptable only as an emergency, temporary fix for one failed connection, followed by a full repair "as soon as possible."
- **CO/ALR switches and outlets.** Devices rated for direct connection to aluminum. CPSC calls them "at best, an incomplete repair", partly because they don't exist for hard-wired appliances or ceiling light fixtures.
- **Other crimp connectors**, including pliers-type ones sold at retailers. CPSC "strongly recommends against" them.

This is where a low quote usually comes from. It isn't necessarily a bad electrician; it may just be a different job. It matters most when the seller does the work, because the seller is choosing the method and you're accepting the result.

## What to put in your repair request

If you're asking the seller to repair before closing, the wording does most of the work. A request that says *address aluminum wiring* can be satisfied by changing a few outlets. Ask for:

1. **The method, by name:** copper rewire, COPALUM or AlumiConn, as described in CPSC Publication 516.
2. **Every aluminum connection**, not a sample: outlets, switches, dimmers, light fixtures, junction boxes, hard-wired appliances and the panel box.
3. **The count:** the invoice should say how many connections were repaired. You can check it roughly against the house as you walk through it.
4. **A permit and a final inspection** where your city requires one. Publication 516 says wiring changes "should be performed and inspected in accordance with local regulations." A permit that's issued but never finaled becomes your problem; see [resolving open permits before closing](/guides/resolve-open-building-permits-before-closing/).
5. **The paperwork before closing:** the invoice, the permit record, and the electrician's licence number.

Then check it at the [final walkthrough](/guides/final-walkthrough-checklist-before-closing/). Take two or three cover plates off with the seller's permission, and look for a copper lead and the connector you were promised. A plain twist-on connector on an aluminum wire means the job wasn't the one you asked for.

If the seller tells you the wiring was already fixed years ago, ask the same question: which method? An invoice naming COPALUM or AlumiConn answers it. *Pigtailed* on its own doesn't, because twist-on pigtails are the repair CPSC says not to rely on.

## Repair, credit or a lower price

A seller repair puts the seller in charge of the method. A credit puts you in charge, which is often the better trade with this particular repair, because the method is the whole question. But a credit has limits.

With a conventional loan sold to Fannie Mae, a seller credit can pay your closing costs, not your down payment, and it can't be more than those closing costs. Anything beyond them, or beyond the lender's cap, is treated as a price reduction instead. The cap depends on your down payment. Above 90% loan-to-value it's 3% of the price or appraisal, whichever is lower; between 75.01% and 90%, it's 6%.

We ran that against the top of our engine's range. At the 3% cap, an $8,000 credit needs a price of at least $266,667. At 6%, it needs $133,334. Below those prices, part of the credit simply can't be paid as a credit. Our [option-period guide](/guides/option-period-negotiating-home-repairs/) has the full table and the closing-cost limit worked through.

If the credit won't stretch, a lower price does the same job without a cap, and you hire the electrician yourself after closing. If you go that way, get the written quote before you agree the number. Then the reduction is based on a scope rather than a guess.

## If the report only says aluminum is possible

Some reports hedge. Publication 516 gives the check: aluminum cable is marked "Al" or "Aluminum" on its jacket every few feet. You can often read it in an unfinished basement, attic or garage. It's also one wire size heavier than copper on the same circuit: No.12 on a 15-amp circuit and No.10 on a 20-amp one, where copper would be No.14 and No.12.

Our engine raises this check for houses built from 1965 through 1973. CPSC's own window runs to "the mid 1970s" and includes additions and rewired circuits, so an older house with a later addition can have it too. If the year matters to your decision, [check the build year by address](/guides/when-was-my-house-built-by-address/) and look at when any addition was permitted.
