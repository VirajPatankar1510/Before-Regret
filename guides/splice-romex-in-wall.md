# Can You Splice Romex Inside a Wall?

A Romex cable that comes up short inside a finished wall is one of the commonest snags in a remodel: a new switch location, a moved outlet, a cable nicked by a drywall screw. Romex is the name most people use for nonmetallic-sheathed (NM) cable, the flat plastic-jacketed cable with a hot, a neutral and a bare ground wire inside. This page covers what the code text says about joining two lengths of it inside a wall, which exceptions people point to and why they rarely fit, and the three fixes that do. If the old wiring on one side is knob and tube, our guide to [splicing Romex to knob and tube](/guides/splice-romex-knob-tube-wiring/) covers that case.

## What the 2021 IRC says about a splice

The 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) handles the splice question in two sentences, both taken from the National Electrical Code. Section E3905.1 says "A box or conduit body shall be installed at each conductor splice point, outlet, switch point, junction point and pull point" unless one of six exceptions applies. Section E3905.10 adds where that box can be: installed so the wiring inside "can be accessed without removing any part of the building or structure".

Read together, the two sections rule out the usual shortcut. A splice in a box that then disappears behind drywall or plaster has a box but fails E3905.10, because reaching it means cutting the wall. A splice wrapped in tape with no box fails E3905.1 outright. A splice in a box with a cover plate on the wall surface passes both, and that is the whole idea behind every fix further down this page.

The splice itself has its own rules. Section E3406.11 requires conductors to be "spliced or joined with splicing devices listed for the purpose" and the joint insulated. Section E3406.11.3 requires at least 6 inches of free conductor at each junction point, measured from where the wire leaves the cable sheath, and where the box opening is less than 8 inches in any direction, each wire must reach at least 3 inches past the opening. A cable that is short by an inch is usually short by more than that once the 6 inches are counted.

## The six exceptions, checked against a wall splice

The six exceptions to the 2021 IRC box rule are sections E3905.1.1 to E3905.1.6. We read each one against the question people are actually asking: does it let two lengths of NM cable be joined inside a closed wall?

| Section | What it allows | Does it cover an NM splice in a closed wall? |
|---|---|---|
| E3905.1.1 | A junction box built into listed equipment serves as the box | No. It covers a wiring compartment inside an appliance or fixture, not a joint in a cable run |
| E3905.1.2 | No box where cable enters or leaves conduit used for support or protection | No. It covers a cable passing through a protective sleeve, not a joint |
| E3905.1.3 | A wiring device with its own enclosure, identified for the use, with brackets, for NM cable | No. That is a self-contained switch or receptacle whose face stays on the wall, not a joint between two cables |
| E3905.1.4 | A fitting identified for the use instead of a box | No. The fitting must stay accessible "and does not contain spliced or terminated conductors" |
| E3905.1.5 | Splices in buried conductors without a box, under E3803.4 | No. It is for cable buried in the ground, spliced with materials listed for that |
| E3905.1.6 | No box inside a luminaire listed as a raceway | No. It covers wiring inside the light fitting |

The buried-cable exception is the one that gets misread. Section E3803.4 says "Direct buried conductors or cables shall be permitted to be spliced or tapped without the use of splice boxes", and "direct buried" means cable laid in the ground. A wall cavity is not direct burial, so this exception does nothing for a splice behind drywall.

## In-wall splice kits and their limits

In-wall NM splice kits are sold for exactly this snag: a sealed plastic housing that joins two NM cables and is meant to be left inside the wall. None of the 2021 IRC's six exceptions to the box rule names such a device. The National Electrical Code may say more, but its free copy needs an account, so we couldn't read it. Whether one is acceptable in your house is a question for your building department, asked before the wall is closed.

A splice kit maker's own instructions narrow the use further. We read TE Connectivity's instruction sheet 408-4504-1 for its Type NM-1 splicing device, part 1116377, revision H. The sheet says:

- It is for 15-amp 14 AWG and 20-amp 12 AWG solid copper conductors, on branch circuits of 300 volts AC or less.
- It is "Not for use with non-metallic sheathed cable made prior to 1990."
- It "must be installed and serviced only by qualified electrical personnel."
- A splice must not be re-terminated if the wire was previously inserted into the contacts, and the individual wires are not stripped.
- It must not be used where directly exposed to water.

The 1990 line matters most in an older house. In a house wired or rewired before 1990, the cable in the walls may predate that date, and on the maker's own terms the kit isn't for it. Other brands have their own sheets; we found only shop listings for them, so we don't describe them here.

## Three fixes for a cable that comes up short

A short NM cable inside a finished wall has three code-friendly fixes, and the right one depends on where the joint would land and whether a new outlet there would be useful.

**Splice in a box with a blank cover.** Cut a box into the wall where the two cables meet, make the joint inside it with listed connectors and enough free wire, and close it with a blank cover plate. The cover stays on the wall surface, so the splice can be reached without removing any part of the building. Some people find a blank plate on a wall ugly; it is also the cheapest route that the code text plainly allows.

**Turn the splice into an outlet or switch.** If a receptacle or switch at that spot would be useful, the box that holds the splice can hold the device too, and the joint is then at an outlet, where the code expects one. Any new receptacle has to suit the circuit it is on, which your electrician checks.

**Run new cable end to end.** Pulling a new length of cable from the last accessible box to the new location removes the splice altogether. It means more wall work and fishing the cable, but leaves nothing hidden, and it is the fix to choose when the old cable is damaged along its length or predates the 1990 limit on TE's kit.

## Buying a house with splices you can't see

A home inspector can only report on splices that are visible: an open junction box in the attic, a taped joint in the crawlspace, a cable that disappears into a wall over a new switch with no box anywhere near. Splices sealed inside walls are outside what a visual inspection can see. Our guide to [unpermitted work behind the wall](/guides/unpermitted-work-behind-the-wall/) lists the signs that a previous owner's wiring is likely to have more of the same where nobody can look.

Permits for splice repairs vary by city. The IRC's permit exemptions (section R105.2) include "Minor repair work", but whether cutting in a box and splicing a cable counts as minor repair is your building department's call. If the house is yours to fix after closing, ask the department before the work, and keep the invoice or permit card that shows what was done and where the box is.

## Questions to ask the electrician

Ask these before the wall is closed, and get the answers on the invoice:

1. Where is the splice, and how is it reached: blank cover, outlet, switch, or no splice at all?
2. Were listed connectors used, and is there enough free wire in the box?
3. If an in-wall kit was used, is the cable newer than the kit's age limit, and did the building department accept it?
4. Was a permit needed, and was it pulled?
