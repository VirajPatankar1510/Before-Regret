# New Mexico Contractor License Lookup and the $7,200 Limit

New Mexico's contractor license search isn't on a state website. The Regulation and Licensing Department sends you to [New Mexico E-Services for Contractor Licensing](https://public.psiexams.com/search.jsp), a page run by PSI, which the Construction Industries Division (CID) calls its licensing vendor. Finding a name there is only half the check. A New Mexico license is good only for the classification specified on it, so the code matters as much as the name. And below a certain size of job, the person you hire may legally have no license at all. Both are covered below, from what we read and saw on 16 November 2026.

## Searching on the PSI page

![The New Mexico E-Services for Contractor Licensing search form, with a Search Individual section (certificate number, first name, last name, city, ZIP code) and a Search Company section (company type set to Contractor, license number, business name, city, ZIP code)](/images/new-mexico-license-search-form.jpg "The search form as we found it on 16 November 2026, cropped above the CAPTCHA.")

The form has two halves, and picking the wrong one is the easiest mistake:
- **Search Company** is for a contracting business. You must choose a company type first. The list mixes Contractor with LP-Gas and six manufactured-housing types, so leave it on Contractor. Then search by license number, business name, city or ZIP code.
- **Search Individual** is for a person, such as the qualifying party whose certificate stands behind a company's license, by certificate number or name.

Two rules trip people up. A business name has to begin with at least its first word, typed exactly, including symbols. The form's own examples are A-H, B & H and 3J'S. And every search needs at least 3 characters plus a CAPTCHA.

We stopped at the CAPTCHA. We don't solve them, so we can't show you a record or say which fields it lists. If you can't find a business by name, ask for its license number and search that instead. It avoids the name-spelling rule entirely.

## Reading the code: what GB-2 means

Every New Mexico license carries a symbol of letters and a number, set out in 14.6.6.8 NMAC:
- **First letter, the field:** G for general construction, E for electrical, M for mechanical and plumbing.
- **Second letter, the type of work:** A for asphalt, B for building, F for fixed works, S for specialties.
- **The number:** the specific trade.

The codes a homeowner meets most:

| Code | What it covers (14.6.6 NMAC) |
|---|---|
| GB-2 | Residential: houses and apartment buildings of not more than four family units, plus the specialty trades when limited to residential work. Two years of foreman-level experience. |
| GB-98 | General building: residential and commercial buildings. Includes GB-2 work, but excludes structures covered by the electrical (EE) and mechanical (MM) classifications. Four years of foreman-level experience. |
| ER-1 | Residential electrical wiring. |
| MM-1 | Plumbing. Four years of experience. |
| GS-29 | The catch-all for any specialty the rule doesn't list. |

Why it matters: under 14.6.3 NMAC a license grants authority only in the classification on it and on its qualifying party's certificate, and it can't be lent to anyone else. A GB-2 builder can build your addition, but the electrical and mechanical work falls under other classifications, and the GB-98 scope excludes them too. One remodel can easily involve several license holders, and each should hold the code for its part.

## The $7,200 handyman limit

For work on a home, New Mexico's small-job exemption is narrow. It's written into the statute (NMSA 1978 § 60-13-3 D(14)) and repeated in the division's Handyman Certificate rule, 14.6.2.8 NMAC. Someone without a license may do work only if all of these hold:
- one project at a time;
- no more than $7,200 in compensation in a year, across all their jobs together;
- the work is "casual, minor or inconsequential, such as handyman repairs";
- it isn't part of a larger operation by them or another contractor;
- they don't advertise, or keep a sign or card suggesting they're in the contracting business;
- they file a declaration with the division every year saying all of this.

The exemption never covers electrical wiring, plumbing or gas fitting. So a handyman can't swap a water heater or add an outlet without a license, however small the job.

Notice the limit is a year's total, not the price of your job. A small repair can still fall outside the exemption if the person's other work that year pushes them past the limit. Asking whether they've filed their annual declaration is a fair question, and so is asking how they find customers. Someone who advertises, or uses a sign or card presenting themselves as a contractor, isn't covered by the exemption.

## What owners may do themselves

The same section exempts owners. Under § 60-13-3 D(10) and (11), you can build or work on a single-family home you own and live in (or will), on your own or with helpers paid wages, without being a contractor. The work still has to meet the act and its codes, which means permits and inspections. CID's own answers to homeowners draw the lines sharply:
- **Wiring your own house:** no electrical contractor needed, but you need a permit and inspections. You get the homeowner permit only by passing CID's homeowner electrical exam with at least 75%, and a failed exam can't be retaken for 30 days. Only you may do the work.
- **Solar panels on your own house:** a contractor license is required.
- **Plumbing your own home:** allowed, with an isometric drawing and a $25 plan review fee.
- **Ductwork:** not allowed. A licensed contractor must pull the permit, install it and call for inspections.
- **Replacing a water heater or furnace:** a New Mexico licensed contractor pulls the permit before the work.
- **Re-roofing:** needs a permit with the manufacturer's specifications. If the roof already has two or more coverings, they must come off first, and any pumice or granular fill must be removed.
- **Fences:** no permit. A block wall needs one.

The homeowner permit rules themselves are in 14.5.2.18 NMAC. CID said residential permits were taking 3 to 4 working days.

## Buying a house: the permit trail

The lists above are also a buyer's checklist. A new water heater, furnace, ductwork or roof in a New Mexico house should have a permit behind it, and for the first three CID says a licensed contractor pulls it. CID warns that insurance "may be denied if no proof of permitting/inspections/Certificate of Occupancy can be provided." Ask the seller for the permits on the big items before your inspection period ends.

## If the contractor isn't licensed

The division can stop the job and file a criminal charge in Metropolitan or Magistrate Court. CID's summary of § 60-13-52 A sets the penalties by the value of the work:

| Value of the work | Possible sentence |
|---|---|
| $5,000 or less | 90 days in county jail, a fine of $300 to $500, or both |
| Over $5,000 | 6 months in county jail, a fine of 10% of the work's value, or both |

A convicted contractor can't be licensed for 1 year. CID takes reports through an online Request for Investigation portal or a paper form.

The rule that matters most in a dispute is § 60-13-30. A contractor can't sue you to collect payment without alleging and proving they were licensed when the claim arose. And a contractor working without a required license has "no right to file or claim any mechanic's lien," so they can't put a lien on your house for the bill.

## What the $10,000 bond does, and doesn't, cover

Every licensee must keep a $10,000 bond in force for the whole license period (14.6.3 NMAC). Read the purpose closely, though. The bond pays to cure code violations that the division certifies, that the licensee caused and didn't correct. It isn't a refund fund for a bad deal. Claims must be made within two years of the final inspection or the certificate of occupancy, whichever comes first. On a recently built or remodeled house, that date tells you whether the window is still open.

Finding past discipline is harder. When we looked, the department's Enforcement Actions page linked to three divisions (Boards and Commissions, Cannabis Control and Securities), and Construction Industries wasn't one of them.
