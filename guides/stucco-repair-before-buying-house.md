# Stucco Repair on a House You're Buying: Patch or Red Flag?

A fresh patch is one of the few signs of past trouble on a stucco wall that a buyer can actually see. Sometimes it's ordinary upkeep. Sometimes it marks where water came out, while the place it got in is still open. The patch can't tell you which. The questions around it can.

The guidance here draws on the National Park Service's *Preservation Brief 22: The Preservation and Repair of Historic Stucco* by Anne Grimmer, available as a [free PDF on nps.gov](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/1739/upload/preservation-brief-22-stucco.pdf). It was written for historic buildings. Its account of how stucco fails is still a useful way to read any patched wall.

## First, know which kind of wall it is

Cement stucco and EIFS (synthetic stucco) are built differently, so the same clue can mean different things. [Telling the two apart](/guides/eifs-vs-stucco-how-to-tell/) takes a few minutes at a showing, and it matters here because of one check in particular.

The brief says unsound cement stucco areas "that have lost their key will echo with a hollow sound when tapped gently." The key is the grip the stucco has on the mesh or wall behind it. An EIFS wall, though, sounds hollow almost everywhere, because there's foam under a thin coating. On cement stucco, a hollow spot is a finding. On EIFS, it's normal.

## Reading the damage around a patch

**Hairline cracks.** "Small hairline cracks usually are not serious," the brief says. It adds that they can be sealed with a thin coat of the finish material, or even paint. What it warns against is ordinary caulk: caulk weathers differently and attracts dirt, so caulked cracks end up standing out. Caulk lines across a wall tell you someone has been sealing cracks, so ask who did it and why.

**Bulges and hollow spots on cement stucco.** The brief calls bulging or cracked areas "obvious places to begin" when assessing damage. A bulge, or a hollow-sounding area around a patch, suggests the stucco there has come away from what holds it.

**Patches beside openings.** In the brief's words, most stucco deterioration comes from water getting in "through the roof, around chimneys, window and door openings," or from ground water and splash-back at the foundation. A patch under a window corner, or where a roof meets a wall, sits exactly where water tends to enter.

**A whole wall redone.** For historic stucco, the brief reserves complete replacement for "extreme deterioration," which it defines as "a loss of bond on over 40-50 per cent of the stucco surface." A freshly re-coated wall on a house that's for sale deserves the same questions as a patch, only more of them.

## Why the cause matters more than the patch

"After the cause of deterioration has been identified, any necessary repairs to the building should be made first before repairing the stucco," the brief says. The fixes it lists are the usual suspects: roof, gutter, downspout and flashing repairs, better drainage, and steering rainwater away from the walls.

A neat patch with the water source still open will crack again. Meanwhile the damage goes on behind it. The brief explains how: "Water infiltration will cause wood lath to rot, and metal lath and nails to rust, which eventually will cause stucco to lose its bond and pull away from its substrate."

## Questions to ask the seller

- What was damaged, and what caused it?
- Was the cause fixed first, such as a roof, gutter, flashing or window repair? Is there an invoice?
- Who did the stucco work, and when? A contractor's invoice tells you far more than "we touched it up."
- Were photos taken before it was covered, or was any moisture testing done?
- Is the work under any warranty, and is it mentioned in the seller's disclosure?

Vague answers aren't proof of anything, but they shift the burden onto your inspection.

## When a patch means calling a specialist

Book a specialist moisture survey before your inspection period ends if any of these apply:
- a patch sits beside a window, door or roofline;
- the area around it sounds hollow on cement stucco, or feels soft;
- there are stains below the patch, or on the inside of the same wall;
- the walls turn out to be EIFS.

On an EIFS wall, our report engine puts the job at **$400 – $800 for a moisture survey by an EIFS-qualified inspector**. For cement stucco, ask for a quote. [How to choose an EIFS inspector](/guides/how-to-choose-eifs-stucco-inspector/) covers what to ask when you call.
