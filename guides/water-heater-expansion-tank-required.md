# Does a Water Heater Need an Expansion Tank?

A water heater expansion tank is a small tank on the cold pipe feeding the heater, with a cushion of air sealed inside it. Water swells as the heater warms it, and in some houses that extra volume has nowhere to go. Whether your house is one of them is what decides if the code wants a tank, so that's the question to settle first.

## What the 2021 codes actually require

The 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) requires thermal expansion control in two situations. Section P2903.4.2 applies where a backflow prevention device, check valve or other device sits on a supply serving a storage water heater and thermal expansion raises the pressure: then "a device for controlling pressure shall be installed." Section P2903.4.1 applies where thermal expansion pushes the pressure downstream of a pressure-reducing valve higher than that valve's setting, on water services up to 2 inches.

The 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC) states its thermal expansion rule in one sentence, section 607.3. Where a storage water heater's cold supply passes through a check valve, pressure-reducing valve or backflow preventer, a thermal expansion control device goes on the heater's cold water supply pipe, downstream of all of them. The IPC mentions tanks only to say how they're sized: to the maker's instructions, and so the system stays within the 80 psi limit in section 604.8.

Neither the IRC nor the IPC makes a tank compulsory in every house. Both make expansion control compulsory in a closed one. Your city or state adopts its own edition and may amend it, so the building department has the final word on the version that applies to you.

## Is your house a closed system?

A house's water system is closed, in this sense, when something on the incoming supply stops water from flowing back toward the street. Three things do it:

- **A check valve at the meter.** Kingsport, Tennessee's water department says it is fitting meter assemblies with a dual check valve on all new connections, and on existing ones as they're scheduled, and that this makes the customer's system a closed one. Enterprise, Oregon says it has put meter setters with check valves on every new connection and every one it replaced in the last ten years.
- **A pressure-reducing valve.** The IRC caps static water pressure at 80 psi and requires a pressure-reducing valve where the main pressure is higher (section P2903.3.2).
- **A backflow preventer** on the service, for any other reason.

The meter change is the one that catches people out. Kingsport's notice explains that before the check valve went in, the public main gave the house a cushion that absorbed the pressure built up by the water heater. After it, the same heater in the same house has nowhere to push, and nothing about the plumbing inside has changed to prompt anyone to add a tank. If you're not sure what's at your meter, your water utility can tell you; Kingsport and Enterprise both publish it.

## What thermal expansion does without a tank or valve

Kingsport's thermal expansion notice lists what can happen on a closed system with no expansion control: the water heater's T&P valve leaks, solenoid valves on icemakers and dishwashers fail, toilet fill valves leak, washing machine hoses leak or burst, faucets drip, and hot water gets pushed into the cold side of the house.

The T&P valve on the heater is not the fix. Kingsport calls it "an emergency relief valve and is not intended to compensate pressure increases created by thermal expansion." Enterprise, Oregon gives the usual settings: the pressure side is normally pre-set at 125 psi or 150 psi, and the temperature side opens at about 210 degrees Fahrenheit. A valve set at 125 psi is a last line of defense in a house the IRC wants at 80 psi or under. Our guide to [why inspectors always check the TPR valve](/guides/tpr-valve-inspectors-always-check/) covers the valve itself.

## A tank is one option, not the only one

Kingsport's notice names three devices that contain thermal expansion: a thermal expansion relief valve, a thermal expansion ball-cock and a thermal expansion tank. The code is written the same way. It asks for a device, which leaves the choice to the plumber and the local inspector.

What sets a tank apart is that it stores the extra water rather than letting it out. Amtrol's instructions describe it: the sealed-in air precharge keeps water out of the tank until the system pressure exceeds it, then the expanded water moves in, and when you open a hot tap the air pushes it back until the tank is empty again.

## How an expansion tank should be installed and set

Amtrol's installation instructions for its Therm-X-Trol tanks, which we read in full, give a plumber four things to get right:

1. **Where:** on the supply line to the water heater, between the heater and the check valve, backflow preventer or pressure-reducing valve. That matches the IPC's "downstream of all" wording.
2. **Which way up:** "Mount vertically only."
3. **The precharge:** with the tank empty of water, adjust the air precharge to match the cold water supply pressure, and only at ambient temperature.
4. **Who:** Amtrol says the product must be installed by a licensed professional.

The precharge is the step worth asking about. The air cushion only does its job if it matches the house's supply pressure, and that pressure differs from house to house, so a tank put in without checking it hasn't really been set up. Amtrol also asks for a yearly check of the whole system, tank included, by a licensed professional, more often as it ages.

## If you're replacing the water heater or buying the house

A water heater replacement is the cheapest moment to settle this, because the plumber is already working on that cold pipe. Our report engine puts a water heater replacement at $1,200 – $3,000. We have no figure of our own for the tank, so ask for it as a separate line on the quote.

If you're buying, our report engine asks the inspector to record the manufacture date from the water heater's data plate. Ask them to note whether there's an expansion tank or other device on the cold supply too. The questions worth putting to a plumber:

1. Is there a check valve, backflow preventer or pressure-reducing valve on my supply, and where?
2. Which expansion device are you proposing, and why that one?
3. What's my supply pressure, and what did you set the tank's precharge to?
4. Does this work need a permit here?
