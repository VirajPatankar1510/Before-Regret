# Don't Skip the Home Inspection: Buyers' Biggest Regret

In February 2025, Realtor.com asked 1,267 people who had bought a home in the past year what they regretted. "I wish I had done an inspection" was picked by 8% of buyers overall, and by 12% of Gen Z buyers. That isn't the top answer. The top answer, at 16%, was "there is more home maintenance than anticipated", and 9% said "the home was not in the condition I expected." Those two are what an inspection is for: it shows you the maintenance and the condition before you've committed, while you can still negotiate or walk away.

So the case for the inspection isn't only that people regret skipping it. It's that the regrets buyers mention most are the ones an inspection would have previewed.

## Things that get mistaken for an inspection

**The appraisal.** If you're getting a mortgage, someone from the lender's side will look at the house, and it's easy to assume that covers it. It doesn't. HUD's notice to FHA borrowers, *For Your Protection: Get a Home Inspection* (form HUD-92564-CN), puts it in capitals: "Appraisals are NOT Home Inspections!" An appraisal estimates value for your lender. HUD adds that "FHA does not perform home inspections," that a home inspection "will only occur if you arrange for one," and that if you find problems after closing, "FHA cannot give or lend you money for repairs, and FHA cannot buy the home back from you."

**Waiving the contingency.** In a competitive market, buyers sometimes give up the inspection contingency, the clause that lets them renegotiate or back out over what the inspection finds. That's a different decision from not inspecting at all. If the seller allows access, you can still pay for an inspection to learn what you're taking on, even without the right to walk away over it. HUD's own advice is to book early: "If you request an inspection early in the process, you may be able to make your purchase contract contingent on its results."

**The lead-paint waiver.** For a house built before 1978, federal law (40 CFR 745.110) gives you 10 days to have a lead inspection or risk assessment done, unless you and the seller agree in writing to a different period. You can only give it up in writing. If a waiver form is in your paperwork, that's what it is. Our guide on [finding the year a house was built](/guides/when-was-my-house-built-by-address/) explains how the pre-1978 line is drawn.

## What a standard inspection doesn't do

Not skipping the inspection also means knowing where it stops. The American Society of Home Inspectors' Standard of Practice (effective March 1, 2014, the version ASHI still publishes as current) sets out what its inspectors are *not* required to do. Your state may have its own standard, so ask your inspector which one they follow. HUD suggests asking for it, or for a sample report, before you hire them.

Under the ASHI standard, the inspector is not required to:

- inspect septic systems or wells, or even determine whether the water supply and sewage disposal are public or private (section 6.2). Our guide on [whether an inspection covers the septic system](/guides/home-inspection-include-septic-system/) goes further;
- determine water quality (6.2.B);
- inspect underground items (13.2.E);
- determine the presence of wood destroying organisms or mold (13.2.A.11), or of environmental hazards, which in the standard's wording includes radioactive substances, the category radon falls in (13.2.A.12);
- inspect the inside of flues and chimneys that aren't readily accessible (12.2);
- judge code compliance, remaining life, or the cost of corrections (13.2.A).

None of that makes the inspection less worth having. It tells you which extra checks to add for the house in front of you.

## Choosing the inspector

HUD's form gives a short, sensible list: get references from friends, real estate professionals, local licensing authorities, and organizations that qualify and test home inspectors. Ask each one for their Standards of Practice or a sample report. A sample report shows you how they write things up, which matters more than it sounds. A report full of "recommend further evaluation" with no detail leaves you guessing. One that photographs the problem and says what it is gives you something to negotiate with.

When the report comes back, read the summary first, then the sections on any system that's old for its type. Our guide on [when it's reasonable to walk away after an inspection](/guides/when-reasonable-walk-away-after-inspection/) helps with the next step.
