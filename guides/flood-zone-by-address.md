# Flood Zone by Address: Look It Up Before You Buy

The flood zone for a US address is public and free to look up. It matters for two separate reasons: it decides whether your lender will make you carry flood insurance, and it is the first clue to how often water has reached that street. It is not the last word on either.

## Find the zone for an address

FEMA's [Flood Map Service Center](https://msc.fema.gov/portal/home) holds the official flood maps, called Flood Insurance Rate Maps. FEMA's own instructions are three steps:

1. Open the Map Service Center and type in the street address.
2. Click the dynamic map button.
3. Read the zone on the FIRMette, a printable section of the official map around that address.

Save or print the FIRMette: a lender, an insurance agent and a surveyor all work from the same official map. If the page will not load or the parcel sits right on a zone line, the city or county planning or building department can confirm what the effective map shows for that lot.

## Read the zone code

The letters on the map are FEMA's flood zone designations. They fall into three groups.

| Zone on the map | What FEMA means by it | Lender requires flood insurance? |
|---|---|---|
| A, AE, AH, AO, A1&ndash;A30, A99, AR, V, VE, V1&ndash;V30 | High risk: inside the Special Flood Hazard Area, the land FEMA expects a flood with a 1-percent chance of happening in any year to cover. V zones are the coastal ones | Yes, for a mortgage from a federally regulated or insured lender |
| B, or X (shaded) | Moderate risk: between the 1-percent flood and the 0.2-percent (500-year) flood | No federal requirement, though a lender can still require it |
| C, or X (unshaded) | Minimal risk: above the 0.2-percent flood elevation | No |

The 1-percent flood is what people call the 100-year flood. The name misleads: it is a 1-in-100 chance every single year, not a flood that arrives once a century. Over a 30-year mortgage those yearly chances add up.

## What the zone decides, and what it no longer decides

**It decides the insurance requirement.** Federal law, [42 U.S.C. § 4012a](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/4012a), bars federally regulated lenders from making, increasing, extending or renewing a loan on a building in a Special Flood Hazard Area unless it carries flood insurance. The required amount is the outstanding loan balance or the maximum coverage available for that type of building, whichever is less, for the life of the loan.

**It no longer sets the price.** Since FEMA moved the National Flood Insurance Program to its current pricing approach, known as [Risk Rating 2.0](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance/risk-rating), premiums are not set by flood zone. FEMA prices each building on flood frequency, the type of flooding (river overflow, storm surge, coastal erosion, heavy rainfall), distance to water, elevation and the cost to rebuild. Two houses in the same zone can get very different quotes, which is why the zone alone cannot tell you what you will pay.

## Outside the high-risk zone is not outside the water

A zone X result is good news, not a guarantee. When we counted every claim the National Flood Insurance Program has paid, by the zone each policy was rated in at the time, [26.6% of those that record a zone were outside the high-risk zone](/research/outside-the-zone/): $20.6 billion paid on properties in zones X, B or C. In Texas the share was 50.7%.

The same study found why those figures still understate it. Inside the mapped zone, where a mortgage usually compels it, 47.9% of homes carry flood cover. Outside it, 1.25% do. Every uninsured house outside the zone that flooded is missing from the claims records entirely. Our [Risk Without Cover](/research/risk-without-cover/) study looks at the other side: in the median county, 15% of homes inside a mapped flood zone carry a policy.

The study page lets you look up any county's share of claims paid outside the mapped zone.

## Before you sign

- **Get a flood insurance quote during your inspection period, not after.** Because the premium is priced to the building, an agent can quote this house, not a zone average. Ask for the quote in writing.
- **Ask the seller whether the house has ever flooded or had a flood claim,** and ask them to pull a [CLUE report](/guides/get-clue-report-before-buying-house/), the record of past insurance claims on the property, which only the owner can order.
- **Ask whether the seller already has an NFIP policy.** FEMA allows a building policy to be [assigned in writing](https://www.fema.gov/about/glossary/assignment) to the new owner when title transfers, which can keep coverage in place from day one.
- **Watch the timing.** A new NFIP policy normally starts after a 30-day waiting period. When it is bought in connection with the mortgage and paid at or before closing, it can take effect at closing instead, so arrange it with your lender rather than leaving it to the week after.
- **If the map looks wrong for this lot,** for example a house on obviously higher ground drawn inside the zone, the owner can ask FEMA for a [Letter of Map Amendment](https://www.fema.gov/flood-maps/change-your-flood-zone). If FEMA grants it, the federal insurance requirement may no longer apply. That is a process for the owner, so ask the seller whether one was ever filed.

## What the map cannot tell you

A flood map is drawn for a whole community at one point in time. It does not show whether this particular basement takes on water in a heavy rain, whether the street drains are adequate, or what has changed upstream since the map took effect. Those come from the seller's disclosure, the inspection and the neighbors.
