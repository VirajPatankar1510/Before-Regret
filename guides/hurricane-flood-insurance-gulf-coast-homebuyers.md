# Hurricane Isaias: Flood Risk and Insurance for Gulf Buyers

As of the National Hurricane Center's 10 a.m. CDT advisory on Friday, October 9, 2026, Hurricane Isaias was a major hurricane with maximum sustained winds of about 120 mph, and its center was expected to reach the western Florida Panhandle coast that evening. A Hurricane Warning was in effect from Ocean Springs, Mississippi to the Bay/Gulf County line in Florida, and a Storm Surge Warning from the mouth of the Mississippi River to the Suwannee River.

If you are in that area, follow the [National Hurricane Center](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/) and your local emergency officials. Nothing on this page is storm-safety guidance.

This page is for a different moment: a buyer who is under contract on a Gulf Coast house, or about to be, and wants to know what a storm like this one says about flood risk and insurance before closing. Two federal sources frame the answer. FEMA says plainly that most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage. And the Government Accountability Office reported on October 1, 2026 that FEMA's flood maps miss a large share of properties at high flood risk.

## A flood zone is where to start, not the answer

A FEMA flood zone tells you whether a property sits in a Special Flood Hazard Area, the zone where a lender making a federally backed mortgage will require flood insurance. It does not tell you everything about the water.

The [GAO's October 2026 report, GAO-27-108012](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-108012), says the FEMA maps used to decide who must buy flood insurance "do not capture all flood risks, especially heavy rainfall." Using First Street's property-level flood scores, where a score of 4 to 10 counts as high risk, GAO estimates that about 13 million high-risk properties sit outside FEMA's Special Flood Hazard Areas. It also estimates that 86 percent of high-risk properties had no National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) policy as of April 2026.

Our own analysis of NFIP claims points the same way: 26.6% of the claims that record a flood zone were on policies rated outside the high-risk zone, as [Outside the Zone](/research/outside-the-zone/) sets out. To find the zone for a particular house, here is how to [look up the flood zone by address](/guides/flood-zone-by-address/). Treat the answer as the minimum, not the whole picture.

## Your homeowners policy and your flood policy are two different things

FEMA's flood insurance page puts it in one line: "Most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage." Flood insurance is a separate policy. The GAO report lists the belief that homeowners insurance covers flood among the misperceptions that discourage people from buying it, noting that most policies specifically exclude it.

Flood coverage can come from the NFIP or from a private insurer. GAO found private policies accounted for 14 percent of all flood policies in 2025.

Wind is handled separately again. On the coast, wind damage may be covered by the homeowners policy or by a separate wind policy, such as one from the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association. Which policy answers for which kind of damage is a question of policy wording, so read the declarations page, not the summary on a quote. Water that comes up through a drain is yet another question; this guide on whether [homeowners insurance covers a failed sump pump](/guides/homeowners-insurance-cover-failed-sump-pump/) walks through that line.

## Why a storm in the Gulf changes your closing timeline

Two timing rules matter most while a storm is in the Gulf.

The first is that coverage can become hard to buy at all. The Texas Department of Insurance tells consumers that "once a named storm enters the gulf, most insurance companies, including the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA), stop selling new policies or making changes to existing ones." If you have not bound your homeowners policy yet, ask your agent today whether your carrier is writing new business, and get the answer in writing.

The second is the flood waiting period. FEMA says there is "typically a 30-day waiting period for an NFIP policy to go into effect," with exceptions, including when the coverage is required by a government-backed lender or relates to a community flood map change. The Texas Department of Insurance puts the buyer's version simply: unless you are buying coverage for a new home, these policies do not take effect until 30 days after you buy them. Confirm with your agent which rule applies to your policy and the date it starts.

While you are checking, read what your purchase contract says about damage that happens between signing and closing.

## The hurricane deductible is easy to miss

A hurricane deductible can be a percentage of the dwelling limit rather than a flat dollar amount.

Florida law spells this out. Under Florida Statutes § 627.701, before issuing a home policy an insurer must offer hurricane deductibles of $500, 2 percent, 5 percent and 10 percent of the policy's dwelling limit. A policy with a separate hurricane deductible must say so on its face, in bold type: "THIS POLICY CONTAINS A SEPARATE DEDUCTIBLE FOR HURRICANE LOSSES, WHICH MAY RESULT IN HIGH OUT-OF-POCKET EXPENSES TO YOU."

The arithmetic is worth doing before you sign. On a $300,000 dwelling limit, a 2 percent hurricane deductible is $6,000 and a 10 percent deductible is $30,000. Mississippi's Insurance Department gives the same advice in its consumer hurricane checklist: check the deductible amount in your policy, noting that several large insurers writing coverage on the Gulf Coast have used deductibles set as a percentage of the dwelling's value for wind and hurricane.

## A checklist before you buy on the Gulf Coast

- **Look up the flood zone** for the address, then read it as a floor, not a ceiling.
- **Ask the seller directly:** has the property ever flooded, or had a water-damage insurance claim filed? Get the answer in writing.
- **Ask for the claims history.** A buyer cannot order a home's CLUE report directly, but can ask the seller for it; here is [how to get a CLUE report before buying](/guides/get-clue-report-before-buying-house/).
- **Check the permit history** for roof, window or structural work that followed earlier storms, using [building permit history by address](/guides/look-up-building-permits-by-address/).
- **Get three written quotes before your inspection deadline:** the homeowners policy with its hurricane or wind deductible shown in dollars, a separate wind policy if the house needs one, and flood coverage from the NFIP or a private insurer.
- **Ask your agent four questions:** Does my lender require flood insurance for this address? When does each policy start? Is the carrier binding new policies right now? Which deductible applies to a named storm?
- **Look at the street, not just the lot.** Drainage is shared. Walk the block, notice where water would go, and ask neighbors what happened in the last big rain.

## What a free report can and cannot tell you

A [free Before Regret report](/) for an address does not decide its flood zone. It marks the flood zone as not yet verified for that address and points you to FEMA's Flood Map Service Center to look it up. What it does give you is the list of checks that matter for a house of that age and county, and the questions to put to the seller, including the one about past flooding and water-damage claims.

The decisions that protect you this week still come from three people: your insurance agent, your inspector, and, if the contract's storm terms are unclear, your real-estate attorney.
