# Writing article FAQs

## What this is for

Published guide articles in the `articles` table each carry a `faq_json` column: an array of
`{question, answer}` pairs that render as a visible accordion at the foot of the guide and are
merged into that page's `FAQPage` JSON-LD (see `GuidePageView.tsx` and `scripts/prerender-guides.tsx`).

These are written by hand, article by article, rather than generated by the site's Gemini
pipeline. The reason is specific: article generation is open-ended (the model states new facts
from training data, which is where invented figures and mis-attributed citations come from — a
recurring, real problem on this site). FAQ writing here is deliberately the opposite: a closed-book
task where the *only* permitted source of facts is the article body already published. Keeping it
closed-book is what makes the output trustworthy, and the save script mechanically enforces it.

## Workflow

**Run everything from the `Before-Regret` repo root**, not from a parent directory — `cd` there
first if the session started somewhere else. The scripts live inside the repo so Node resolves
`dotenv/config` and `@neondatabase/serverless` by walking up to the repo's `node_modules`; a copy
parked in `/tmp`, or a shell sitting in the repo's parent, cannot resolve them and will fail on
import.

**1. Pull a batch** (default 10 articles; pass a number to change it). 3–4 articles per batch tends
to produce better FAQs than 10, because step 2 requires reading each article in full and a smaller
batch keeps that honest:

```bash
npx tsx .claude/skills/article-faqs/scripts/faq-fetch.ts 4
```

This writes `.tmp-faq-batch.txt` (the full source to read) and `.tmp-faq-draft.json` (a skeleton to
fill in). It selects articles that still have no FAQs, so batches never overlap and you can stop and
resume freely — there is no offset to track.

**2. Read the whole batch file.** Not a skim. You cannot write a grounded FAQ for an article you
have only partially read, because the whole point is knowing what the article does and doesn't say.

**3. Fill in `.tmp-faq-draft.json`** with 3 Q&A pairs per article (2 is acceptable if an article
genuinely has only two non-redundant angles; more than 4 usually means padding).

**4. Verify, then save:**

```bash
npx tsx .claude/skills/article-faqs/scripts/faq-save.ts --dry-run   # check only
npx tsx .claude/skills/article-faqs/scripts/faq-save.ts             # check, then write
```

The save script re-reads each article's real stored body and refuses to write anything if any check
fails. Fix what it reports and re-run — do not work around it. It is checking the exact failure
modes that matter here, and a check that fires is the script doing its job, not an obstacle.

**5. Clean up** `.tmp-faq-batch.txt` and `.tmp-faq-draft.json` when the batch is saved.

Guide pages are statically prerendered at build time, so newly saved FAQs are not visible on the
live site until the next deploy. A DB-only change needs `git commit --allow-empty` to trigger one.

## What makes a good FAQ here

The quality bar is set by the one article that already had FAQs (`id=77`, Travis County permits).
Read that one if you want a reference; the pattern is:

**Questions** are specific, long-tail, and phrased the way someone would actually type them into
Google or ask an assistant — "Who pays to close out an expired permit in Austin, TX?" not "What
about permits?". A question that could sit unchanged on fifty other articles is a wasted slot.

**Answers** lead with the direct answer in the first few words ("No.", "Generally no.", "The
current property owner —"), then give the mechanism, then end with something the reader can act on.
Two to four sentences. They must read as complete on their own, since an answer engine may lift one
without the surrounding page.

**Each FAQ must earn its place** by covering something the Quick Answer and the other FAQs don't.
The most common way this work goes wrong is three FAQs that are all mild rephrasings of the article's
own summary — every word true, no value added, and exactly the padding pattern search engines and
answer engines discount. Before writing, skim the article for the specific, concrete things a reader
would still wonder about: the distinction the article draws, the exception it names, the thing it
warns is commonly misunderstood. Those make good questions. The article's thesis does not — the
Quick Answer already covers it.

## The grounding rules

Every factual claim must be traceable to the article body. In practice:

- **Never introduce a figure the article doesn't state.** No costs, years, percentages, thresholds,
  timeframes, or amperages that aren't already in the text. If the article says "30 to 90 days", the
  FAQ says "30 to 90 days" — not "about three months", and never a number the article never gave.
- **Only cite `[CODE]` markers the article itself cites.** The citation must belong to the specific
  claim, matching the same precision rule the article generator follows.
- **Don't resolve an ambiguity the article leaves open.** If the article says approval "depends on
  the carrier", the FAQ says that too. Inventing a confident answer where the source hedges is the
  subtlest way this goes wrong, and the one the automated checks can't catch — so it's on you.
- **Match the site's voice**: hedged where reality is uncertain, no personalized legal/financial
  advice, plain description of a counterparty's standard practice rather than accusation. These are
  the same hard rules in `src/server/articleGenerator.ts`; read its system instruction if unsure.
- **Formatting**: plain sentences. No markdown, no bullets, no headings — answers render inside an
  accordion, and `**bold**` will show up as literal asterisks.

Use `--` for an em-dash, matching the rest of the site's copy.

## What the save script checks

Understanding these helps you write clean drafts rather than iterating against the checker:

| Check | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Every digit-bearing figure appears in the source | Invented numbers are the likeliest and most damaging error |
| Every `[CODE]` citation appears in the source | Prevents attributing a claim to an authority the article never cited |
| Answer vocabulary <70% contained in the Quick Answer | Catches rephrase-padding |
| No two questions in an article near-duplicate | Catches filler slots |
| Question ends in `?`, under 100 chars; answer 120–900 chars | Keeps them searchable and substantive |
| Refuses to overwrite existing FAQs without `--force` | Protects hand-written work already in place |

A figure flagged as missing is sometimes correct-but-reworded (article says `4`, FAQ says `4.0`).
Fix it by matching the source's own phrasing rather than by loosening the check — exact fidelity to
the source is the property being protected.

## Example

From article `id=7` ("Does Knob-and-Tube Wiring Need Replacing Before Closing?"), whose Quick Answer
already covers *insurers refuse policies, so the system usually must be replaced before closing*:

> **Q:** How can I tell if knob-and-tube wiring is still active?
>
> **A:** Looking at it isn't enough -- determining whether observed knob and tube wiring is active or
> abandoned requires electrical testing. Inspectors use non-contact voltage testers on exposed
> conductors, because it's common in older homes to find leftover knob and tube framework that was
> disconnected decades ago when a breaker panel was installed, while the functional circuits behind
> living room or bedroom walls remain connected to the live service. Ask your inspector to also check
> whether any three-prong outlets are wired to ungrounded knob and tube circuits using fake ground
> connections, known as open grounds or false grounds.

Note what it does: answers a question the Quick Answer never touches, draws entirely on a specific
paragraph of the body, introduces no figure, and ends with something the reader can ask their
inspector tomorrow.
